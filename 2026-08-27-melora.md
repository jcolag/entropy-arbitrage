## Melora

For our usual context, this episode lands between [*Dark Page*]({% post_url 2025-07-10-dark-page %}) and [*Attached*]({% post_url 2025-07-17-attached %})...maybe.  It looks like the latter aired on the same night as *Rules of Acquisition* (next week's episode), so depending on which order they aired, maybe **TNG** didn't have a specific episode between this and the next.

> Medical log, stardate 47229.1. Chief O'Brien and I have been working overtime to prepare for the arrival of a new cartographer. Ensign Melora Pazlar is the first Elaysian to join Starfleet and, as such, requires special accommodations.

We have never seen this specific situation before, but the rush to provide accommodations for people with different physical needs certainly follows from the narrow range of physical forms that Starfleet plans for, such as the temperatures provided to guests shown in episodes such as [*Haven*]({% post_url 2022-07-28-haven %}) or requiring people to specify precise temperatures for water in [*The Defector*]({% post_url 2023-07-06-defector %}).  Even going back to the original animated series, when they had the opportunity to portray *anything* on the ship, they made it clear that ships couldn't deal with small officers in [*The Terratin Incident*]({% post_url 2021-10-28-terratin %}) or aquatic officers in [*The Ambergris Element*]({% post_url 2020-05-28-court %}).  When important ships designed to handle a wide variety of missions don't have designs that work for different kinds of officers, it fits perfectly for a new and distant outpost like Deep Space Nine to not even have considered it until they have a different kind of officer on the way.

> **DAX**: I haven't seen one of these in three hundred years. I'm surprised it was even on file in the replicator's data bank.

...What happened three hundred years ago?

No, really.  Surely, people still injure their legs severely enough that they can't walk, and it defies reason to think that everybody has access to a certified professional with one of their woo-woo boxes, and that nobody has objections to using them, or that they always work instantly.  If not, then you can't tell me that we only have one planet of people living in low-gravity, with only a few non-shut-ins.  Where do the people without working legs go, then?

The original series hinted that they get abandoned and even hidden away, such as Pike way back in [*The Menagerie*]({% post_url 2020-03-26-trek-menagerie-1 %})---well within three hundred years, I might add---so it does seem worth questioning why Dax hasn't seen a wheelchair in centuries.

> **BASHIR**: I know exactly why. She went through the Academy the same way. Once her basic needs are met, she refuses any special assistance. She's extraordinary.

Ooh, inspiration porn already!

That sounds like a flip comment, but it tells us that even Starfleet Academy doesn't have accommodations for people who can't walk, and so everyone focuses on her resilience as a feel-good, inspirational story instead of fixing the systemic problem.

I also can't help noticing the second use of the word *special* to refer to making spaces usable to people with different needs, a word that had become increasingly insulting, due to its frequent use in segregated educational programs that could accommodate students with disabilities and those programs "rhyming with" older remedial classes.  The term "special" still has enough of a stigma around it that [one automated pass in my proofreading](https://alexjs.com/) begs me to "use *has a disability*, *person with a disability*, *people with disabilities* instead" every time that I use the term, even though I always mean it for an uncommon case that needs specific handling...

> **PAZLAR**: Ensign Melora Pazlar reporting for duty.

If Pazlar looks familiar, then you might recognize {% wiki Daphne Ashbrook %}, probably best known (among a fairly hefty career as a guest star) for her "companion" role Grace Holloway in the feature-length attempt to move the *Doctor Who* franchise to the United States a couple of years after this, after a multi-year hiatus.  That plan would flop, and fans of the franchise would need to wait more than another decade for the renaissance of the past twenty years...not that such incidents might have any resonance with today...

> **PAZLAR**: I'm sure the Chief Science Officer has more important things to do than to chart a sector in the Gamma Quadrant.

Would she, though?  I honestly don't understand what the science officer position *does*, here.  Remember, she got this assignment before anybody knew about the wormhole, meaning that they mostly intended the crew to keep the place running until the Bajoran government either joins the Federation, funds a crew of their own, or destroys the monstrous symbol of the occupation and builds a new space station.  That doesn't sound like it calls for a lot of science *unless* you send her to the Gamma Quadrant.

She sometimes seems to do some survey work for the Bajoran government, sure, but that doesn't call for a multi-doctorate with centuries of experience.

> **ASHROCK**: Paltriss only created eighty of them. Each a work of art.

If Ashrock looks familiar, then you might recognize him as {% wiki Don Stark %}, who we actually last saw in as one of the holographic mobsters in [**First Contact**]({% post_url 2025-12-18-first-contact-1 %}) (still in this episode's future), but has made appearances across television and film for over fifty years.

> **FALLIT**: Eight years. Eight long years.

Largely for completeness, since I don't know if *I* recognize him, but if Kot looks familiar to you, then you might recognize him as {% wiki Peter Crombie %}, apparent frequent guest on television until his death a couple of years ago.

> **PAZLAR**: I'm sorry if I seem overly sensitive, but I'm used to being shut out of the Melora problem. The truth is, there is no Melora problem until people create one. This may sound ungrateful, because Doctor Bashir has been wonderfully helpful in the preparations for my arrival, but frankly, I wonder why a medical opinion is necessary in this discussion.

No, a "Melora Problem" exists, because Starfleet doesn't provide accommodations until someone demands them.  I'd call that an inclusion problem, which would show itself with or without Pazlar's participation.

> **PAZLAR**: And I've achieved that, Commander, without being dependent on anybody. To be honest, I prefer to work alone. It's simply easier for me.

Sorry, but how the Hell does Starfleet Academy let anybody graduate with this attitude?  The entire job revolves around working with colleagues, no?

> **BASHIR**: I'm sure you never set out to attack anyone personally, but you do seem to attack a lot.

She hasn't attacked anybody.  I'd call her *defensive*, maybe even unnecessarily so, but they only seem hurt so far because they expect effusive praise for doing (nearly) the bare minimum.

> **CHEF**: Gagh Tek Or.

Most of the guests, I admit that I phoned it in, in that, yeah, these performers exist.  However, if you recognize the not-even-named Klingon, then you might recognize him as {% wiki Ron_Taylor_(actor)|Ron Taylor %}, who played the Cowardly Lion in the original run of **The Wiz**, originated the role of Audrey II in **Little Shop of Horrors**, played the coach on **Twin Peaks**, briefly (because of an appearance on **L.A. Law**) landing a gig singing the national anthem at baseball games.  He also sang with his own band and along with a bunch of musicians who you absolutely know.

We'll see him again in a couple of months.

> **PAZLAR**: Excuse me, Julian, but I can't eat this.
>
> **BASHIR**: I know how it looks but---

This, I believe, marks the first time that we've seen anybody from the Federation accept foreign food as a normal part of living far away, instead of some repulsive abomination to avoid.

> **BASHIR**: Actually, first I started to study tennis.

While things had started to change slightly with the spike in consumerism in the 1980s, tennis still had strong associations with wealth.  Players like Jimmy Connors and John McEnroe appealed to people because they seemed to represent a class conflict.  Honestly, the sport still has those associations, which goes a long way to explaining the deep hatred aimed at Serena Williams for the audacious crime of out-performing everybody.

In other words, this smells like it marks Bashir as coming from wealth.  Maybe he didn't grow up with equestrian money like Pike, Kirk, and Picard, but probably money.

> **DAX**: Dax to Pazlar. Computer, level one security access.

Why would she think that they would meet at her quarters?  Does she start every mission with the lowest-ranked officer's door?

> **PAZLAR**: What kind of an architect would deliberately design a raised rim at the entrance to every door?

I'd say that it sounds like a good question---especially given how often I stub a toe on a shockingly large door jamb, not to mention how much trouble the robot vacuum has with it---but Starfleet also has doors that lock everybody in when the power goes out, most recently seen nearly killing people in [*Q-Less*]({% post_url 2026-04-09-q-less %}), though far more prominently in [*New Ground*]({% post_url 2024-07-18-new-ground %}).

> **BASHIR**: Melora, no one on this station is completely independent. In space, we all depend on one another to some degree.

Well, we finally get some sense, here...

> **PAZLAR**: You may want to brace yourself until you get used to it.

The fun in the scene, not to mention the preparation for emergencies, makes me wonder why Starfleet (and other governments) wouldn't outfit its deployments with the ability to adjust the gravity, at least in certain spots.  Other than television budgets, I mean.  Low-gravity, especially without needing to go into some dangerous situation like vacuum, seems like it would attract far more interest than slogging through stories in virtual reality.

> **DAX**: Delvok never seems Vulcan to me. There's too much emotion in his music.

Racism already?

> **BASHIR**: No more servo-controls, no more chair.

While this idea has excited her since Bashir casually raised it---and maybe justifiably so, given her current interests---it still seems absurdly creepy for him to immerse himself into "fixing" her.

> **ODO**: I have no reason to hold him for now, and he knows it. I'll watch him the best I can, but I suggest you carry a comm badge with you at all times. Call me at the first sign of trouble.

I realize that this probably also falls under the budget explanation, but it seems bizarre for Odo to have a spare *Starfleet* badge with him.

> **PAZLAR**: I understand. Julian...
>
> **BASHIR**: You let me fly for the first time. I let you walk. We're even. I'll see you in the morning.

And you'll notice that he stopped listening to her.  How romantic, right...?  {% emoji eyeroll %}

Also, speaking of the romance, didn't he make a huge deal about no longer serving as her doctor?  It seems like half a dozen career-ending ethics violations when improvising humanoid medical experimentation on a romantic partner while pressuring her to participate.

> **DAX**: *The Little Mermaid*.
>
> **PAZLAR**: Mermaid?
>
> **DAX**: It's an Earth fable by Hans Christian Andersen. She trades her magical life under the sea for a pair of legs to walk on land.
>
> **PAZLAR**: Didn't she live happily ever after?

In fact, {% wiki The Little Mermaid %} has a failed romance---their prior discussion, you might recall---and her inability to murder her ex causes her to dissolve into foam, where her moral stand and self-sacrifice convinces someone to turn her into an air creature with a soul (one of her bigger goals) able to explore (another major goal), and would go to Heaven after a couple-hundred years of encouraging good deeds.  She didn't *not* live happily ever after, then, depending on your criteria.

Maybe notably, when Andersen's ill-defined associate Edward Collin announced his engagement, shortly before he wrote *The Little Mermaid*, he wrote Collin, "I languish for you as for a pretty Calabrian wench ... my sentiments for you are those of a woman. The femininity of my nature and our friendship must remain a mystery," and sent a copy of the story.  I'll try not to read anything into Andersen, nearly forty years later, falling out of bed and...not *quite* turning to foam, but he never recovered from those injuries, and then fought liver cancer until his death.  But the Danish government also paid him for serving as a national treasure, so maybe he also lived happily ever after, depending on your criteria...

Mind you, when this episode came out, the first connection to the phrase for most people almost certainly evoked {% wiki The_Little_Mermaid_(1989_film)|the 1989 Disney adaptation %}, which coincidentally featured René (Odo) Auberjonois as the weirdo chef.  I'll try not to read anything into lyricist {% wiki Howard Ashman %} getting diagnosed with HIV/AIDS---again, not *quite* turning to foam---during production and tragically dying of the disease in 1991.

You know what?  After going through all that, it seems indefensible to not cast Pazlar as a man.  I don't dislike Ashbrook as much as a lot of the audience did, but the two men probably most associated with the story in the public imagination at least had same-sex relationships, and Bashir already has the fails-to-scan-as-heterosexual relationship with Garak.  If you want to play up the connection with the story, then, you really need to tell *that* story.  It would never have gotten past the (allegedly) deeply homophobic members of the production team, but still...

> **BASHIR**: You know, it might make an interesting side effect of the treatment. It's worth exploring.

We see a lot of quiet moments of this century's Starfleet often trying to scrounge up novel weapons under some other pretext, maybe most prominently in [*Contagion*]({% post_url 2023-02-02-contagion %}), but this "hey, maybe someone would find stun-resistance interesting, in a purely academic kind of way" feels like the most explicit version of it.

> **BASHIR**: Well, you can always try it again someday, if you change your mind.

Sorry to hammer on this again, but his disappointment seems wildly irresponsible.  At best, he doesn't see the difference between a prospective girlfriend and a project.

## Quick Commentary

This episode plays better than I thought it would.  Bashir comes off like a complete creep, but...he does that.  And Pazlar comes off as *mildly* abrasive, but entirely understandably so, in a story three years after---honestly any more than a few days after---the {% wiki Americans with Disabilities Act of 1990 %} passed.

The non-Starfleet plot seems like a waste of time, especially since it only sets up for the "what if we started looking at handicaps as untapped powers" trope, but I suppose better that than victimize her even more with a rescue...

## Conclusions

We get a surprising amount out of this, when it could've stuck with the one issue.

### The Good

The Federation has at least a handful of citizens who enjoy foreign foods instead of wrinkling their noses at it.

They also acknowledge (vaguely) that living in space requires relying on each other.

### The Bad

The episode focuses, almost to an astonishing degree, on how broadly the Federation---especially Starfleet---fails people with disabilities.  Not only that, but they seem to not even understand that a problem exists, absent from the public eye for centuries, but they do expect praise for ignoring what people ask for.  They see inspirational stories of individuals overcoming adversity, rather than their part in creating the adversity.

We even see victim-blaming in this context, where they treat Pazlar advocating for herself as (somehow) attacking everyone.

And at least one of them sees a handicap as something that needs fixing, even when the patient seems less enthusiastic.  This arguably joins with sexism, suggesting that men should have some control over the women who interest them.

Starfleet admits officers who refuse to work with any colleagues.  And it also continues to look like a playground for the wealthy.

Design-wise, Starfleet has no problems with high door jambs.

And as always, we see people thrilled to perpetuate racist stereotypes, in this case denying an artist's heritage because they don't conform to those stereotypes.

Medical treatments suddenly look far more interesting when they might have military applications.
