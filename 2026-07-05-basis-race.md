## Social Constructs

We often dismiss race as a social construct and leave it at that---because we usually need to defend people against some racial stereotype, so "but we made up race" seems expedient---but then to make sense of how race affects people, we probably need a usable definition of a social construct.  I have raised this before, such as explaining why you [can't rely on over-simplified science for your gender bigotry]({% post_url 2021-02-28-genders %}), the aforementioned affirmative action post, last spring's post on [patriarchy]({% post_url 2025-03-30-patriarchy %}), and so forth, but it always bears repeating:  "Social construct" doesn't mean "not real."

The following qualify as social constructs, among so many other ideas.

- Race
- Gender
- Religions
- Nations
- Borders
- Laws
- Governments
- Money
- Languages
- Social Status
- Corporations
- HTTP
- MPEG
- Capitalism

Nobody doubts the reality of money, right?  English?  HTTP had better exist, or else not many of you will read this.  Actually, the term itself *implies* reality, because we construct them.  You can't, for example, ignore a house because a team constructed it; the walls will still stop you from walking through.  "Constructed" only means that the thing wouldn't have appeared without some agency.

When we call something socially constructed, we mean that it arises and persists through cultural agreement.  Sometimes, we have a formal agreement, such as laws in a democracy, where we can vote and [diligently apply pressure]({% post_url 2020-08-30-parties %}) to change what doesn't work.  Sometimes, the agreement looks more hegemonic, such as with money, where we "agree" because most of us can't pay rent with fun anecdotes[^QoOZ1g], rather than because we like the idea.  And sometimes, the agreement comes from some idea lasting so long that it starts to feel like the natural state of the world, like if future civilizations started building on a buried and overgrown {% wiki Sydney_Opera_House|Sydney Opera House|en %} and identified the "cavern" underneath as an impressive natural structure.

[^QoOZ1g]:  And I don't know why I can't pay my bills with stories, given how Republican appointees on the Supreme Court keep insisting that money pretty much equates with speech...

We distinguish something as a social construct in order to make the point that the agreement can *change*, in other words, not that the thing doesn't exist.  If we stop agreeing to respect a particular country's borders or a particular law, they erode, vanishing if our numbers grow sufficient.

## The Origin of Race

Now, while the concept of people coming from different parts of the world consistently having distinct aspects to their appearances---as in a pre-scientific vision of evolution acknowledging that people living for thousands of years in sunnier areas will generally have more melanin in their skin, but with little interest in how that would happen---goes back at least as far as Ancient Greece, the idea of non-superficial differences between such groups doesn't really appear until colonialism takes hold.  And while nobody writes down their motivations for bigotry, we can probably safely assume the intent.

The timing, after all, strongly suggests that race appeared in culture to justify taking land from and enslaving the prior occupants.  If a nation of people can convince themselves of qualitative differences between themselves and these other humanoid creatures---if they can find some distinction deeper than owning more impressive weapons and declare themselves inherently superior---then the governments, the cultural value of ancient artifacts, and the personal autonomy of those other people probably doesn't matter, especially from thousands of miles away.  After all, your people "discovered" that land, and since nobody who matters lives there, you might as well plop a town on the shore after clearing their city away.  You might as well press them into service as laborers, because they probably couldn't have had anything interesting to do before you showed up.  And who could object to a little grave-robbing to fund the enterprise?  Does an owl complain when students pick apart the owl pellets to discover the rodent bones inside?  Do fiddler crabs mind if you knock down their mating displays once they find their mate...?

And if they look different enough, maybe you can even convince the folks back home to treat these people like livestock, ripping them from their homes and destroying their cultures in favor of populating labor camps.

The world already had experience with this, after all, in the form of gender.  For thousands of years, with some notable interruptions, generation after generation has received indoctrination that women can't handle intellectual work (except around the house), physical labor (except around the house), political decisions, or even control over their own bodies.  And they should also dress a certain way, for...*reasons*, certainly not that, if a woman dresses like a man, most people can't tell the difference between her performance and that of a man's, almost like it would give away the socially constructed nature of gender.

## The Triangle

With social constructs and race under our belts, let's talk about the American form of slavery.

When the Spanish began their genocidal campaigns in the Americas, they immediately started enslaving native people.  And when other European nations showed up, they followed suit, probably forcing five million Native Americans into slavery.  The Spanish had made this---rewarding their conquerors with the labor of non-Christian conquered peoples---a tradition, while the rest happily copied the practice as expedient.

As exploitation of the land shifted to more labor-intensive work such as agriculture, they filled the new labor gap by importing slaves from Africa.  And as I pointed out in my post on [accidental Republican arguments for reparations]({% post_url 2021-02-14-reparations %}), they did so because they didn't want to spend money on legitimate labor, which even if you want to ignore the moral problems, today we call that *wage theft*.  And to save (steal) even more money, they created the uniquely American institution of chattel slavery, where people serve as a kind of livestock, bred and disposed of at the owner's whim.  But even proto-eugenics breeding camps couldn't fill the (ahem) staffing requirements, and so developed the triangular scheme that you've probably all seen in a textbook:  Ships left North America with sugar, tobacco, and cotton to sell in Europe; after selling their stock, they'd load up on rum, textiles, and other manufactured goods to sell in Africa; and after selling out in Africa, they'd pack the ships full of humans for sale in North America.

More importantly, they also developed various forms of {% wiki Scientific_racism|race science|en %}---fabricated superstitions about what special abilities and weaknesses people with different skin colors would have---that by purest coincidence {% emoji eyeroll %} happened to identify middle-latitude Western Europeans as management material.  Anybody darker made good workers, and anybody lighter we could ignore, lest the Irish want everybody to treat them like people, too.

We fought a revolution---you might have heard about an anniversary of the announcement not too long ago---and then a war over the continued institution of slavery.  Those both went about as well as they could have, with the less-egregious group (granting that I have a bias on the first one) winning both wars...but we---not literally me, because I wouldn't exist for a while, yet, but certainly people who I have met, and people who still walk among us---also followed up the latter with a hundred years of aggressively trying to exclude the Freedmen from society.

We had {% wiki Redlining|realtors steer Black people|en %} to less-desirable neighborhoods, paid them less, prevented them from voting, and more.  And oh, if that didn't keep them down, then we {% wiki Tulsa_race_massacre|dropped bombs on them|en %}, violently {% wiki Wilmington_massacre|overthrew local governments|en %}, and {% wiki 1985_MOVE_bombing|dropped more bombs|en %}, with plenty more of the same in between.  Oh, and don't forget about the {% wiki School-to-prison_pipeline|school-to-prison pipeline|en %}, exploiting defunded public schools, fewer economic opportunities, and over-policing to make sure that we take advantage of the loophole in the {% wiki Thirteenth_Amendment_to_the_United_States_Constitution|Thirteenth Amendment|en %}, emphasis mine.

> Neither slavery nor involuntary servitude, **except as a punishment for crime** whereof the party shall have been duly convicted, shall exist within the United States, or any place subject to their jurisdiction.

Gosh, I wonder why Black people have such financial and law enforcement problems.  They must have "something in their culture."  It couldn't possibly have come from four hundred years of refusing to let them succeed, after all.  Don't blame us white people, right?  We didn't...do that entire list of things that we did.

By the way, have I ever mentioned that a few studies have used European records mentioning uncommon family names, coming to the conclusion that [social mobility takes centuries {% cc %}](https://faculty.econ.ucdavis.edu/faculty/gclark/papers/Clark%20Cummins%201800-2012.pdf)?  In short, if your family lived in poverty, individual branches of the family that hit it big often lose that windfall over successive generations, while wealthy families that "lose everything" often revert to wealth within a couple of generations[^lpM0mx].  Slavery and Jim Crow policies held Black people in the United States at the lowest of the low economic and social classes for four hundred years---on the order of how long it takes to actually change a family's economic status---with the expectation that they'll now recover quickly with no help.

### Sabotaged Amendment

Earlier, I mentioned that I had a few different versions of posts along these lines, always focusing on *Callais*, so I might as well give some insight into that.

One early draft that I ended up scrapping focused a lot of attention on the proposed {% wiki Congressional_Apportionment_Amendment|Congressional Apportionment Amendment|en %}, originally intended as the *first* amendment to the Constitution.  It never got ratified, and if it hadn't gotten sabotaged---changing the idea of a maximum district size to a *minimum* size, which probably explains why it stalled out---could have solved gerrymandering by overloading the scam.  After all, gerrymandering only works because two pre-conditions exist.  First, we only have four hundred thirty-five Congressional districts[^I0Kd9y], no matter how many people they represent.  Second, over a century of housing discrimination (red-lining) steers non-white people to narrow neighborhoods.

[^I0Kd9y]:  Hence why I keep telling everybody that you/we need to *write letters* to and make appointments with your/our elected officials.  Voting every couple of years only sends a weak signal far too late.  E-mails and calls send a weak signal.  Regularly sending letters (copying to every office and their colleagues) makes a somewhat stronger point that you can reinforce, and standing face-to-face with them, especially with an organized group, sends a much stronger signal, by making you a *person* instead of part of some aggregate on a spreadsheet.  You share your Congressperson with a minimum of half a million of your closest friends, so I assure you that sending an e-mail or leaving a voice mail doesn't get your concerns much attention at all.

Break either one of those conditions---increase the number of representatives dramatically or evenly distribute Black people (among others) throughout communities---and racial gerrymandering falls apart, because you can't "pack and crack" many small, ubiquitous populations.

More interestingly when you look at the states that already did ratify the proposed amendment with precedents saying that a state can't withdraw its ratification, then add the states that have agreed to the {% wiki National_Popular_Vote_Interstate_Compact|National Popular Vote Interstate Compact|en %} (and so have a population or government with some interest in advancing representation), and the states whose populations have (literally) half the representation of their neighbors in other states, which all seem reasonable to pressure into ratification, you'd only come a couple of states shy of ratification, making it a plausible solution...*if* the text of the proposed amendment hadn't mysteriously changed when passing the House of Representatives.  But as it stands, ratifying it wouldn't change anything, and writing a new one would face many more obstacles.

In that sense, the current situation looks like the worst version[^pdJmu8] of the {% wiki Delphi_method|Delphi method|en %}.  As individuals, you contact your "change agent" (the Representative) with your concerns.  They aggregate those requests and pick up the "most popular," then increasingly send out web surveys for voters to inform them of which "popular" ideas should get a priority.  If you don't see yours on the list, well, with a million other voters in your district, who can even say that your idea hit the top-hundred, let alone however few made it to the survey?  And you either submit the survey or you ignore it, in either case becoming part of a manufactured consensus.

[^pdJmu8]:  They don't call it that, but I have most often seen the Delphi method used at companies for scheduling.  Management announces some tight deadline and asks everyone, either individually or in small groups, to estimate how long their parts will take.  They assemble an anonymized version of the full list, round two, pointing out that too much needs to happen to release by the deadline, and soliciting (again, anonymous) suggestions on how to meet the deadline.  And wouldn't you know it?  The consensus always looks like workers making personal sacrifices and cutting corners on testing and usability.  Did everybody except you *really* suggest that, or did management use the anonymous aspect to create a fictional groundswell?  We'll never know, and you shouldn't trust your lying colleagues...

### Vote-Nihilism

And yeah, I know, cue the "well, actually, The Founders™ never envisioned the United States as a *democracy*."  I remember when that load of excrement only reared its head in far-right circles, but apparently smug people (allegedly) on the left would also like to tell you that [voting rights don't really matter {% cc %}](https://jacobin.com/2026/07/an-aristocracy-by-design).  No matter the political positioning, though, this comes off like telling me that we shouldn't think of *e* as a letter, because it falls into the category of vowels.  Actually, I'd call it worse, because check out the roots of the word.

"Republic" comes from the Latin [*rēs*](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/res#Latin) [*pūblica*](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/publica#Latin), or the people's interest, where sovereignty rests with the people or their designated representatives.  Compare that to "democracy" from the Greek [*δῆμος*](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%CE%B4%E1%BF%86%CE%BC%CE%BF%CF%82#Ancient_Greek) [*κρατία*](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/-%CE%BA%CF%81%CE%B1%CF%84%CE%AF%CE%B1#Ancient_Greek), rule by the people, **such as** when the people elect representatives who administer the government on their behalf.  And you tell me that we have a democratic-republic, which compromises between those forms instead of a democracy?

Gosh, that looks so...identical, now that I see it in front of me.  Technically, yes, the root words have some slight nuance where "democracy" suggests something more akin to commoners (something like "people of the land," in some translations), but in a country that no longer (officially) recognizes separate castes, those functionally mean the same thing...except that a republic technically leaves room for allowing non-citizen residents to vote, because the idea doesn't distinguish between the origins of the people.  But either way, this soundly flattens my old response, that republics form a subset of democracies, hence my letter-vowel distinction that I drew above.  This comes much closer to identifying *p* as a letter but not *π*...

The Founders™ had many flaws.  But they absolutely formed a democracy despite their fears of mob rule, so stop trying to play semantic games to justify voter suppression or to insist that we need to scrap the entire system and start over.  Both ideas serve the billionaires, who believe that they can control of any new constitutional convention or Balkanized country.  And whatever people taught you about the world of difference between a democracy and a republic, they lied.  The biggest difference comes from which language coined the term.

Anyway, reading Douglass's words solidified what shape that this post needed to take and when it needed to go out.
