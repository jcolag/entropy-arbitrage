## 6:14 -- Mon 07 September 2026

[<i class="fab fa-mastodon"></i> Asked on Mastodon](https://mastodon.social/@jcolag/117232059369130021)

> Mostly an idle question, but does anybody know of work done for time-/issue-tracking systems that center teams instead of high/low performers and ignoring the people who facilitate cooperation/communication?
>
> Seems like the state-of-the-art lies somewhere around "mention people who helped" or "don't look at the individuals," which...someone HAS to have topped that by now, no...?

For context, since I didn't get any responses and any readers here have thoughts, I have started thinking again about what the software industry will need to become *after* the AI bubble.  Part of that transformation almost certainly requires shifting to highly expressive programming languages---languages that require less code to express more complex algorithms, I mean---formally proving code correct, designing code for automated testing, and so forth, as I have probably written about before.  But I also increasingly think that we'll need to see the team as the worker instead of the individual developer.

Claude and similar LLMs look compelling, because management sees agents writing lines of code and submitting pull requests, looking like a person.  In fact, they present themselves as the "superstars" that job postings used to embarrass themselves looking for, and that many organizations have optimized for, the jackass who writes a lot of code that nobody can maintain.  That brings down morale, which causes the team to not perform as well.  If we can build systems that identify the *team* as the worker, then we'll need to build projects around responsibility for the entire team and project, rather than narrow specializations, and (I would hope) avoid some of those problematic choices.

As mentioned in the toot, searching around for solutions gave me two useless categories of fake-solution.  One faction tries to identify everybody who contributed to every change (which *explicitly* doesn't solve the problem), while the other politely asks everybody (especially managers) to ignore the names when assigning credit and blame (which will never happen except in toy scenarios).

### Internet Archive

This time around, I saw a lot of people during the week wringing their hands about the Internet Archive's "betrayal" as they [prop up a shady LLM-driven climate model](https://mastodon.social/@internetarchive@mastodon.archive.org/117219537666083746) that might *also* have cryptocurrency connections.  How could they throw in with toxic Silicon Valley fads/scams?  Oh, except that, as I talked about in [*Holding Universal Access to All Knowledge Hostage*]({% post_url 2024-09-08-internet-archive %})---and about two years earlier in [the newsletter](https://buymeacoffee.com/jcolag)---the Internet Archive has *always* loved the "information wants to be free" and "move fast and break things" mantras, and like the rest of Silicon Valley, they mean *your* information and things, not theirs.

They repeatedly commit high-profile copyright infringement, ask people to spread the word to make sure that courts can see that they copy with the intent to illegally publish copies of other people's works, and then use the resulting infringement lawsuit against them to beg for donations to fight the "attack on the idea of libraries."  They have happily accepted cryptocurrency for donations since the beginning, too.  And don't forget that the closest thing that they have to a mission statement, "to provide Universal Access to All Knowledge" (aggressive capitalization theirs), sounds like any Silicon Valley "dominate the market" claptrap.  In other words, it should seem perfectly normal that they'd love AI, probably complete with some old blog post about how this technology could help *undermine copyright*, their apparent actual goal.
