* Ignore for ToC
{:toc}

In many ways, this post feels like a waste of everyone's time to me.  As I recall, I have said everything here repeatedly during the life of the blog, for example.  It also all seems clear to me from context, and probably won't come as any surprise to most readers.  In some ways, it feels defensive instead of summarizing what I've said for more than six years.  And critically, the people who should read it almost certainly won't, because they would rather not change their preconceptions.

![Ten people discussing something in an office or academic space, with people in rolling chairs in a circle, turned away from computer carrels](/blog/assets/Discussion-Group-Wiki-Gap-Sudan-02.png "OK, not an actual book club, but close enough...")

However, if only so that I have a full version that I can point people at when they ask about it, let's talk about the [Free Culture Book Club](/blog/tag/book-club).  And an occasional short (I *hope* that this doesn't run long...) post[^Drd_ce] buys me some time to work on other things.

[^Drd_ce]:  John-from-the-future feels the need to inform you that this hit four thousand words, so does *not*, in fact, qualify as "short."

## What Has Come Before

For those of you who haven't already explored, on Saturday, I run blog posts that I have named the *Free Culture Book Club*.  The posts exist for three interrelated reasons.

- Highlight that a diversity of Free Culture projects exist.
- Encourage readers to investigate them.
- Encourage discussion around the works.

For the [sixth anniversary of the run]({% post_url 2026-05-09-messages-universe %}#six-years-hence--orang-u), I described my expectations for these posts as opposed to the exciting-to-me outcome.

> I have probably said this before, but it might bear repeating that, six years ago, my vision for these posts looked more like the Sunday rant-style posts, posting irregularly when I learned about some new project.  Even then, I worried about running out of possibilities and "needing" to talk about works only available under non-commercial licenses, spreading beyond actual Free Culture to keep the idea active.  Instead, with a gap of only three weeks---when I conflated the license of a preservation effort with that of the underlying project---we have talked about the works of at least two hundred creators across three hundred eleven posts (plus the announcement) in six years.
>
> Over six years, I haven't needed to stretch the definition of Free Culture, haven't needed to talk about reference material and journalism---important, but hard to talk about an encyclopedia, for example, in a "book club" format---and haven't even needed to keep rushing back to [Blender Open Movies](https://studio.blender.org/films/) or return to any creator's other work yet.  In other words, even as a vocal proponent of Free Culture, the amount and variety of material out there impresses me and I hope that it impresses readers, too.

In the [series announcement post]({% post_url 2020-05-02-freeculture %}), it looks like I didn't connect all the dots, but you can see the broad idea take shape that I'll talk about "my impressions" of each work, but in hopes of sparking conversation about it, rather than because anybody cares about my opinion on a film.

A couple of phrases stand out in that post that have done a fairly good job (I think) of keeping me on track.  I talk about wanting to "find the worlds...in the most need of some tender loving care," because Free Culture might work best for works with problems, because the flaws provide a better "place to build collaboration than material with high production values."  That especially guided the disclaimer that I probably added too late to the posts, when I talk about things that "work less well."

> Free Culture exists as a special kind of idea.  By licensing a work appropriately, the creator gives each of us permission, authority, and power to make the work our own.  This section tries to remind us all of that, by indicating areas of the project where you, dear reader, might consider it as an invitation to get involved with the project.

Everybody follows that so far, right?  I say repeatedly, and link to it or repeat it consistently, that all Free Culture has value by existing, and that interested readers should look at any defects or ugliness as an invitation to participate in refining it[^KuOhBI], not a reason to dismiss it.

[^KuOhBI]:  Depending on the situation, the specific problems might indicate a different approach to participation, though.  As I have mentioned over the years, I have done some after-the-fact proofreading for a few of the books, because the author provided a location to file "bug reports" to describe the issues and/or "pull requests" to fix them, and I'd call that fun in the right context.  However, sometimes the problem stems from a sociopolitical stance, where it makes *no* sense to figuratively sit with the author and try to talk them through removing the bigotry (for example) from their story, if only because the bigotry probably serves as the foundation in their eyes, though they wouldn't like you characterizing it like that.

OK, then let's dig a bit deeper.

## Structure

This will probably come as news to nobody, but I build every Book Club post on the same structure[^nhaQZI].

[^nhaQZI]:  This happens in the least-sophisticated way imaginable, by the way.  I almost always copy the most recent post and clear out the no-longer-relevant information, before writing each week's post.  You can occasionally spot artifacts that expose this, such as not updating some bit or a few weeks early on where I accidentally deleted a line from the summary information, which I hope that I fixed...

Every week, the post does the following.

- State the work and, for works that have expanded to fill multiple posts, how far along to look.
- Show some representative image to illustrate the post.
- The dry list telling readers the bare minimum about the project:  The title, where to find a copy, the date, license, creator, medium, approximate length, and any content advisories[^YYnjsW] that strike me as worth noting for a broad audience.
- An overview of what I discovered about the project.
- *What Works Well?*, bits that I want to talk about because I think that they'll resonate with at least significant parts of the audience, to encourage people to check the project.
- *What Works...Less Well?*, bits that I want to talk about, because---as discussed earlier---they provide nice "foot-holds" for people who like the project so much that they'd like to do something with it, whether contributing to the project itself (see below) or building some derivative work.
- *Opportunities*, ways to support the project, whether contributing to it directly, paying the creator to encourage them to keep working, or what you have available if you'd like to maintain your own version of the thing.
- *What's Adaptable?*, elements in the story that I could imagine a creator building on in derivative works.
- Starting in May 2026, I started taking a second look at whatever project that the Book Club talked about exactly six years earlier, because the 2020 and 2026 calendars match, giving me a cheap opportunity to remind people of some of these sometimes-obscure projects.
- Finally, a preview of the post planned for the following Saturday, a reminder that I can always use pointers to Free Culture works that I haven't seen before, and formally (because anybody can comment on any post) opening the discussion to readers.
- I suppose that, technically, the image credits come at the end, but that happens in every post across the blog.

[^YYnjsW]:  Notably, I mean the advisories in the sense of potentially affecting someone working through trauma or that might reasonably inspire destructive behavior in a credulous audience, what they used to call something like "antisocial imitable behaviors."  I don't have a list, but I generally think about common trauma triggers, medical triggers (such as flashing lights), policing, self-destructive behavior, violence, and ideas that have historically encouraged violence.  That guide means that it leaves plenty of room for a reader to get *offended* or find something objectionable, an entirely separate issue.

The structure, I think, distinguishes it significantly from a post like this, which you can probably guess that I dumped out in a stream-of-consciousness ramble, jumped around as ideas popped into my head, and slapped on some section headings to direct attention.

### Origins of the Structure

Where did this come from?  The dry bits took loose inspiration from [Chris Sakkas](https://sanglorian.github.io/)'s old **Free and Open Works** wiki, which he recently revived as the acronym-friendlier and more maintainable [Free, Libre, and Open Works](https://sanglorian.github.io/flow/) (FLOW), which I've meant to bring up for at least a couple of months and don't know if I have.  I don't aspire to building a wiki or a catalog, but I wanted to provide enough clean data for other people to do so.

It probably won't happen unless somebody presents me with a strong argument, but I recently started considering pulling that data out into [Recutils]({% post_url 2020-02-05-recutils %}), {% wiki Resource_Description_Framework|RDF|en %}, or even [YAML](https://yaml.org/), with a blog plugin that turns it into the readable form for the blog.  Again, it probably won't happen, because I doubt that we have many people out there clamoring to query for a list of all works that we've talked about released in the same year as a comic in the public domain that we've talked about---as a silly example that the data files would technically enable---nor do I think that anybody who wanted to do such a thing would have any more difficult a time snagging the information from the posts' Markdown files than they would in YAML.

Anyway, to finish answering the question, the long-form bits about my opinions exist for two reasons.

First, I always hope that they spark a conversation, that someone will read my complaining that some piece of the story makes no sense, check out the work (already a win), and tell me what I overlooked that changes the interpretation.  Or any other "look at the work and *do* something" activity, because I don't necessarily need for the conversation to live entirely on my blog.  If a work moves people to talk about the works on their own sites, that could work really well, if we communicate about it so that people know to follow the conversation around.  But I write my bits in the voice of the person going first in a book club discussion.

Second, my often-idle comments about the work verify that I've read/watched/listened to/played the thing rather than pulling it off probably someone else's list.  And it provides the pretext for publishing the pointers every week as blog posts.  Without me talking, the posts wouldn't really belong on the blog, and *nobody in their right mind* would look at a site that promised, every week, to post a bulleted list of eight facts about a Free Culture project, sometimes the same project as last week.  It also wouldn't make for much of a "book club," if we all gathered in my back room (which doesn't exist) and I refused to say a word about the work despite inviting everybody else to talk about it.

That said, I feel that the word *pretext* remains key, here.  I need to say *something* relevant, but I don't say anything authoritative.  In my notes, I sometimes refer to the idea as a "conversational wiki," as if I talk about (hypothetical) encyclopedic entries.

## Pacing

I should mention that, over the years, I have made two adjustments to how I released the posts about longer works.

Originally, as you can see in that introductory post that I linked to earlier, I considered reading/watching/whatever at my own pace and releasing the single post for that work whenever I finished it.  As a "first draft" of the idea, it makes sense, because I don't *need* to post regularly, and this would make every post self-contained.  However, that doesn't feel like a *book club*, any more than it'd feel that way if you had a friend who showed up uninvited to your house at random intervals to demand that you discuss something with them.

Therefore, I came up with my rule of covering roughly an hour of media per week.  I know that I can meet that speed.  Most Free Culture works fit well inside that size.  And it tops out at twelve-to-thirteen thousand words, meaning that a {% wiki National_Novel_Writing_Month|NaNoWriMo|en %}-style novel of fifty thousand words fitting neatly into four weeks, assuming that adults read between two hundred fifty and three hundred fifty words per minute.  NaNoWriMo gave up the ghost shortly after making the ill-advised (and hilarious) decision to promote LLM use in reaching the word count, its use of the guideline also backed by {% wiki E._M._Forster|E.M. Forster|en %} in his day led to at least the first Free Culture writers that I found[^YICjUu] apparently used it for their novels.

[^YICjUu]:  I didn't write my own novel with any thought of participating in an event such as NaNoWriMo, though I did write the first draft late in 2019, and aimed for fifty thousand words.

That went well until [**Life Blood**]({% post_url 2022-12-31-life-blood-1 %}).  I don't want to single it out, but the book...didn't resonate well with me[^p6x1P7], so I found myself spending *nine weeks* about the value of blonde, blue-eyed babies and women's biological clocks as it walked in circles with its non-mystery and constant violations of our female protagonist.  I wouldn't say that it burned me out, because I jumped right into another long-form literary work, but the experience convinced me that long works would get a "sampling" of three posts, until I eventually start revisiting creators' work.

[^p6x1P7]:  It actually makes a perfect example of what I meant about "...less well," earlier in the post.  With some adjustment, the book could have a fantastic protagonist, and the central plot *almost* fits together nicely, especially if the narration would stop peddling regressive politics.  Trim out the worthless boyfriend (or fix the narration and the protagonist to have some awareness of his clear deficiencies), give up on the "mystery" that everybody figured out in the first couple of chapters, and clean up the flow, and you could have a hit.  And entirely unlike if I pointed out similar concerns in, say, **The Devil Wears Prada 2**, the public license gives you permission to overhaul **Life Blood** to improve it.

That final change gives a long work enough attention that it should convince people to go find it, but doesn't turn this into a blog *about* that specific work.  For specific works, I have adjusted the amount of coverage per week, especially if I need to work through a translation or some other obstacle, but I think that it has otherwise worked out well.

### Creators First

I should probably mention one other aspect, tied to the pacing in some ways, but not directly connected to it, my for-the-duration policy of not (yet) returning to creators who have worked on multiple Free Culture projects.  I have a few reasons behind this, which I may not have talked about.

Most personally, this "keeps me honest," so to speak.  I have clear favorites among the works and creators that I've gotten to talk about, as you have no doubt noticed over the years when I keep using them as examples of projects deserving an adaptation or translation.  Some, I check out their work whenever they publish something new, or at least as quickly as I can get to it.  Others, I talk to the creators somewhat regularly.  For a few, I have contributed to the project in some small way.  And others, I don't have any social connection to them, but like their work a lot regardless.  By not going back to previously covered creators until I have thinned out the crowd of creators that I haven't gotten to talk about, the policy assures me that I haven't turned my blog to reacting to someone whose work I value on some personal level.

Related, this keeps me looking for more works to talk about.  Focusing on already-known creators could probably "buy" me at least a year of posts where I wouldn't need to scramble to work out what to do next.  Actually, as I look at a couple of the more prolific creators, one of them could fill about a year alone, depending on how things break down.  And if I didn't *need* to search for new works all the time, I...probably wouldn't, at least not consistently and not so broadly.  And when I ran out of "recurring characters" to talk about, then I'd need to scramble after getting out of practice.

Next, from a bluntly expedient perspective, because I try to link to the creator's web presence whenever possible, you can find their other work and pick what you'd like to check out far more efficiently than I can write posts to direct you there.  If you have a musician's page on whatever distribution service, you don't need my permission or prompting to click *Play* {% emoji play button %} on the other albums.

Finally, probably because of the expediency issue, I see far more value to Free Culture (as a movement) to spread attention to as many creators as possible.  If I published a post for every Blender Open Movie (as mentioned before), for example, this creates a bunch of unnecessary problems.  First, because they've done solid work for such a long time, you already know about them, so you've probably already watched at least a few of them.  The posts probably won't differ all that much from the one for [**Elephants Dream**]({% post_url 2020-06-20-dream %}), except pointing out that the people grow incrementally more talented as they continue working.  And if someone wanted to use the posts as a springboard to building a list of Free Culture works, then the creators make more sense.  By contrast, if I leave you all to explore Blender's work, then you might discover someone new the following week.

## Terminology

Here, we get to the key issues for a lot of people, I suspect.  When starting down this path in 2020, I chose the "Book Club" name for a couple of reasons.

Most importantly, I wanted to give the impression and extend the invitation that I tried to describe earlier in the post, that we gather as a community on Saturday morning[^fwknHT] to casually discuss some Free Culture thing.  It gestures at the small (for now) community to imagine everybody gathering in my (again, nonexistent) den sharing a bag of bagels or however polite people do this.

[^fwknHT]:  Saturday morning for me, I mean.  In the western Pacific, it comes late Friday night.  You get too early on Saturday morning in the western Americas, Saturday afternoon across most of Europe, and Saturday evening-to-night marching across Asia.  Presumably, you know that already, at least for your time zone.

Next, much like I ultimately changed the title of my "developer journal" posts to [developer diary](/blog/tag/dev-journal) posts, I wanted to help destigmatize a largely gendered term.  In the United States, the first secular book clubs arose among people denied formal education, such as women and Black people.  The radical power in bringing disadvantaged people together to socialize over a book grew into an entire {% wiki Women's_club_movement_in_the_United_States|club movement|en %}.  Women still account for the majority of people leading and participating in book clubs, leading to gendered marketing---books sold with endorsements by famous women like Oprah---leading to many people dismissing the institution despite its role in educating millions.

Conveniently, I also like the other common definition of the term in English, the book *sales* "club," where a company sends customers books and expects payment or returns on receipt.  I don't run this like a business, nor do I want to, but I do want to "sell" the works, fully believing that every work has its audience out there somewhere.  They don't all land for me, certainly, but they'll land for someone, maybe someone with the insight and energy to make it better.

Sure, we don't always talk about *books*, but I think that most people would forgive me the imprecision, much like I can't guarantee that an entire club shows up...

Actually, if you think about how this would've worked in a pre-Internet era, assuming that we had Free Culture licenses, then you'd probably need one of two versions of the book club, or a combination of the two.  A version of the idea would probably do a lot of copying and mailing out piles of paper, cassette tapes, and the occasional VHS tape to either individual subscribers or small discussion groups to share further.

I do mostly mean it as an informal gathering to discuss the works, though.

### Distinction

You'll notice what I haven't talked about, I hope.  Nothing about this assesses or evaluates the works that each post talks about, because---as I have said before, sometimes to remind myself not to say anything cruel---we should see even the worst Free Culture works as valuable gifts, some only in more need of attention and care than others before considering them ready.  In that sense, it doesn't matter whether I like something, not even to me, because I primarily (maybe exclusively) want other people to know about it and give it some help if they see fit.  The project's quality doesn't really matter, either, because any (or all) of us can improve it, if we take a liking to what it could become.

In more than six years of posts in this series, in fact, I can't find a single instance where I indicate that the posts "review" anything.  I have used the term to mean re-read, and twice to refer to the work of professional reviewers, but I deliberately do not present myself as a reviewer or critic, because I don't want anything to do with that sort of thing.  I have opinions, like anybody else, but I don't want to sell you on them.

If you don't see the difference, we can check with Wiktionary.

[**book club**](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/book_club) (n):

> A group of people who meet to [discuss](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/discuss#English) the [books](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/book#English) they have been reading.

...or...

> A commercial organization offering [subscribers](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/subscriber#English) a selection of books from a [catalogue](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/catalogue#English).

[**review**](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/review) (n), among other irrelevant meanings:

> An [account](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/account#English) intended as a critical [evaluation](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/evaluation#English) of a text or a piece of work.

Unless you consider "always good, and can always get better with more attention from the right people" a critical evaluation for every topic, then nothing about this should look like a review in intent or form.

### What If I Don't Care, Though

And yet, when creators have contacted me to express disagreement with my comments on some level, they have all framed this as my harshly reviewing their work, always calling it a review, as if they have a commercial product that I have warned consumers away from.  I sometimes wonder if I should note those interactions in the posts, because if they have some discomfort that I didn't enjoy their work and can't understand the explicitly stated book club premise, I don't want to guess how they'd treat a creator who adapts the work in a way that they wouldn't prefer.

I want to make clear that this doesn't happen at all often; I don't keep track, but call it maybe six creators.  More creators have reached out excited that somebody dredged up work that even that had mostly forgotten; you can sometimes see them leave comments on the post.  And of the maybe-six, only one[^XOaDhC] gave the impression that they wanted me to change my mind or suggest that I did something wrong in the "review," by (in that case) not covering the entire book.  And yeah, sorry that I won't commit to writing a dozen or however-many posts publicizing a single person's work, I guess, but I won't for the reasons above.

[^XOaDhC]:  Did that incident, and their inability to let it go without passive-aggressive comments in multiple channels, spark this post?  Maybe it accelerated it, but other people have asked questions about this over the past year or two, so I already had parts of this post written.

Maybe of interest to draw the distinction, prior to the book club, I spent a few months on what I called the [Free Software Social Networking Showdown](/blog/tag/social-showdown) on Saturdays.  There, I took a look each week at (as you might guess from the title) a Free Software social network, walking through how to set yourself up there, the broad user experience, the community, and---distinct from the book club---a binary rating indicating whether I believed (at the time) that the network warranted my and other people's time.  That work, I'd call reviewing.

It could make sense to insert a "don't expect a review, here" disclaimer, much like I include in the [*Star Trek* posts](/blog/tag/star-trek), and I'll need to think about that.
