## Deep Space Nine Season 1 Summary

We have a new home, here, showing that the franchise can do (a bit) more than fly around looking for trouble.  And it seems interesting to me how it plays against **The Next Generation** in two significant ways.

First, early episodes in particular make the point of overtly rejecting the (as many people have put it before me) "we come in peace; shoot to kill" approach seen across the more established show.  Sisko does so through the lens of unprocessed grief, but he openly tells Picard off in the pilot for daring to deliver a lecture on needing to take unglamorous jobs from---they increasingly tell us on his show---among the most glamorous positions in the Federation.  And he carries this through the season, for the most part, patiently playing along with any foreigner or civilian who comes through, *except* for Starfleet officers who he hasn't learned to trust on a personal level.

They frequently contrast him against two (plus a recurring guest) of the other Starfleet officers, dealing with people and problems as we've seen on the *Enterprise*-D, who seem to often exist specifically because the show needs us to roll our eyes at them.  In that sense, Dax seems like an outlier, but Bashir and O'Brien (and...that other guy who nobody cares about) often play scenes out exactly as they would with Riker, La Forge, Wesley, or Data, but they face consequences for their actions, so that we don't applaud them.

Second, and maybe related, while I didn't pick up on this as much when the show aired, when you think to look at it, it seems like the original vision for this show might have intended to stitch it all together from **TNG** cast-offs, characters who the parent show didn't really care about beyond a couple of plot points.  This starts with the rarely used character *actually* coming from the other show, O'Brien, who somehow went from glorified Walmart greeter to a manager of engineers.  Less direct but still fairly transparent, you have Kira, which they never disguised as hastily deriving from Ro Laren after Michelle Forbes started getting cast in features, because you can still see the imprint of a character who left Starfleet and resents needing to return to that hierarchy.  But we also have the imprint of [*The Perfect Mate*]({% post_url 2024-10-03-perfect-mate %})'s Kamala on Dax, where many of her lines make more sense as someone "newly single" than someone whose gender recently changed.  It doesn't take much effort to imagine Quark as an alias for a disgraced Letek from [*The Last Outpost*]({% post_url 2022-06-16-outpost %}) and/or Bractor from [*Peak Performance*]({% post_url 2023-04-13-peak-performance %}).  And even Sisko, while he didn't show on camera or in dialogue until this series, comes out of [*The Best of Both Worlds*]({% post_url 2023-11-09-best-both-worlds-part-2 %}), and you could imagine the character in an unfilmed scene or introduced in place of Shelby.  In that sense, it almost seems like a lost opportunity not introduce us to an ambitious medical student in [*The First Duty*]({% post_url 2024-09-19-first-duty %}), though I suppose that they built that episode almost entirely out of ignoring opportunities that the franchise could've built on...

Anyway, I should point out one inevitable inconsistency in this post and maybe the next few season summaries:  The line feels extremely blurry when thinking about the foreign-ness of the Bajorans, due to the structure of the show.  As such, I note interactions with individual Bajorans (when they feel representative) as fairly routine racial politics, but interactions with representatives of Bajoran institutions as dealing with foreigners.  I made this decision (but not the same decision for the Ferengi), because they work as a single crew, with the intent of them all getting Federation citizenship soon.  In addition, Starfleet officers have probably only really gotten to know (until the pilot) Bajorans with Federation citizenship, since we can probably assume that Starfleet (like many militaries) requires officers to have citizenship in the country by the time of their commission.

## Conclusions

As I did with the original cast seasons and **The Next Generation**, and unlike the discussions of individual episodes, I'll skip the judgment calls and instead break everything down by field of practice.

Before we get moving, as usual, I feel it always worth a reminder that *Star Trek* doesn't show our future.  We see deviations in the timeline, especially when the writers try to predict their futures.  I won't collect them---many people have picked apart the franchise timeline and decided how to resolve the contradictions, including the studio itself---but I wanted to make that point clear early.

### Training and Professionalism

We at least start to see signs that people in the Federation don't always see society as a strict hierarchy[^E1].  This may have swung to the opposite extreme, though, since the crew barely notices when the entire management team fails to show up for their shift[^MH] and openly insult their intelligence[^BL].  This could tie into the older idea that nobody should listen to administrators or diplomats[^F].

Starfleet personnel, at least, continue to show themselves as largely self-absorbed[^E2], though with the occasional ability to snap themselves out of it[^E2].  And they don't seem to get along at all well with their non-Starfleet colleagues[^E2] [^Pa], though some put in the effort to support them[^Pa].

At least some management seems terrible, with the engineering crews lacking resources and priorities, apparently in the belief that they can compensate with effort and by not providing updates[^B] [^N].  They also have no issues with sick people showing up for work[^B], nor do they notice that the two groups in their integrated crew don't spend any non-mandatory time together[^HP].

O'Brien in particular, but also people following his lead, routinely seem negligent enough to endanger people[^Q], with his team getting more done when he takes time off[^N] [^HP], with his estimates arbitrary numbers that sound comforting[^F], while praising his own work in reports to Starfleet[^F].  Likewise, having served as a soldier, he hides from danger to quip[^S].  He also sulks on the job when not getting his way[^F] [^HP].

In fact, safety seems like an absurdly low priority, taking their small ships into dangerous territory with little justification[^BL], not bothering to update the rest of the team when they might take an extra day or so on their trips[^BL], plus having a reputation for endangering everybody with experiments[^WH].  And maybe related, Starfleet continues to push the idea that good leadership means leaving people to die[^MH].

Sisko seems capable of doing much of the work of his colleagues[^Dx] and supports them[^Pr], which might compensate for the seeming incompetence of some colleagues who use plain English as an obscure jargon to justify their actions[^BL] [^F] or assume that somebody else will do their jobs[^WH].

At least many members of Starfleet process trauma from early in their careers, not through therapy, but by inflicting those traumas on young officers who they mentor[^F].

### Economics

We see clear evidence of a class system, with people in civilian work and construction jobs treated as inherently inferior[^E1].

Nobody recognizes the idea of a flea market[^CP].  However, random Federation citizens see the potential of real estate speculation more readily than avowed capitalists[^Pr].

Labor law doesn't appear to protect against sexual harassment by employers[^CP].

On inhabiting a foreign base, the Federation retains the old, substandard mattresses[^Q].

### Science and Technology

It (still or again) seems unclear who in the Federation has routine access to replicators[^B].

#### Design

Doors seem to primarily exist to endanger people in an emergency[^Q].

Computers track everybody's movements, except when it matters, and with no fallback[^Pa] [^MH].  They also seem to evade questions[^V].  Some would prefer computers to act subservient than get things right[^F].

On the security-specific side, they allow their computers to direct people to weaponry[^CP].  And we have actual confirmation that computers use short passwords[^HP].

#### Health Care

While we never see any participation in counseling, we see multiple examples from different angles showing that their therapy primarily relies on people "muscling through" their trauma, instead of understanding and engaging with it[^E1].  They certainly don't have any protocol to reach out to someone in emotional distress in order to nudge them in a better direction[^E2].

We continue to see branded drugs in use[^B].

The Federation sees death as something that some people recover from[^Pa].  They also believe that humans have large unused portions of their brains[^Pa].

#### Academia

Education for children seems to exist as mostly a day care service, isolating the children from the public[^MA], and with no interest in training for the instructors[^MA] [^N].  Adults broadly also see education as an inconvenience, where they enjoyed abusing substitutes[^N].  And the school seems like little more than somewhere for the teacher to have an audience while she rants[^HP].

Elite universities can overlook some criminal activity if having someone on the faculty provides some advantage[^Q].

People broadly seem less curious than safety would demand[^Pa], and see their own ignorance as an interesting topic of discussion without trying to fix the problem[^Du].

At least O'Brien puts more trust in weather forecasts than the actual weather[^S].  But they don't look up the definitions to words that they don't already know[^Pr].

### Government, Law, and Corrections

The Prime Directive still apparently means whatever the speaker needs as a rhetorical device in any given conversation[^E1], so flexible that it can become an argument for ethnic cleansing[^BL].

Federation law doesn't seem to have a problem with threatening to arrest and convict children in order to coerce cooperation from their families[^E1].

People understand that law enforcement groups can't investigate themselves[^MA].  They also seem to have a sense that the spirit of a law or regulation doesn't always find expression in the letter, and so have a willingness to bend the rules for moral reasons[^CP] [^Pr].

Federation law operates on a presumption of retributive justice[^Dx] and see law as primarily important in enforcing a social order, even if that harms innocent bystanders[^F].  The specifics seem unclear, though[^Du].

### Religion

Federation citizens seem to openly object to either any or other religions, even those provably recounting factual incidents, possibly even objecting to the existence of religion[^HP].

### Ethics

Many of its own officers see Starfleet as often standing on the wrong side of history[^Pr].

### Family

At least O'Brien apparently needs supervision to read his daughter a bedtime story[^WH].

#### Gender Roles

At least some men feel entitled to the affections of a woman who they find attractive[^E1] [^Q] [^WH], even crossing clear boundaries[^MA] and even taking advantage of a compromised psychological state[^WH], though they definitely understand *how* to conduct themselves romantically[^Q].  People also continue to expect women to present themselves as friendly and open[^Q] [^WH], and feel free to gossip about them[^Q].  Society also blames them for the bad behavior directed towards them[^Dx], which the women internalize[^WH].

People have archaic (even for the 1990s) ideas about dating[^MH], including aggressively flirting with them at their place of employment[^F].

Some people have lingering forms of transphobia, but understand that it doesn't get to affect how they treat people, and that they need to work on it[^MA], though the overtly misogynist culture on display seems more accepting of people changing gender[^MA].

An expectation exists that (civilian) women don't have serious careers[^MA], possibly extending to military officers of other cultures[^HP], and men don't generally seem to value the independence of their lovers[^Q].  At least some men also need reminders of their romantic commitments to stop them from ogling and assessing submissive women[^S].

People don't seem to believe that women can fight[^MA], and violence against women has a significantly different moral valence than identical violence against men[^Dx].

Nobody seems to recognize---or they recognize but don't care about---common signs of potential domestic violence[^CP].  In fact, we get the impression that "normal" people don't pay much attention to women[^Dx].  Probably related, many men see friendships with women as an inferior substitute for sex[^WH].

Some men, by contrast, value their relationships with women[^Pr] and stand up for their rights and general autonomy[^F].

#### Sex

They seem to view homosexuality as literally unthinkable or impossible[^MA].

#### Race Relations and Nationalism

We finally see more diverse crews[^E1], though in less-prestigious postings.

However, we also see all sorts of racism directed against the Bajorans, including trafficking in stereotypes[^E1], using inappropriate and demeaning terminology[^E1] [^HP], ordering people to manipulate the government[^E1], dismissing the place as a wilderness to colonize[^E1], ignoring their holidays[^N], treating their culture and religion as an inconvenience to endure or route around[^BL], treating their problems and traditions like a show for their amusement[^S], dismissing their concerns entirely[^S], treating their work as inherently inferior[^HP], and dismissing their religion (which all evidence points to as proven historical fact) as annoying superstition[^HP].  Maybe unsurprisingly, most Bajoran officers don't bother to socialize with their Starfleet colleagues, with nobody on the Starfleet side noticing[^HP].  Less common, we see the non-humanoid part of the Trill described in monstrous ways[^E1] and broadly racist comments about other cultures[^BL] [^WH].

To the extent that clones qualify as a "race," people generally seem to see clones as nothing more or less than ordinary people with a less sociable birth[^MA].

Safety for children seems to work as a proxy for racism[^MA].

#### International Relations

Some people see it as heroic to destroy opportunities for peace with a rival power[^E1].  And Starfleet apparently claims all benefits to anything their people discover, with the locals who would technically own it coming in as a distant second consideration[^E2].  Without immediate strategic or economic benefit, it also appears that the Federation quickly loses interest in "causes."[^PP]

The Federation still has a troubled reputation outside its territory[^E1] [^E2] [^WH] [^HP], and even within its borders[^E2], with more measured and realistic-sounding criticisms[^E2] [^WH].  They do have some supporters[^Dx].  However, Sisko raises eyebrows for accepting a small government's sovereignty[^V], suggesting truth to the criticisms.

When pursuing adding a government to the Federation, nobody seems to feel the need to pitch it in terms of benefits to either party[^E1].  They also appear to operate under the assumption that the newcomer should agree and sign on to Starfleet's agenda in the area[^PP].

We see a blatant belief that every culture commits war crimes and terrorism[^PP], and don't seem to object to "reluctantly" proposing genocide as a solution to their problems[^BL].  Most of them also seem to have an absurdly narrow definition of war crimes, which seems to exclude everything but mass murder[^Du].  They also find it funny to joke about putting visitors from outside the Federation in danger[^CP], and arm themselves when coming to their aid[^CP].

Parents blame their children's rebellion on foreign influences[^MA] [^MH] [^N].  They openly see the Ferengi in particular as inherently criminal and incompatible with the Federation[^N], comfortably repeating propaganda[^V].

People expect foreigners to immediately assimilate into Federation culture, including speaking idiomatic English[^CP], and the Federation similarly seems to believe that first contact follows a standard script[^MH].  While they see foreign cultures as repulsive, they support humans appropriating the repulsive aspects of those cultures for their own purposes[^CP], likewise seeing foreign cultures as repulsive for doing what Federation citizens do[^F].  They also treat foreigners far less formally than they do their own colleagues[^Pa].

The Federation seems to have a specific, narrow view of "civilization" that automatically excludes anyplace that they don't know about[^Q].  This may relate to the Federation taking months before even considering contacting cultures on the other side of the wormhole[^V].

They also seem to dismiss the idea of calling experts in to repair foreign equipment[^Dx].  But the Federation signs treaties with other governments allowing for the violent abduction of suspects[^Dx], and doesn't seem particularly concerned with the rights of extradited citizens[^Dx].  They also sometimes work to deliver pharmaceuticals to other cultures[^Pa].

Oddly, they don't read anything into the---largely adversarial---Cardassians not securing their abandoned files[^BL].

The Federation occasionally (rarely?) uses its outsize power in trade negotiations to pressure other governments into improving their policies[^DP].

#### Socialization

Some people do show empathy, both for individuals[^E1] [^E2] and communities[^E1].  The powerful, however, refuse to engage with any damage that they might have caused[^E1], though not all[^E2].

Enough people feel stifled by how the Federation operates that they seriously consider moving beyond the border[^E2].

Federation citizens seem to have learned that you can physically touch your friends and colleagues and say kind things, for non-sexual reasons, without exploding[^MA].

At least some human groups kill animals for entertainment[^CP].

While everybody finds Bashir grating, none of them seem to see any value of teaching him (even impolitely) in order to reduce that annoyance[^S].

People see enthusiasm as embarrassing[^E1] [^DP].

When O'Brien lashes out violently at a shop-keeper who doesn't want to do business with him, people seem oddly comfortable with it[^HP].

#### Fashion

We get glimpses of civilian clothing[^E1], here and there, though the possibility exists that Bajorans and other cultures have more of an impact than the mainstream Federation does or would.

Starfleet officers, at least, keep formal clothing for long periods, packing and carrying them to wherever they get stationed[^MH].  It also appears that they make their uniforms stifling and uncomfortable by design, since smaller governments seem to have access to better technology[^Pr].

#### History

Military history gets updated quickly enough that recent actions by the timeline of the franchise become literal textbook examples[^E2].
