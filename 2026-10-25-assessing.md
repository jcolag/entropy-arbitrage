Yes, another late-in-the-day Sunday post means that I only realized that I should write it late Saturday night.

![An abstract piece, with round objects in two layers on the bottom, pushing up to irregular pyramidal structures, up to wavy vertical beams; all objects alternate shading](/blog/assets/oli-maia-abstract-007.png "Other than the segregated shapes, this actually almost fits with my direction")

Last month, I put together a post talking about the [current threats in Free Software and Free Culture]({% post_url 2026-09-13-what-next %}) more broadly, how these threats build on the flaws in the movement from the start, and tried to think about how to reasonably deal with these problems.  I still don't have solid solutions, and invite people to (as usual, but more) contribute to the conversation or build on what I have in your own direction.

Right now, though, I see---and saw in that post---a handful of starting points.

- Find a safe base to build on, projects with politics that already reject AI, fascism, and elitism.
- Make freedom political, that we want freedom for humans and our communities, and we care only to the extent that we can use it to serve social justice.
- Rebuild the movement as something that excludes people who make others feel unsafe or try to dominate the conversation.
- Organize, so that the movement becomes a force to keep creators honest.
- Professionalize, so that at least a subset of creators verifiably understands the tools and ethical issues.
- Recruit (many) people who'll keep this vision of the movement active and pushing forward.

This time, I want to imagine what some of this might look like on the other end, proposing a speculative vision of what we could plausibly do.  These might not all describe the same organization, and in no way to I propose that people follow me because I outline some vision.  I want to get the conversation started, not declare it finished, as so many of our institutions seem to believe.

## Quality and Health

Yesterday, I commented on Lemmy[^cOr2qD] that, while Free Software has a huge hypothetical advantage over proprietary software in that we can [audit a project's code for quality](https://lemmy.sdf.org/post/60881145/25509980), the individualism and libertarian that pervades conversations about Culture and Software Freedom prevents it, because it imagines that either each of us carefully picks through the many millions of lines of code running on our machine---leaving time for pretty much nothing else, I imagine---or trusting the developers, the latter of which doesn't differ substantially from proprietary software.

[^cOr2qD]:  Think Reddit for the Fediverse.

I'd like you to *imagine*, though, a Free Culture project (or several that compete) dedicated to doing this sort of work.  You'd have the same volunteer infrastructure as a large Free Software project, but testing important projects, evaluating whether somebody can reproduce the published builds, and maybe even formally verifying whether they work as advertised.

You couldn't have it connected to any specific project, because you can't have quality assurance beholden to a development team.  Separating them from projects also makes them the experts in this validation.  Also, if you imagine this as every team having its own QA subgroup, then that doesn't differ from today.

We might similarly imagine such a team evaluating the health of project teams, based on objective measurements of what works.  Do they welcome and onboard new contributors in a way that sticks, for example?  Do they make decisions in ways that maximize long-term benefits to users, or does the process go with whatever the boss prefers?  Especially if we position the movement as more political, then those questions matter at least as much as the technical merits.

## Licensure and Regulation

The Free Culture space has plenty of petty certifications, for lack of a better term.  If you have a few hundred dollars to throw around, for example, Creative Commons will train and certify you as having "expertise in CC licenses in order to openly share and engage in our digital commons."  You (and Creative Commons) can feel free to disagree, but this always seemed incredibly silly, because if the licenses have nuance, then they need to rewrite those licenses.  Also, they only have six licenses, and you can read all of them in a sitting.

Likewise, the FSF has their [Respects Your Freedom certification](https://ryf.fsf.org/about), which actually covers less than their description implies, and what it covers has a bunch of exceptions, so that they can get any manufacturers on board.  And, as you might guess, they don't care about the code running coprocessors, but they do care that manufacturers "must use FSF approved terminology," especially "GNU/Linux."  Yes, for the FSF to approve your product, you *can* ship proprietary software, but absolutely may not refer to the operating system as Linux, because I guess that only one of those "respects your freedoms," in their eyes.

### People

However, as I have hinted, we could have actual valuable training that makes the recipient a more valuable contributor to projects.

- Why we do this
- The most common Free Culture licenses, their differences, and why they matter
- The structures of and cultures around major projects
- Development and distribution tools
- Funding models
- Ethical standards

We could probably broaden this, but you get the point.  Rather than these micro-credentials and corporate partnerships, we could have professionalized Free Culture creators who can help projects more than "show up and handle tickets."  And the issuing organization can review that work, to ensure high standards.

### Projects

We could also have project-level certification, in theory.  Does the project include anything that would prevent a fork?  Does its management have policies that limit its power?  Can an interested third-party reproduce the build that they release without much hassle?  Do they keep all their decision-making out in the open?  What quality assurance processes do they have?

Personally, I would much rather run software that "does Free Software right" than software that happens to have gotten popular enough that it has gotten substantial testing.  And many readers probably already know that, since my Monday posts occasionally chronicle my ongoing attempts to find a Linux replacement, and for a device with an ARM processor in particular.

## Funding

As I mentioned in that previous post, it increasingly looks like Omarchy exists to centralize funding in Free Software, in order to use the money to pressure projects into accepting more fascist-friendly policies as a condition to receive a grant.

Replicating this for more altruistic purposes would take a lot of effort, coaxing people with money to part with it, and having policies in place that prevent those donations from coming with influence over the plan.  However, the movement also probably needs to do this, because we can't afford to rely on corporate types who want a competitive advantage for their investment or AI-forward foundations who see inclusion as a problem to stamp out.

We also need to see more funding for *small* projects.  Everybody sees the need to fund the famous projects that already have plenty of funding.  But the lone artists or developers trying to get something new off the ground can make that support go much further.

## Collective Action

I called out this possibility in that previous post, but it bears repeating.

Like in the corporate world, few actual creators want LLMs involved in the process.  We have all given the technology a fair shake and found it lacking in critical ways, even before you consider the costs on every level.  Unlike employment, nobody rational dreams of making their hobby go away.  Almost nobody thinks that Free Culture needs to ship faster---even though it doesn't *actually* happen faster---at the expense of quality.  Normal people worry about the odds of plagiarism increasing.  You get the idea.

Likewise, nobody (who we'd want to deal with) wants leadership that can't wait to tell you why he (always "he") thinks that racism makes good policy or why we shouldn't judge the people in Jeffrey Epstein's orbit.  We certainly don't want leaders who think of community as a sometimes-convenient accident or speak at length about how they no longer see the project that they control as relevant.

We could come up with a thousand more examples, but it should suffice to say that leaders do this to protect their personal interests---especially their interest in giving orders and having them carried out without question, rather than needing to convince volunteer activists---or the interests of their donors.  And because of the disconnect, you can increasingly see rhetoric implying that we have clear corporate-like class divisions, where leaders don't need to care what anybody else thinks, and the leaders' favorite contributors only need to care about the leaders; the rest of us only matter as a last resort.

This doesn't need to happen, but like the corporate world, it requires solidarity, not stronger individual negotiating.  And it might require solidarity across projects.

As I floated before, imagine that, when Project-A's leadership announces something destructive or otherwise behaves badly, not only do their developers walk away---say, putting their time towards handling issues from a pre-arranged well-managed project---but an announcement goes out to *other* project teams to do the same, pressuring the leaders of Project-B, -C, -D, and so forth, to also try to intercede with Project-A.

Now, when some leader says *do the open source thing and fork it* if you don't like something, then a walkout not only makes the point that good leaders don't act like that, but it also makes it clear how much support a fork might get.  It turns the threat from leadership into a threat *to* leadership.

## Recruiting

We need more people in the space.  I don't think that anybody would find that controversial.

People have noted that, in past years, Linux had at least some outreach, in the form of user groups and install parties, but most have fallen apart.  Likewise, I have mentioned that [Software Freedom Day](https://digitalfreedoms.org/en/sfd) now actually advises *against* trying to sell people on the movement.  And even those only deal with convincing people to use Free Software (not even Free Culture in general), not creating or maintaining it.

As far as I know, only [Outreachy](https://www.outreachy.org/) does much of anything to try to bring people into working on projects.  But even they, you'll notice, assume that candidates will approach them.  Candidates need to already trust in their value, understand that opportunities exist, and believe that they can get the paid internship.  While we need that sort of bridge for those people, they have already gotten on board.

The movement needs more than figuratively preaching to the choir or nudging people to replace proprietary products.  We need to *find* people who we can convince to create and share.

## Education

Journalist Natasha Singer has made the rounds of interviews lately, promoting her book---which I haven't read, but probably should---**Coding Kids: Big Tech's Battle To Remake Public Schools**, about companies donating equipment and supplying computer science course curricula to connect students with their products.  Those students then go on to jobs where they often demand to work with what they already know, turning them into unpaid sales representatives for the company.

You might already know that I'd love to see something like this, but centering on Free Culture, because I wrote a post about the hypothetical of funding a [Free Culture-centric education]({% post_url 2025-05-04-30m-free-education %}).  I focused primarily on literature, because literature classes have a tendency to center on the experiences of white elites, but that approach would work extremely well to create a high school computer science (or computer art, or similar) curriculum that centers on Free Software and Free Culture assets.

And as a bonus, that potentially feeds recruitment, because you now have cohorts of budding developers familiar with large projects that they can contribute to.  You could make that *part* of the curriculum, contributing code, documentation, code reviews, and so forth, making them better developers than a class that only has narrow projects with a proprietary system.  I have to assume that schools would find that valuable.

## Advocacy and Pressure

You might recall that, last summer, I had some unkind things to say about the [Creative Commons Signals initiative]({% post_url 2025-06-29-signal-change %}), an incoherent attempt to blame the victims of AI companies by suggesting that we should try---as individuals, mind you, because why organize?---*asking the companies nicely* to treat our content better.  For reasons that I'll probably never understand, they still push this, occasionally, claiming that they'll fix the "implementation" flaws that they think people objected to Real Soon Now.

This year, they seem to want to reevaluate slightly, publishing a paper on [guidance for license-users](https://creativecommons.org/2026/09/03/guidance-on-using-cc-licenses-in-an-ai-ecosystem/) amid AI-enabled abuse.  And they came up with, in effect, "stay the course."  Oh, and they also need to push CC Signals.  Their analysis doesn't really involve AI at all, as far as I can tell, only picking their favorite license for different kinds of work.

Meanwhile, I have also pointed out that the [FSF wants to get involved in AI safety](https://www.fsf.org/news/fsf-is-working-on-freedom-in-machine-learning-applications), hinting that they'll happily throw justice under the bus, if they can get some company to accept the Four Freedoms somehow.

I never want to see yet another Foundation that wants to write papers about the value of sharing to society.  But I do want to see some organization approach the existing foundations and ask them---with the heft of an engaged membership behind them---to remove their heads from their lower intestines and actually try to solve problems that "their" creators face.

For example, both organizations have concluded that training an LLM doesn't violate any copyright, because it pretty squarely nails all four criteria for Fair Use in most countries.  I agree with that analysis, seeing it as the only possible result if we don't amend laws.  However, it reminds me of an old joke.

> A person gets into a hot-air balloon, but everything goes wrong and they get knocked unconscious.  Waking up, an unknown time floating without control, they find themselves drifting near the surface, and see someone walking down the road.  Excited, they scream to get the pedestrian's attention, calling down, "where am I?"
>
> "You're in a balloon," the person replies, and continues walking.

The joke goes on to finger the walker as an engineer, providing technically correct but functionally useless answers.  Some versions compare the balloon-rider to management, coming down from on high demanding answers without providing context and complaining about the resulting quality, but we only need that first part.

Creative Commons---and I guess the FSF, though they seem less communicative on the topic---happily provides the technically correct but completely useless answer, narrowing down our location to the balloon.  They seem entirely uninterested in what happens when somebody uses the already-trained [LLM to plagiarize work released under the terms of a Free Culture license](https://www.davidrevoy.com/article1178/license-update-ai-derivation-prohibited-on-all-my-art-lore-stories-and-comics).  I use licenses that require attribution and reciprocal licensing, for example, but none of these foundations seem interested in creating a social norm around respecting the licensing terms or even copyright in general, or even saying that this usage definitely violates their license.

It feels like a murder mystery show gone wrong, in some ways.  We find out that somebody poisoned the victim with strychnine.  When we find out that one of the suspects bought several canisters of strychnine, somebody intercedes to remind us that we have no laws against buying strychnine.  And then, we work out that the buyer has indeed poisoned the victim...everybody repeats that no laws prevent buying the poison, so we can't do anything about it.  Roll credits, stop worrying so much about "justice" or "laws against murder."

They *could* do it.  They have the political capital and the platform to at least try.  But they don't, and repeatedly make it clear that we individuals can't force them to change direction on anything.  Would they listen to a peer organization?  I suspect that they might, especially if that organization represents a fair number of creators.

## Politics

I hinted at this at the top, and in my previous post on the subject, but honestly, at this point, I see no reason that Free Culture should try to stay politically neutral.  Culture Freedom, maybe especially Software Freedom, has little to no meaning to people, if the secret police wants to abduct them or if a political party wants to otherwise eliminate them from the public sphere.

We do this work *for* community, remember.  If our community hurts, then the projects and the movement hurt.  Therefore, we can only support the projects and advance the movement by protecting and supporting the community.  That means getting political.

## Opening the Floor Again

I'd like to draw attention to the fact that many of these purposes defy standard organizational structures.  Among the reasons that I keep pointing out that I don't see myself trying to build this---apart from not having the experience or the audience, and not seeing the point of yet another half-baked organization led by a white guy---none of the structures that I can think of map neatly to really any of these needs.  You can't have a recognized union, because labor law focuses on reimbursed employment.  You probably can't have a traditional tax-exempt non-profit, because this requires a lot of political messaging.  Many other traditional structures require already having solved the problem, so that the "congregation" (to borrow a pre-industrial analogy) already has more authority over how things run than the "clergy."

And I don't think, not even for a moment, that I have created an exhaustive list.  Surely, we need more.  Therefore, as I said last time through:

> Like Wikipedia's "anyone can edit" line, I always write posts with the expectation that people *may* reply if they want.  Here, though, I feel some urgency to actually seeing some of these problems solved.  As such, I make the [blog's Matrix room](https://matrix.to/#/#entropy-arbitrage:matrix.org) available as a place to workshop these or other ideas, and we can always branch off to other rooms, if it becomes more serious than brainstorming.  Comments on the post also work.  Or contacting me directly, we can hammer out ideas to propose in another forum, and branch out to bring more people in.
>
> I think that we, as a community, *can* solve the problem, but we do need to see it all as a single problem, and need to stop thinking of it as a problem to solve by waiting out the attacks or walking away to a quieter space.

If you have given this problem any thought, I'd love to hear those thoughts, and see if we can put something useful together.
