## Fantastique Unfettered #4 (Ralewing)

Before we get into this, I do want to reiterate that---as far as I can find---the project only exists as the four "dead tree" issues of the magazine.  To read along, you'll need to have your favorite book store grab a copy.

The first issue (page 66) provides us with a mission statement for the overall project.

> Fantastique Unfettered, a Periodical of Liberated Literature exists to provide well written, compellingly readable, original stories of fantasist fiction to readers. We will publish both established and new writers alike, and intend no certain genre as transcendent over another, though Science Fiction should be sent to our sister publication, M-Brane SF. Some stories might look like SF or Fantasy depending on one's perspective (stories that follow in the tradition of Philip K. Dick, for instance). We are interested in such tales. There are also specific areas of overlap that are outlined in our Writers Guidelines.
>
> Fantastique Unfettered will continually seek ways to generate revenue with the end goal of paying creative talent the best rate possible.
>
> Fantastique Unfettered is not a magazine. It takes many forms. Those forms include the near-anachronistic magazine. What forms will follow, we look forward to discovering.
>
> Fantastique Unfettered is guided by a transcendent purpose and the concept that the Periodical, whatever its Form, is at heart an idea factory.

For reference, Christopher Fletcher's [M-Brane SF](http://www.mbranesf.com/) seems to stand alone as the only part of this overall project that still has an Internet presence, though I don't see any evidence that it released any of its work under a Creative Commons license.  It also holds out hope for a relaunch in 2013.

I might also point you to the piece that editor Brandon H. Bell---whose name you might recall from his editorial work on [**The Aether Age:  Helios**]({% post_url 2024-07-20-helios-1 %}) that we talked about a couple of years ago---[wrote for Question Copyright](https://jcolag.codeberg.page/qco/208-small-press.html) around when these magazine issues came out.  It actually comes from the second issue of the magazine, which we'll talk about next week.

> Our alignment cover of Fantastique Unfettered, Issue 1is not indie against corporate, small against large, or fan against pro. Those are foolish stances. Our alignment is one against obscurity, expressed via a pragmatism that acknowledges money may or may not follow our good karma. We certainly hope it does: our goal, after providing quality fiction to our readers, is to pay writers professional rates.
>
> ...
>
> I’m not an ideologue; I’ve stated in blog posts that I don’t know how well CC-BY-SA scales, and for the Stephen Kings of the world, traditional copyright may be the only reasonable default for their work. Creative Commons is a tool, in a toolbox that includes traditional copyright, and I have no prohibition against the latter (though even if I reach “rock-star” level, I would ensure my work returns to the culture at some point).
>
> ...
>
> Writers deserve to be paid for their work, and we hope that you, dear reader, will take an active interest in supporting short fiction. If not _FU_, then some other venue. As a writer, I hope to someday make loads of cash at my craft and to have people bemoan my place on the NYT list. _That hack_, they’ll complain as I laugh my way to the bank. (Yeah, it’s a _writer thing_.) So, a final reminder that our use of Creative Commons licensing is not purely ideological or a revolt against traditional publishing.
>
> ...
>
> To close on a theme, perhaps our Caesar is that societal voice addressed to those who would participate in the culture, that suggests: **you are a consumer, only**.
>
> We have come not to praise Caesar, but to bury him.

As a tangent, I find it funny to see it in the context of Question Copyright, because they include a big "we don't agree with everything here" disclaimer.  I can't help wonder if that writer's bigger concern comes from the suggestion that copyright might have some validity, since they don't mean "question" in the sense of exploring to find the good and bad parts.  Alternatively, because they fully believed that since "information wants to be free," that art shouldn't cost anything except through the [cost of manufacturing and distributing physical media](https://questioncopyright.org/understanding_free_content.html), so the idea that writers deserve payment for their work might have struck them as heretical.

Unlike the other issue titles, "Ralewing" doesn't have a clear derivation.  I can't find a closer match than building on the French [ralé](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/ral%C3%A9), which could refer to groups of lower-class people or, probably by extension, a predator's meal, particularly a bird's.  That doesn't fit, but at least might vaguely gesture in the right direction.

For this issue, we'll specifically read *Three Tales of the Devil's Wife* by Carmen Lau, *Self-portrait* by Shweta Narayan, *'cubus* by Dan Campbell, *Azif* by Lynne Jamneck, *The Bachorum Principle* by Brenda Stokes Barron, *Clones evaporate faster* by Kristine Ong Muslim, and *Sons of the Law* by Hal Duncan.

## What Works Well?

While I'll talk later about the more limited selection of fiction in this issue, I do want to call out the wider diversity in this case.  Mostly in past issues, we mostly saw either magical realism or Tolkien-inspired high fantasy, with one or two steampunk bits.  This time around has elements of that, but expands to (unresolved) mystery, science fiction, and Western.  That comes much closer to "something for everyone."

Maybe I'll provide Shweta Narayan's *Self-portrait*, to give you a rough idea of the quality.

> I am silences worn frayed, stitched  
> with ragged lines of text  
> unvoiced. My words leave  
> holes. 1 filled the air, once  
> gasping to keep up  
> with this cuckoo tongue, thinking  
> it was mine. My body knew. Fought  
> its patchwork monster self  
> years before I owned it. Now I wait  
> for lightning  
> for the story  
> to start, for these ill-made seams  
> to come undone.

I find it fairly well-done.

Likewise, *'cubus* provides a nice little meditation on sexual double standards.

## What Works...Less Well?

{% include lesswell.md %}

{% imgr The prospective cover for the fifth issue|fantastique-unfettered-5-sm.png|I got as close to the spine as I could. %}

It doesn't look like anybody involved knew it---they even preview the cover for the fifth issue, with one story called out, which you can see either above or to the right, depending on how you read these; they also solicit advertising for the next year of magazines---but this still *feels like* a project petering out with its final issue.  We have three interviews, a reprint of a then-decade-old novella, an extensive review/rebuttal of a non-Free novel, and even at least one typo (the next issue's number) in the table of contents.  As a result, the original fiction, which I had imagined as the primary purpose of the project[^kujX__], gets squeezed to seven stories.  And yet, the introduction does call it "the death issue"...

[^kujX__]:  Although, as I think about that, it comes off as an unfair assessment, since traditional literary magazines do tend to mix fiction and non-fiction pieces.  That doesn't cover the coverage of non-Free work, though.

Speaking of typos, *The Bachorum Principle* has a fairly glaring recurrence, describing the five "tenants" of the principle, presumably intended as [tenet](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/tenet), not someone who leases real estate.  As I've mentioned over the years, I wouldn't make a big deal about this in many cases, because we usually see digital projects that often have some sort of version management where someone (like myself) could contribute a fix.  But since this lives almost exclusively on paper, anybody borrowing from the text needs that flagged, and one consistent error like that raises the likelihood of others that I didn't notice.  In other words, take care if you do anything with the contents.

## Opportunities

I wouldn't bet on any involvement, beyond the extent to which buying the magazines trickles back through the system to any creators who still have a claim after thirteen years.

## What's Adaptable?

We get the Devil's wife.  A (mentioned) poem combines the legends of the incubus and succubus into a presumed single kind of entity.  And we have the twenty-second century settlement of Numerica on some distant planet.

## Six Years Hence:  Where Are the Joneses?

Well, I don't know that I have anything to say about [**Where Are the Joneses?**]({% post_url 2020-09-26-joneses1 %}), given that the project wrapped so long ago that I don't think that Ford even makes the car that the show uses as product placement anymore.  Both Emma Fryer and Neil Edmond continue to get work, though.  The production company, {% wiki Baby Cow Productions %}, already had a presence in British television, but has since gone downright "respectable," going on to launch (owner) Steve Coogan's assorted *Alan Partridge* shows, **Moone Boy**, **Up the Women**, and Alan Carr's **Changing Ends** on top of a bunch of other work that you might've heard of, in addition to getting involved with **Red Dwarf**.

Apparently, a group calling itself Kino Technologies released an iOS app that let people experience the multimedia story---the episodes, blog posts, and tweets---as if the show released in parallel in 2024.  I'd link to the website and ask for volunteers to try it out, but the whole thing looks hilariously shady, even skirting the line of whether this counts as a derived work, since it certainly doesn't mention the license anywhere or make the app available elsewhere.
