## In the Hands of the Prophets

Closing out the first season, here, this episode landed between [*Descent*, part 1]({% post_url 2025-05-15-descent-1 %}) and [*Descent*, part 2]({% post_url 2025-05-29-descent-2 %}), though three months (plus or minus a couple of days) closer to the former than the latter, as you might guess.

This episode, meanwhile, will keep us busy.

 > **O'BRIEN**: It's a natural sweetness from the sap of the jumja tree. It's full of vitamin C.

If you ranked every major *Star Trek* character based on the likelihood that they'd talk about healthy eating, where would you place Mr. O'Brien?  I feel like I'd go with somewhere around the third quartile, more likely to push (pardon the stereotype) corned beef than essential vitamins.  And yet...

 > **O'BRIEN**: Oh, Neela told me.

We met Neela in [*Duet*]({% post_url 2026-07-02-duet %}) last week.

 > **KEIKO**: Just keeping you on your toes, O'Brien.
 >
 > **O'BRIEN**: Oh, very funny.

It does seem pretty laughable that she'd remotely worry about somebody else finding the bigot alluring.  (No offense to Colm Meaney, who people presumably find attractive.)

 > **WINN**: Please, continue.

You'll no doubt recognize Archbishop Karen as the late [Louise Fletcher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louise_Fletcher), who most famously played Nurse Ratched in **One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest** opposite Jack Nicholson.  Even I recognized her at the time, and I've never watched the film.  But she also popped up on all sorts of (mostly odd, it seems) productions.

We'll see her...some might say "uncomfortably often."

 > **WINN**: Excuse me. By entities, do you not mean the Prophets?

This exchange bothers me in exactly the ways that lend itself to these posts, so let's get into it.

This conversation *should not exist* by any reasonable understanding of how their universe works.  Winn, we'll find out, believes in more isolationist policies---remember that in [*Emissary*]({% post_url 2026-02-26-emissary-1 %}) (I didn't have need to quote her), Sisko needed to sway Opaka's opinion in order to get the Bajoran Provisional Government to hold together long enough to accept the Federation's help and not collapse into civil war---meaning that she **probably** wouldn't take the time to learn English fluently over the seven-or-so months (the dialogue will later confirm seven months) with no expectation of a persistent Federation presence.  Let's assume, then, that she uses one of the translation devices that they float in front of us when the plot demands it but then forget about when they want to insert a pun into the script.

In that case, we definitely shouldn't have this conversation, because any reasonable translation system would translate *wormhole critters* and related terms in English to *Prophets* in Bajoran language(s), because they mean exactly that.  Like, not religious (we'll get to that next), but if you needed to translate from a Slavic language to German, you would translate the various derivations of *němьcь* as (the equivalent of) "Germany," not "country where they speak nonsense,"[^zxftLh] because you know the intent.

[^zxftLh]:  The root means "speechless," referring to the apparent relative difficulties of understanding German centuries ago.  The Baltic root *vākyā*, which also has derivations used as the name for Germany, refers to people shouting unintelligibly.

The Catholic Church already had almost exactly this problem in **our** history.  I gather that [Matteo Ricci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matteo_Ricci) arrived in China as a missionary.  He got to work by translating important works from Chinese to Latin, and then used what he gleaned from Chinese culture to then "localize" Christianity for the Chinese, such as using Confucius to shade the teachings of Jesus, identifying his God with the Highest Deity of Chinese theology, and integrating ancestor worship into the rituals.  That makes some sense, right?  It parallels how Christians appropriated holidays as they spread throughout Europe and the Americas, fitting the religion to the audience.  The Pope (Alexander VII) pretty much said *whatever gets them to tithe, my man* (paraphrasing), and this all sounded exciting enough that missionaries from other orders started checking out China as a side trip from the Philippines.

And unlike the Pope, they *lost their expletives-redacted minds*, insisting that you could only ever convert people to Christianity by eradicating any existing beliefs to start from a blank slate.  They saw the "accommodation" principle as barbaric.  And when they had a new Pope available (Clement XI), he overturned Alexander's accommodation as the [Chinese rites controversy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Rites_controversy) exploded all over the Vatican.  The policy became that the Church would only ever teach literal translations of what they teach everywhere else or not at all, and certainly would never allow any use of terms that might conflate the Big-G with some yucky pagan deity[^awdx6z].  And on hearing that, the Emperor told the missionaries exactly where they might stick their blank slates, offering the choice of either following Ricci's policies or losing their permission to stay in China.  This lasted until *1939*, when Pius XII---maybe an echo of his absurdly weak opposition to genocide that failed to even mention the targets---told China that they could have some Ricci-style concessions, but only in the more secular aspects of the religion.

[^awdx6z]:  They would allow *no* pagan influence, I mean, provided that you politely ignore the appropriated festivals and the likely borrowings from Norse, Iranian, and likely Indian mythology to better fit Medieval visions of Christianity...

Whew.  In other words, the Catholics flopped so badly and so famously in China over (primarily) terminology that nobody in our world would *dare* half-ass the translations of terms of religious significance unless they specifically intended to offend.

Now, we might write off this likely translation issue as possible inexperience with the language or a one-off writing error, but we have seen this before, and real-time viewers would soon.  Remember that, in [*Parallels*]({% post_url 2025-08-07-parallels %})---airing shortly before *Sanctuary* from next season, which we'll talk about in September or so---Troi translated *For He's a Jolly Good Fellow* into Klingon, complaining that she couldn't find a Klingon word for "jolly."  In that post, I pointed out the concerns raised by official translations not having adjectives that match emotional states that we can see those people express, and how that has worked in our world, speculated that such crude and biased translations might account for the "flattened" portrayals that we see of many cultures in this franchise, and suggested that I might return to the idea soon.

Welcome to "soon."

We need to speculate a bit, here, but let's take these two examples as presented and assign blame to the translator---the Federation doesn't recognize a way to communicate happiness with Klingons and can't or refuses to present Bajoran ideas using appropriate Bajoran terminology---then what does it mean for international reactions?  When, in *Emissary*, O'Brien mouthed off about working with "Bajoran women," does his bigotry come from only hearing them in translation that makes them sound obnoxious in some way?  Have relations with the Klingons had such tension because the translation only uses the most belligerent subset of the languages?  When our Ferengi characters discuss everything in terms of profit, does this reflect how they speak in their own language, or does the technology translate emotions[^0TpTMk] into economic terms?  Given that we haven't really seen a Vulcan actually *do* anything unemotionally, does the translation remove all markers of emotion from their speech to fit the stereotype?

[^0TpTMk]:  It strikes me as funny that writing the episodes probably works exactly like this.  You could imagine writing an entire story/script where your first draft has everybody speak in your voice.  Then you take a second pass and adjust the diction and metaphors used in each character's speech to reflect some cultural fixation.  Add the common "hack" pass where you add rhetorical tics specific to each character, and everybody sounds unique, more or less.

As mentioned in that *Parallels* post, we have multiple famous examples of adjusting translations for political gain.  And now we have gigantic multinational corporations managing most of our translations for us with software full of biases, so it doesn't seem out of the question[^lIkNu0] whether all this has an impact and how.  And as I recall, this weird tension will continue through the series, with each culture muttering under their breath to correct what someone in the other group has said about the Prophets or wormhole.

[^lIkNu0]:  As a complete aside, I can't guarantee that it'll ever come to anything, but for an unreleased Free Culture universe that bears a not-incidental resemblance to *Star Trek*, I actually have/had a fairly extensive outline for a series of stories that riffs on this idea of how their translation system works, why, and what it affects.  Those of you who read the newsletter got a small teaser back in February following up on the comment that I made in the post for [*These Are the Voyages...*]({% post_url 2026-01-29-these-are-voyages %}) about how little variation the franchise has between concepts.

Actually, on that note, let's even take this a step further:  Who names cultures in this universe?  I can't think of a single other case where a group has a non-negligible impact on the show, but everybody describes them rather than assigning a name to them, especially when they *have a name*, such as the Prophets.  It especially seems bizarre, when the discovery confirmed Bajoran beliefs as factual accounts of alien interference.  In our world, probably peaking around when this aired, New Age types busily reimagined the [Anunnaki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anunnaki) as a literal alien culture come to Earth to mess with us thousands of years ago.  If *they* can learn and use the Sumerian name, then surely the Federation folks can use "Prophet" as a proper name.

Hey, I did warn you that this little exchange plays right into these posts...

 > **KEIKO**: I respect that the Bajoran people believe that it does.

I mean...the tone suggests that she doesn't.

And let's follow up on that point that the discovery of the Prophets turns Bajoran mythology into history.  When Kirk-and-company met Apollo during [*Who Mourns for Adonais?*]({% post_url 2020-08-20-mourn %}), Quetzalcoatl in [*How Sharper Than a Serpent's Tooth*]({% post_url 2022-02-03-serpent %}), a civilization of demons in [*The Magicks of Megas-Tu*]({% post_url 2021-10-07-megas-tu %}), or maybe the Abrahamic God Herself in [**The Final Frontier**]({% post_url 2022-04-14-tff %}), whether they saw the entity in question as an ally or adversary, they never tried to hedge around the terminology.  We didn't get so much as a log entry mentioning "the entity who the Ancient Greeks may have worshiped as Apollo" or the like.  If we want to compare to extraterrestrial deities, Picard and his team had no problems using the name Andra in [*Devil's Due*]({% post_url 2024-02-01-devil-s-due %}) or (in the future of this episode) Kahless in [*Rightful Heir*]({% post_url 2025-04-24-rightful-heir %}), not to mention all the other times that Picard has talked about the truth in myths and legends.

Again, then, why do they have so much trouble in this case?

The only close analogy that I can think of in our world---again, other than deliberate disrespect---comes from times when the troubling term has a distinct religious meaning in their culture.  For example, I have seen Jewish and Muslim folks get uncomfortable when people talk about **Indiana Jones and the Last Crusade**, because referencing the Arthurian [Holy Grail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Grail) legend made them feel like they needed to acknowledge Jesus's blood as inherently holy.  Pagan mythologies, no problem, but start talking about that Jesus guy as a divine figure, and that becomes a problem.  And while I haven't seen this sort of behavior in any other context, it also doesn't make any sense, since we haven't heard about the Federation's prophets.

 > **O'BRIEN**: Sorry I couldn't get here sooner. Another fusion reactor went down. How's it going?

Again, I point out that everything works fine when O'Brien goes somewhere else.

 > **O'BRIEN**: Done? Did you remember to re-initialize the isolinear coprocessor?

Funny (as in seemingly racist or misogynist, not enjoyable in any way) how O'Brien made a big production of her brilliance at the top of the episode, but now questions whether she really did *all* the steps.

 > **NEELA**: Maybe you left it in Ops.
 >
 > **O'BRIEN**: No, I don't misplace my tools.

I seem to recall Keiko telling us during [*In Theory*]({% post_url 2024-04-25-in-theory %}) that her then-new husband wouldn't pick up his dirty socks, so why would we believe that he keeps track of his tools?

 > **KEIKO**: I'm not going to let a Bajoran spiritual leader dictate what can or can't be taught in my classroom.

Most of the time, I like Keiko.  But seriously, nobody claims the right to *dictate* what she teaches, and she does seem to need some oversight when (a) she can't name the creatures living in a location visible from any window who have a local name, and (b) lectures her students about "apparently self-sustaining" particles as if she came in without a lesson plan and decided to riff.

In fact, we saw a lot of similar behavior in [*A Man Alone*]({% post_url 2026-03-19-man-alone %}) when introducing the idea of the school.  There, she gave the strong impression that she didn't want Federation children mixing with the locals, and almost outright suggested that she only believed in schools as a way to separate children from the broader population.  She also admitted then that she had no credentials, and would need to build a curriculum that accommodated different cultures and philosophies.

In all these months, it doesn't sound like she bothered to deal with any of those concerns, and now finds doing so offensive.

Hey, speaking of separating children from the general population, where do they stash Molly?  Both parents seem to not trust rank-and-file Bajorans, and the entire school subplot comes from the lack of child-exclusive spaces on the station, so they probably don't have a day-care center.  Somebody probably would've mentioned it by now if she plays Dabo or has an apprenticeship with Odo.

 > **KEIKO**: I'm not teaching any philosophy. What I'm trying to teach is pure science.

Look, I don't believe in teaching religion in school; no, not even really in schools run by religious orders, because schools don't have the structure for that sort of instruction.  However, when you have a school on another culture's turf, and their religion *literally happened, with proof in front of your eyes*, you stopped teaching "pure science," and started teaching erasure of the local culture.

We don't really have this sort of situation on Earth, as far as I know.  We've found corroborating contemporary secular documentation of a handful of Abrahamic legends, and some of those stories include key details about locations, suggesting that some related event happened at those places and times.  Scientists have proven a smattering of supernatural-sounding incidents as barely plausible, often noting that this largely and appropriately kicks the story back to faith.  But we haven't unearthed any ancient sites with the legendary events in progress or an ancient deity still around to tell us "yeah, I totally did all that ill-advised stuff."  If we did, though, it would seem *really weird* and not at all scientific to refuse to acknowledge the cultural connections associated with the event/site on the basis that you don't subscribe to the relevant religion.

We do, however, have an over-abundance of history of the dominant cultures working to erase the native culture in a colony, including sending their kids to schools where they may only work in English and may not reference their own traditions.

 > **WINN**: But you are the Emissary. Don't you know the cherished place you have earned in the Bajoran spiritual life?
 >
 > **SISKO**: I'm not sure I'm comfortable in that role.

This probably doesn't have anything to do with our goals, but it seems strange that this hasn't come up before now.  From the Bajoran perspective, Sisko got pulled aside by gods and got permission for everybody to visit a (previously) mythical other-space.  And the rabble shows up to see her but not him?

 > **O'BRIEN**: With that interlock, someone could access every critical system on the station.

Maybe they should do something to secure that sort of thing, so that losing one doesn't put the entire station at risk...

 > **O'BRIEN**: Not for sale, huh? How would you like a jumja stick...
 >
 > ...
 >
 > **ODO**: Why wouldn't you want to sell to them?

The fact that O'Brien jumped to physical violence seems like a pretty good reason to not serve him.  And maybe cut back on the sugary treats for breakfast, no matter how hangry you get.

 > **KEIKO**: And when we get to theories of evolution or creation of the universe, what then?

This sounds like an escalation, but for the viewers at home at the time, Young Earth Creationists trying to interfere with school curricula on exactly these two topics had hit the mainstream United States[^eqvrEB] fairly recently at the time.  Specifically, in 1987, the Supreme Court ruled against the Constitutionality of requiring teaching Creationism in [*Edwards v. Aguillard*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwards_v._Aguillard), for example, which prompted rebranding the ideas to Intelligent Design to try again.

[^eqvrEB]:  Again, I mean.  The "Scopes Monkey Trial" and similar litigation had happened in the mid-1920s, as most Americans probably learned at some point.

In other words, this line serves the same purpose in this episode that Kirk's hammy speech near the end of [*The Omega Glory*]({% post_url 2021-01-14-omega %}) did, asking the right-wing viewers to ask how their goals differ from the antagonists'.  Then, Kirk explained to the aliens and us that the principles in the United States Declaration of Independence "must apply to everyone, or they mean nothing! Do you understand?"  Here, Keiko points out that only Nurse Ratched would fear teaching evolution in a science class.

I wonder if they meant us to contrast O'Brien's adolescent jealousy of somebody touching his stuff---which he presumably doesn't actually own, since he works for Starfleet---with Keiko's mock-jealousy at the top of the episode...

Also, they *directly affected Bajoran history*.  They sent the orbs.  They gave that guy a list of future events, the story of which Sisko and Dax used to find the wormhole.  Why does everybody act like the Bajorans jumped to conclusions by identifying the wormhole-inhabitants with their mythical Prophets? Anyway, but you'll still hear an argument parallel to this one around Black people in the Americas, descendants of the chattel slavery system.  Rather than talking about the validity of Christian mythology, though, it revolves around the fact that the slaveholders converted them.  It gives some an unpleasant feeling to take on the oppressors' religion, and so they have shifted to Islam, West African religions, and so forth, but others talk about how religion got their ancestors through their lives, for longer than the Bajorans lived under the Cardassians.

We're all very good conjuring up enough fear to justify whatever we want to do. Today, I am only a Vedek. If the Prophets will it, someday I may be Kai, and I can be a better friend to you then.

It seems like a significant management failure that they have this pervasive segregation, and nobody in charge has even noticed it, let alone taken any steps to integrate the crews better...

Well, that sounds like damning with faint praise if ever I've heard it, because we've seen O'Brien knock every non-Irish culture.

Oh, sure, when I said it week after week about **The Next Generation**...

If you'd stopped by and told me that just one year later they'd be gone, I'd be wearing a uniform, up here in charge of protecting some wormhole...

It took them long enough to use the term, even if he should've used the definite article instead of personalizing it.  For reference, by the way, while astronomers officially refer to it as the [Helix Nebula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helix_Nebula), people often refer to the same nebula as (among other names), the Eye of God.  All the constellations take their names from Greco-Roman mythology.  They can absolutely call the first known stable wormhole the Celestial Temple and not burst into flames...

## Conclusions

Oh, boy.  I have to work for the first time in a while, this week.  Fingers crossed that I remember how this works...

### The Good

It takes him most of the episode, but Sisko at least starts the process of acknowledging that a lot of Bajoran mythology provably happened.  And no, I couldn't find anything nicer to say than "advocates acknowledging facts."

### The Bad

The episode centers on the refusal, until the end by only one person (Sisko), to use terminology that derives from Bajoran religion.  It might imply biased translations throughout the franchise, but we don't have enough evidence to confirm that, so we need to limit it to the more confrontational disrespect of the religion.  They seem to find it painful to even acknowledge the existence of the faith.

It still looks like O'Brien causes the technology problems on the station.  He also seems to have no problem threatening civilians with violence.

O'Brien also reminds us that he doesn't see Bajorans and/or women as his equals, despite sometimes praising their work.  And he believes that nobody should touch "his" tools without explicit permission.

The school sounds like a mess, seemingly a place for Keiko to vent at kids.

We have also returned to examples of terrible security practices, with both tiny password maximum lengths and unsecured access keys used as plot points.

The Bajorans also drop a few more hints that people don't trust the Federation, describing them as godless, putting on airs, not nice, and (in effect) nihilistic.  The Bajoran crew also has little interest in mixing with their Starfleet counterparts and vice versa, with management not noticing.
