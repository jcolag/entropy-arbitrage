## 9:07 -- Thu 17 September 2026

{% embed https://knowablemagazine.org/docserver/fulltext/soft-archaeology-1600x600.mp4| In the lab, archaeologists showed how they could rapidly produce thick rope by threading cattail fibers through a replica of a Stone Age tool|false| %}

[<i class="fab fa-mastodon"></i>](https://mastodon.social/@jcolag/117286530005209734){: aria-label="Thursday morning's post on Mastodon with this article" }
[Rope, twine and thread: Invisible technologies of the Stone Age](https://knowablemagazine.org/content/article/society/2026/prehistory-lost-threads) from Knowable Magazine @KnowableMag@mstdn.science

> Yet while these experiments might have helped to solve one archaeological mystery, they also highlighted a much larger problem. The inhabitants of Hohle Fels must have manufactured significant quantities of rope, yet none of it has survived, and all that remains is the most durable component of this ancient production system — the ivory lochstab.

Hashtags:  #Archaeology

This reminds me of the job application that I filled out, probably twenty years ago now, where they asked me to discuss my "experience with technologies."  I gave them a paragraph on inclined planes and fire.  They never called back, weirdly enough...

Also, more Bronze Age, but I often think about how much the math on ancient structures change, if you assume that people could make rope and levers.  For example, every time I see one of those diagrams of how the Ancient Egyptians built pyramids---which always seems to involve building a pyramid five times the size to use as scaffolding---a single rope with a sack of rocks thrown over the existing structure does almost all the work with far fewer workers and equipment.

## Bonus

Because it accidentally became a tradition early on in the life of the blog, I drop any additional articles that didn't fit into the one-article-per-day week, but too weird or important to not mention, here.

{% embed https://wagingnonviolence.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/260207_DSF6023_Beihua_Guo-scaled.jpg|Volunteers mix harvested buffelgrass with dirt and sand|false| %}

<i class="fas fa-square"></i> [Making change from the dirt up](https://wagingnonviolence.org/2026/09/change-from-the-dirt-up-buffelgrass-adobe-housing/) from Waging Nonviolence

> In Tucson, regular people — especially the less privileged — [once built their own houses](https://borderlore.org/dirt-cheap-a-conversation-with-adobe-brick-maker-david-yubeta/) from dirt and plants and water, and could repair them when needed. Then, in the 1960s, the city razed most of downtown, uprooting hundreds of families to make way for a brutalist convention center meant to attract business dollars. Today, what remains of that old adobe neighborhood is one of the most prized parts of the city to live in and homes there command the highest prices. Building adobe structures citywide to serve the unhoused could be a way for the city to make amends for those 1960s-era [crimes](https://uapress.arizona.edu/book/la-calle) against people, community and aesthetics.


I don't know if building materials represent the actual bottleneck in housing people, but bringing the community together probably helps, regardless.
