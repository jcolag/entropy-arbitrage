Every year, when I bring up (or anybody else does) International Women's Day, some weirdo shows up to inquire about International Men's Day as if they've found some gotcha.  And somehow, they never see the punchline of the joke coming, that we celebrate it on November nineteenth, as in this Thursday.  Nobody really cares about it, though, because whereas International Women's Day arose from labor protests, the Suffragists, and cooperation between capitalists and socialists, International Men's Day arose from men asking "but what about the *men*" and imagining some stigma for the gender that holds the overwhelming majority of money and influence.

![A chair standing in an empty, dusty room](/blog/assets/5072523945_174fd7bc6e_o.png "The chair would never eat quiche, either")

For the past couple of years, attention on men has landed on the Male Loneliness Epidemic, with people scrambling to devise some way of getting my fellow men some companionship.

And...let's break this problem down into two huge components, maybe ignoring some smaller pieces to maintain focus, because if we don't understand the causes, we won't get viable solutions.

As a quick disclaimer, I should point out that, personally, I don't generally feel lonely, myself.  I don't mind socializing, and will have conversations with random strangers, but I can also happily spend---and have happily spent during lockdowns, for example---weeks at a clip isolated.  When I have concerns, I do have a list of people who I can check in with, but I more frequently check in with people to make sure that they haven't gotten too lonely.  In other words, not that I have a tendency to do this at all, but I want to make it clear that I didn't write this as a personal cry for help.

## Third Spaces or Not

Many other people have made this point, but contrary to the headlines in the news, we have something of a loneliness epidemic *regardless* of gender.  Those people often propose that this has happened due to the drying up---the acquisition and antisocial repurposing of---the {% wiki third place %}, social environments where you neither live nor work.

This does feel like an appealing explanation.  I certainly don't want to deflect attacks on capitalism, myself.  But I also do find myself looking at the idea skeptically, because...raise your hand if you don't live within walking distance to either a public park or a school, both classic outdoor third places[^UGFB78].  "Library deserts" certainly exist, but most people complaining don't live in one.

[^UGFB78]:  Before classes start and after sports practices end, you'll generally find people walking the grounds or exercising.

At least where I live, shopping malls still blight the landscape.  I feel like most of us can get to a place of worship faster than a McDonald's.  Towns half-jokingly fight over who has more bars, pubs, and similar alcohol-centric establishments per capita.  The (third) places exist, but people don't see them as such, because they don't go.

And I get it.  Parks in my area increasingly want to position themselves as friendly, and owners who measure out their maximum six feet of leash and let the dogs do whatever they please doesn't incline me to share the space with them.  I stopped shopping in malls decades ago and don't drink alcohol.  My attendance in places of worship almost exclusively involves supporting friends at major events.  But not enjoying the expected activities at your local third space differs dramatically from them not existing.

Well...except that it doesn't differ too much in practice, and the entire fields of marketing and public relations exist to sway what people enjoy.  We now live---also not an original thought---in a sea of desperately needy devices demanding our attention and trying to make their use into an {% wiki operant conditioning chamber %}, convincing us that we'll never get any dopamine again if we don't keep checking every feed.

Many households also have a bottomless media library that increasingly plays on nostalgia, while trading on promises that it'll get to the interesting stuff later.  We can get almost anything delivered to our front doors.  Rising prices might prevent many of us from "going out" in commercially oriented spaces.

Have we lost our third places, or did we stop going to them because marketing has convinced us that we'd rather stay home?  Or worse, have we romanticized the idea to create imagined third places that we have never actually visited, not going to the park because nobody there will introduce us to other people enjoying nature?

### But Also Maybe

As I mentioned, though, I don't really want to discount the idea, here, if only because the declining demand for someplace along the lines of a bowling alley means that we have fewer of them as they empty out and close down.  As I hinted at earlier, shopping malls have shifted from a standard set of stores with broad appeal to collections of niche stores that collectively have some appeal, to primarily to exclusively luxury brands, where nobody has shut the mall down.

Community centers barely exist at all.  Towns seem to increasingly focus on seasonal recreation that shuts down for most of the year.

## Zero Sum

I do want to talk about the gender-specific part of this problem, though.  Do you know what I actually hear whenever I hear some Extremely Serious Journalist (usually male) or young man wring their hands over male loneliness?

In my head, this concern translates directly to every toxic mantra of modern civilization.  At some point, I characterized it as the following.

> I see every relationship in my life as a zero-sum game that I must win at all costs.  Kill or be killed!  Hey, totally unrelated, does it seem weird to anybody else that nobody asks me over for dinner?

Society raises men to see women as targets, other men as rivals to destroy, and children as irrelevant.  Yeah, that certainly sounds like an exceedingly lonely life.  Only one demographic enjoys spending time around that, really:  Men who believe the same thing, but have enough power that they play the dominant role in the relationship.

"I don't think those things," I hear you men cry from the back of the room.  But keep in mind that we often launder those ideas through benign language.  Worries about a woman's modesty or purity, including theories about what best highlights women's beauty in general?  The idea that women [play hard to get](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/play_hard_to_get) or need a suitor to "wear them down"?  An assumption that women have power in the home?  All those (and plenty more) dress up the urge to control women as something hoped to *help* them, no different from colonial powers insisting that their ethnic cleansing and forced labor campaigns "civilized" the people getting kicked off their land.  If you worry about your status, think of yourself (probably wrongly) as the leader of your friend group, or plan out ways to defend your home in the event of an emergency, or many other possibilities, then you have also tricked yourself into seeing your male relationships as rivalries.  And no, you don't see children as irrelevant to your life, but you probably see a father taking care of his children as exceptional and a non-father taking care of somebody's children as suspicious.

Like they say, alpha males work a lot like alpha software:  Full of half-baked ideas, broken, and possibly even dangerous to have around.  In fact, as many of you probably already know, the term derives from the "alpha wolf," a role in wolf packs that only existed from ailing wolves in captivity; in the wild, wolves mostly travel in family units without much hierarchy.

I have talked about aspects of this before as hegemonic toxic masculinity.  We call it toxic because the ideas and behavior degrade your relationships[^FPDHxF].  And we refer to it as hegemonic because it imposes on and takes from you, but also demands that you *buy into the idea* and even consent to your low position.  If you don't **believe** that men need to exert authority over other genders and struggle with each other to determine a strict hierarchy, then you get worse treatment.

[^FPDHxF]:  It also hurts *you*.  Think of "boys/men don't cry" or similar bans on emotion as equivalent to "boys/men don't defecate."  It'll come out eventually, probably worse than if you dealt with it when you had the need, and holding it back will only do you possibly significant internal damage.

If you want to find a cause for the male loneliness epidemic, distinct from the female loneliness epidemic and the isolation of children that probably doesn't depend on gender at all, then you want to start here.

## Bad Solutions

You can see why I mentioned that, without an understanding of the underlying reasons, we won't get viable solutions.

When we imagine the problem as a result of some imaginary bias against men, then we get solutions that try to further elevate men socially, making the problem worse.  If we see this as something analogous to a market failure, then we end up with deranged theories[^0Ys2ks] about the government controlling women to "redistribute" them.

[^0Ys2ks]:  I won't bother to track down a link, but an economist probably more than a decade ago actually and seriously suggested an effort to redistribute the sexual availability of women to appease incels.  I actually had an incident on Quora where I pointed out the creepiness and futility of this idea, and Mister Economist himself tried to defend himself to me.  I tried to compare what he proposed to "redistributing" copyright on economics papers.  He promptly and diligently missed the point and explained that he totally supported the rights of copyright holders to dispose of their rights however they pleased, but wouldn't approve of forcing them.  {% emoji facepalm %}  Funny how and when consent matters...

I don't want to suggest that my hypotheses tell the entire story, because other potential reasons could exist and interact with the two that I called out.  However, we can rule out certain now-traditional explanations.

## Better

When we do look at the underlying problems, we can at least start trying to solve them.  To my chagrin, though, my advice will sound a bit simplistic.

Regardless of gender, start taking advantage of your local third places.  Maybe we have fewer such places, or maybe not.  Some exist, though, and if you don't frequent what remains, then you'll probably lose them as they close from disuse.  More importantly, use them to meet up with people.

Meanwhile, fight that urge for individualism.  Find activities that require a group and sell your group on them.

And for the men, take better care of your relationships.  Think about the conditioning that we all live with, and fight it when you start to see it impacting your relationships.

We have the most simplistic option, too:  Reach out to the people who you've forgotten over the years.  It won't always pan out, but knowing that other people feel lonely does give you an excuse to reconnect with people...
