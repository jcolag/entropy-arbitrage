## Operation Outbreak

The comic's foreword has this to say for itself.

> The following story is a work of fiction, but real public health threats and diseases are presented. CDC hopes that this story raises youth awareness of infectious diseases and the public health risks they pose. In addition, CDC hopes that this story helps to encourage youth interest in the many different career paths available in public health at the local, state and federal levels. Lastly, CDC hopes that this story promotes youth education and interest in science and public health. We at CDC hope that readers are inspired to become the next generation of real life disease detectives and public health superheroes. This graphic novel is the result of a partnership between the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), 4-H, and the U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA). 4-H is the nation’s largest youth development program. 4-H is a public-private partnership with federal, state, local and non-profit support.
>
> Youth and their families access 4-H programs at a community or county level through the cooperative extension division of their land-grant university. 4-H offers a youth development experience for all youth ages 5–19. For more information, please see <https://nifa/usda/gov/program/4-h-positive-youth-development>.

For context, remember that this came out in 2018.  In the United States, we did have the first Trump administration making a mess, but pre-COVID-19, that hadn't stretched to the attempts to undermine and discredit agencies including the CDC.

## What Works Well?

While I realize that the writer(s) based their plot on prior incidents, I do have to admit that it *feels* prescient for a comic released in 2018 to worry about a zoonotic upper respiratory virus threatening to become a pandemic, considering how most of us spent (at least) the end of 2019 into 2020.

If you wanted to learn about the CDC without any knowledge of the organization, you could probably do far worse than this.  This actually reminds me a lot of how, in the 1980s, companies and non-profit organizations would occasionally license popular characters to produce a comic book where the characters learn about whatever the sponsor wanted to pitch to kids.

## What Works...Less Well?

{% include lesswell.md %}

I don't like bringing this sort of thing up, for a variety of reasons---see the disclaimers in these posts for details---but a lot of the comic feels...shoddy, I guess.  We have faces that often look absolutely terrifying.  Multiple long scenes exist for exposition, not even of the story, but for parts of the CDC website.  And we don't even really get a resolution to the story, only an assertion that some resolution occurred.

For one minor example, the book goes *so far* out of its way to avoid admitting that the characters live in Georgia that it draws attention to the effort.  The kids live in day-trip distance to the Roybal campus[^cfe0at] (identified by the sign as they pull up) in Atlanta.  And they live in Thomas County, a name seemingly only used in {% wiki Thomas_County,_Georgia|Georgia %}, {% wiki Thomas_County,_Kansas|Kansas %}, and {% wiki Thomas_County,_Nebraska|Nebraska %}.  However, everybody refers to "the state," as if nobody will guess where the CDC has its headquarters.

[^cfe0at]:  You might recall the spot from the news last summer, when an anti-vaccine creep started shooting at the buildings from the pharmacy across the street, killing a police officer as law enforcement arrived.

I'd also point to the "appetizer" plot, where the school's 4-H Club plays a game where each of them---transposed to some stock fictional genre---solves a public health crisis.  It feels like a silly game for teenagers to play, but could work as a way for the creators to stretch beyond "normal people standing around in a room."  However, we get the following stories.

- Germ Slayer, the superhero, has a city dying of a mysterious illness on his hands, and uses his phenomenal powers to..."deliver the cure from the CDC."  I have no idea how the superhero angle fits in, if the CDC does all the slaying.
- A nameless medieval archer teaming with the two sword-wielders and (maybe) thief to kill goblins...as a hasty metaphor for the human immune system, though.
- A nameless space station peon who fails to notice the alien virus turning the rest of the staff into monsters...but don't worry about it, because our protagonist got a vaccine, no word on how it stops the violent attacks.  Not *we have a vaccine* and give it to everybody, but *I got a vaccine*, so the rest of the crew can die.
- The Disease Detective, who traces a foodborne outbreak to salmonella.

Now, on one hand, this probably reflects reality.  If I snagged four teenagers off the street and (after explaining the experiment to the cops) asked them to humor me for a similar exercise, I probably wouldn't get better results.  But when you *script* the entire sequence and someone needs to draw it, surely someone had to have noticed that one doesn't have a public health crisis at all, one fails to solve the crisis, and one solves their problem as a glorified bike courier.  Only one of the four actually makes good on the promise, and even the Disease Detective already knows that she has a foodborne illness, which makes for a pretty bland fantasy sequence.

## Opportunities

If you want more work like this, then I can only imagine that you'd need to back candidates for President of the United States who'll support educational initiatives from the CDC, 4-H, and USDA.

## What's Adaptable?

We have the aforementioned Germ Slayer and Disease Detective.  The latter fingers Krankheit's (est. 1918) as the source of the salmonella poisoning, the name translating to sickness or disease in German, so *probably* not a real family restaurant.

## Six Years Hence:  Nothing to Hide

The game's developer, Nicky Case, had already abandoned the project long before we got to it, so it shouldn't surprise anybody that nothing has happened, here.  Case has continued on with other work, increasingly explanatory essays.
