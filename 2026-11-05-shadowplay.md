## Shadowplay

For our usual context, this episode lands between [*Masks*]({% post_url 2025-09-18-masks %}) and [*Eye of the Beholder*]({% post_url 2025-09-25-eye-beholder %}).  Over at the competition, it aired between *Infection* and *The Parliament of Dreams*.

> **DAX**: Odo, I didn't tell you that story to get Seelee in trouble.

Gossip.  They call that gossip, not a story.  After some quick research, I can only find one constructive reason for gossip:  Helping vulnerable members of the group navigate an organization.  Everything else involves vigilante persecution of vulnerable people to "build community," erosion of trust in targets, and establishing dominance over others...and notably, Odo doesn't need help navigating his own department.

We can safely assume that Dax wanted to establish dominance.  But since Odo has his weird "law enforcement, but not in an official capacity, except that everybody official recognizes him in the role" gig, his investigations presumably rely a *lot* on gossip, so I can't imagine a version of this scene not ending with a reprimand, and it defies belief that the person allegedly so good at social interaction due to centuries enjoying the company of shady characters didn't have that on her mind.

All that to say, she *definitely* wanted to get Seelee in trouble.  And she probably succeeded, considering that we'll never meet her.  But Dax didn't want anybody attributing that trouble to her, because gossips never do...

Oh, and loosely related, this goes back to my assumption that they wrote a lot of Dax assuming that they'd use Kamala from [*The Perfect Mate*]({% post_url 2024-10-03-perfect-mate %}).  A character who biologically imprints on a mate and reshapes her entire personality to suit that person, especially if something severed that connection, would have entirely valid reasons to find this an interesting story with multiple twists instead of bland gossip.

> **DAX**: The point is that sometimes we don't see true love, even when it's staring us right in the face.

I can't even *imagine* how you get that out of the story.  Nothing stared anybody in the face, because the two protagonists made a snap judgment and moved on.  At best, the moral sounds more like "heteronormative gender roles stink," because in a rational world filled with healthy relationships, nobody waits for someone to ask them out, if they want to go out.

> **DAX**: I've noticed she's been making a lot of visits to the Security Office lately.

How does this show have two separate women dropping by the same office to sexually harass Odo?  Surely, *someone* in the Federation explains that someone working at a station, in a job requiring some politeness, can't reasonably consent to spending time with you that way.  Or maybe this happens everywhere, and they only mention it when it happens to Odo.  The franchise seems to hint that breaking a limb doesn't cause more than mild inconvenience, so Bashir could have plenty of coy visitors.  Or they could make up a fake trade deal to present to Sisko every other Friday morning.  I assume that, if someone says something racist or sexist, O'Brien instinctively knows to show up, kind of like visiting him in an office...

Also, military officers monitoring the comings and goings of civilians sounds *lovely*.

> **ODO**: Because women don't react to me in that manner.

Again, we literally saw [*The Forsaken*]({% post_url 2026-06-18-forsaken %}) near the end of last season, Odo.  A woman did exactly that thing.

> **DAX**: That's not what I meant. I'm talking about an intimate friendship.

It would have made the back half of the series so much smoother, had Dax taken a slightly different, and arguably more in-character, angle on Odo mentioning Kira...

> **DAX**: I'm sorry, but after seven lifetimes the impersonal questions aren't much fun anymore.

I suppose that this answers my question from earlier, that Dax actually does see colleagues as present for her amusement.  And I probably should've seen that coming, given that she said as much about an entire culture in [*Rules of Acquisition*]({% post_url 2026-09-03-rules-acquisition %}).  But more to the point, who sent her out to have fun?

> **KIRA**: Don't like you? You collaborated with the Cardassians, you cheat your customers, and you're a danger to this station. I don't just not like you, I despise you.

Only indirectly related to the post, because we have raised the other half of this issue for other episodes, but other than ethnicity, what makes Quark a collaborator, but not Odo?  Yes, Quark opened or continued to run a business primarily or exclusively serving the staff of a labor camp, but Odo enforced the occupying government's oppressive laws, lending it the illusion of fairness by refusing---as we saw in [*Necessary Evil*]({% post_url 2026-09-10-necessary-evil %})---to railroad convenient scapegoats into executions.

> **SISKO**: You're almost as old as he is. When I was your age, I had a job, and it was a terrific experience.

Good for him, but you can't guarantee that.  I also had a great job through most of high school that became a second/surrogate family for me---the healthy kind, not the corporate kind where they actually mean something more like a crime family, where everybody owes unconditional allegiance to the boss, but they murder you if you displease them; I actually got emotional support and other help---and had "a terrific experience," there.  But I also picked up smaller jobs on occasion, and wouldn't call them[^xDbezL] good experiences at all.

[^xDbezL]:  I say this at the risk of violating my (personal) rule of never providing any information on past employers, but at roughly forty years ago, as a minor, and possibly paid under the table, I doubt that anybody can use this to identify a specific person to blame.  And I don't even remember the person or company, so I couldn't even confirm it if you managed to guess right.

> **SISKO**: Quark's? Phew! I was thinking more along the lines of you helping out Chief O'Brien.

This, in other words, follows on the heels of [*Paradise*]({% post_url 2026-10-29-paradise %}), which started with Sisko asking O'Brien if he'd take on the mentoring role.

Also, he used Nog as a positive example.  How did he *think* that would pan out, if not getting a job waiting tables?

> **SISKO**: You do like Chief O'Brien, don't you?

Look, I realize that I haven't much cared for O'Brien at least since they gave him his Old World white patriarchal "personality" in [*Data's Day*]({% post_url 2024-01-18-data-s-day %}).  And I further acknowledge that children and teenagers have a distinct tendency at times to find adults to dislike without a real reason.  However, when a young person---honestly, when any vulnerable person---hesitates to let a specific adult have authority over them like Jake does here, I feel like that warrants more interest than *challenging* their worries.  And that feels like it should especially hold true in a case like this, where Jake spends hours every day with *Mrs.* O'Brien, which would give him pretty good insight into the effects that long-term exposure to Miles has on people...

> **SISKO**: This is the perfect opportunity to learn. Besides, it'll look great on your application to Starfleet Academy.

We have a reminder that they mostly don't even see careers outside Starfleet.

> **COLYUS**: Have you committed one?

If our judge looks familiar to you, then you might recognize him as {% wiki Kenneth Mars %}, probably best known during his life from appearances in Mel Brooks films, but might have the widest range of acting credits that I've seen, from perfectly ordinary sitcom characters to that weird TV film version of Broadway's **It's a Bird...It's a Plane...It's Superman** that dropped a bunch of songs and characters to fit the run-time.

> **RURIGAN**: So, these are the strangers I heard about. Shouldn't they be in a cell?

And if Rurigan looks familiar, then you might recognize him as {% wiki Kenneth Tobey %}, who you'd probably know from 1951's **The Thing from Another World**, based on the same short story as the John Carpenter film.

> **RURIGAN**: Taya? Don't be afraid, Taya. This is a friend of Protector Colyus. He just wants you to answer a few questions.
>
> **TAYA**: Do I have to?

She retired shortly after this, but you might recognize Taya as {% wiki Noley Thornton %}, who we last saw in [*Imaginary Friend*]({% post_url 2024-10-10-imaginary-friend %}) as the not-imaginary one.

Also, it seems like a missed opportunity not to have some conceptual connection between Jake's concerns about working with O'Brien and Taya not wanting to talk to Odo.

> **BASHIR**: I look forward to it. Garak has been lecturing me on surveillance techniques. This will give me a chance to try some of them out.

That makes two officers, in this episode alone, all-in on spying on random civilians.

> **BAREIL**: That's exactly what I wanted to avoid.

We last saw Bareil in [*The Circle*]({% post_url 2026-07-30-circle %}).

> **JAKE**: Nothing. It's just that...why does everyone assume that I'm going to go to the Academy?
>
> **O'BRIEN**: You want to go, don't you?

Sisko pressures him to spend time with O'Brien, then O'Brien awkwardly pressures him to join Starfleet...

I don't mean that nearly as flippantly as it sounds.  Twice, now, Jake has implied that he has a problem, to two separate adults.  Each time, the adult in question has responded by implying that he doesn't actually mean what he says and should retract that concern.

> **O'BRIEN**: I know how you feel. You know what my father wanted me to be? A musician. It's true. I was supposed to be a cello player. He made me practice every day. I got to be pretty good, and when I was seventeen, he sent a recorded audition to the Aldebaran Music Academy.
>
> ...
>
> **O'BRIEN**: I got in. It was the happiest day of his life. I didn't know how to tell him I didn't want to go, so two days before I was due to leave, I went into town and signed up for Starfleet.

Too bad, really, because a science fiction series about a reluctant cello player---**Star Trek:  The Dying Swan**, no doubt, using part of {% wiki Le cygne %} for the theme and everything---sounds like something that could've become a lot of fun.  But no, instead we got **Voyager** and **Enterprise**...

Also, though, do you notice what O'Brien's story doesn't contain?  He has no explanation of what drew him to Starfleet.  In *Paradise*, he made a big show about how he didn't understand or care about the technology.  He doesn't care about exploring or diplomacy.  Does anything motivate him?

> **RURIGAN**: I was happy living on Yadera Prime, that is, until the Dominion took over and changed our whole way of life. I realized one morning that I didn't really belong there anymore, so I came here, and I used a holo-generator to recreate all the things that I'd lost. I've lived here in my village for nearly thirty years. I've watched its people marry, have children, grow old. And sometimes I even forgot that they were holograms. But it's over. It's over. And I would appreciate it if you'd take me back to Yadera Prime.

We previously heard about the Dominion in *Rules of Acquisition* and [*Sanctuary*]({% post_url 2026-09-24-sanctuary %}), sounding like serious jerks then, too.

> **ODO**: Technically, I suppose you're right. Maybe by our definition, Taya's not real. Her memories are stored in a computer. Her body is made up of omicron particles. But who's to say that our definition of life is the only valid one. I'm sure if you asked her, she'd say she was real. She thinks, she feels.

As the science officer and a Federation citizen, Dax's disinterest in this conversation speaks volumes, doesn't it?  Between Data starting in [*The Measure of a Man*]({% post_url 2023-01-19-measure-man %}) and various hologram characters since [*The Big Goodbye*]({% post_url 2022-08-04-big-goodbye %}), the Federation definitely knows that at least some computer programs develop some level of independent consciousness, but has shown a rather distinct lack of interest in exploring the possibilities further or granting them civil liberties as a class, preferring instead to grant conditional privileges to the rare individuals who have support from powerful people.

In fact, if you'll pardon my jumping around the timeline and probably repeating myself from some other point in the blog, we know that this never gets better.  Near the end of **Voyager**, we learn that the Federation forces conscious holographic individuals to handle hard and dangerous labor, and the Doctor's life story of self-improvement inspires them to resist, but they don't show us the aftermath.  The *Enterprise*-D/-E will have a couple of more incidents where they threaten to exterminate some holograms or Data because they present an inconvenience, including [*Emergence*]({% post_url 2025-10-30-emergence %}) airing soon after this episode.  And then *Children of Mars*, the end of **Prodigy**, and the beginning of **Picard** show us that, in the wake of an alleged terrorist attack, the Federation immediately engages in ethnic cleansing as collective punishment, banning the entry of and "disassembling" (killing) all synthetic life inside its borders.  By the end of **Picard**, the Federation quietly repeals the ban, but I don't know if that makes up for the genocide.

Dax *could* raise any subset of this---other than the still-future bits in the previous paragraph---that the Federation knows of several distinct cases of conscious software, or that society seems torn on what to do about it.  But she pretty much zones out, instead, probably wondering how else she can crush Seelee without leaving a paper trail...

> **ODO**: Don't you see? She's real to you, and she's real to me too. They're all real. And you can't turn your back on them now.

Also, she has consciousness, along with everyone else in the village.  We watched them process the emotions of losing people and discovering that they don't have a solid physical form.  They make it sound like we should treat fictional characters like actual people, maybe even leave room at the dinner table in case they swing by.  It seems bizarre not to make that distinction, especially in a media franchise where cast members have had a lot of trouble getting work, at times, due to the inability of producers to see them as playing any other role; they'd all have this front-of-mind, in fact, because they had an entire cast over at **TNG** worried about the future of their careers after it'd end in a few months.

Or, to update the conversation to 2026, several companies provide access to AI chatbots tuned to simulate participating in a romantic relationship.  Does anybody have some obligation to maintain that service, on the basis that some customer might have come to view their characters as real?

> **SISKO**: It's your life, Jake. You have to choose your own way. There is only one thing I want from you. Find something you love, then do it the best you can.

Good for the commander, but this relaxed attitude almost makes the pressure seem worse.  Rather than some ideal career, this makes it sound like a default.  And why keep pushing him in that direction, if it didn't matter?

## Conclusions

We mostly spend our time on the novel culture and (primarily) Odo's reaction to it, but still...

### The Good

Despite the unrelenting pressure on children to join Starfleet, Sisko at least seems to support other outcomes.

### The Bad

We see a wide tolerance for malicious gossip, at least from Dax, as well as her treating people as story elements to entertain her.  And they also seem to support people (at least women) cornering Odo in his office to socialize with him.

Starfleet officers also seem shockingly excited to engage in surveillance on civilians.

We see a lot of pressure for children to join Starfleet.  They also seem shockingly quick to dismiss concerns that those children might raise.  Some families, however, prefer to pressure their children into music careers.  And while some parents accept alternate outcomes, that doesn't reduce the pressure.

Maybe most oddly, Dax seems to have no interest in the conversation of whether to treat conscious holographic characters like people, only concerned on whether she should turn the machine back on.
