<i class="fas fa-square"></i> [The ‘religious left’ may be rising – but Black Christian women have been there all along](https://theconversation.com/the-religious-left-may-be-rising-but-black-christian-women-have-been-there-all-along-296110) from The Conversation @TheConversationUS@newsie.social

> Women’s role was less public, [but no less vital](https://search.worldcat.org/title/45024509), including social and political organizing. Among the women Ross surveyed is Ella Baker, who organized the initial meeting for what would become the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee and often “used Christian scripture to admonish listeners to fight” for freedom.

I don't really have much to say, here, other than it ties to the "Black women have saved us" narrative that lasts for about ten minutes after every not-terrible election, and then society goes back to not caring about Black women...

<i class="fas fa-square"></i> [Is Nvidia interim CEO Colette Kress standing on the ‘glass cliff’ that sets women up to fail?](https://www.fastcompany.com/91641113/is-nvidia-interim-ceo-colette-kress-standing-on-the-glass-cliff-that-sets-women-up-to-fail) from Fast Company

> The phenomenon is referred to as, not punching a hole in a glass ceiling, but rather, standing on the edge of the glass cliff. That’s because, while the promotion itself might look like a win for women in business, the precarious position the new leader steps into often sets her up for failure.

Yeah, I have no doubt that Kress could run **a** company, but the industry has a habit of doing this as the company collapses, and then using that as evidence that you can't have women in charge.  Kress herself will do fine in her career---look at Marissa Mayer, for example---but she won't "trade up" to long-term leadership roles at large companies.
