## Linux

First, the [Linux kernel](https://lore.kernel.org/linux-media/CAHk-=wi4zC+Ze8e+p3tMv8TtG_80KzsZ1syL9anBtmEh5Z40vg@mail.gmail.com/), [systemd](https://github.com/systemd/systemd/blob/main/AGENTS.md), [Fedora](https://docs.fedoraproject.org/en-US/council/policy/ai-contribution-policy/), and [Debian](https://www.debian.org/vote/2026/vote_002) all decided that they need "contributions" from disengaged people who ask LLM agents to write some code for them; the *Grok jocks*[^4YnXAG], if you will.

[^4YnXAG]:  I mean that as analogous to {% wiki shock jock %}, though other derogatory interpretations probably wouldn't displease me too much...

For those not familiar with the structure of things, an operating system has a bunch of parts.

The arrows don't really matter so much, except for a "generally relies on" relationship, and because my older version of [Mermaid](https://mermaid.js.org/)---which has apparently become some AI nonsense, since?---doesn't support anything more appropriate than a flow-chart, here.  In any case, I only want to use it as broad illustration.

All Linux distributions use the Linux kernel, rather by definition, which interacts with your computer hardware.  Most also use the low-level [GNU tools](https://www.gnu.org/) and utility programs, hence the Free Software Foundation's obsession with getting people to call it "GNU/Linux."  Sitting on that base, you have a collection of services (increasingly managed by systemd), a package manager (APT, DNF, or similar), a display server (traditionally X11, increasingly Wayland), and a display *manager* to get you logged in; that collection generally gets chosen for each distribution.  Some distributions also choose a desktop environment (such as GNOME or KDE) and default applications.

### Impact

With that approximate architecture in mind, we can get to the troubling part of those recent announcements:  Every Linux distribution necessarily uses the Linux kernel, and most use systemd, both happily accepting LLM-generated code.  The overwhelming majority of Linux distributions start with Debian and Fedora, which similarly accept LLM-generated code with a veneer of asking people to do so "responsibly," whatever that means.

Why do I care about this?  I have a handful of reasons.

- The policy introduces reliance, in *core Free Software projects*, on proprietary software.
- Generated code has uncertain provenance, laundered through neural network training and the randomness in text-generation and presented as original, but with no guarantee.
- The policy makes the decision---on behalf of all users---that we want to "move fast and break things" as Mark Zuckerberg put it, accelerating code-writing while testing and documentation continue to lag.
- LLMs especially break maintainability, since fixing takes intelligence, whereas rewriting only takes the same machine translation techniques.
- Studies continue to show that LLM usage actually slows work down while making developers less capable.

In other words, we will likely see declining quality on the most critical part of computing, while undermining Software Freedom by introducing the risk that the LLM infringed copyright.  I freely admit that some projects can probably get away with some LLM usage, where users don't mind an occasional lapse in quality if it means faster releases, or where nobody expects to make derivative works, and so wouldn't necessarily care about code provenance.  But for an operating system, which you need to rely on for all your computer work, to say nothing of the literal life-and-limb dangers for an embedded device, that seems like a bad trade.

## Doze Linux

Then, we have the other big problem, which we can reduce to the single "word" *Omarchy*.

Over the past couple of months, Omarchy has transformed from a laughably shoddy vibe-coded customization of [Arch Linux](https://archlinux.org/) with a cheap 1990s hacker aesthetic---by someone now more known for lamenting the days when a person could visit London without seeing brown people than for creating the first significant web application framework that nobody cares about anymore, no less---to a project with a [weird fascist mission statement](https://stopomarchy.neocities.org/) and an incongruous magnet for money, raising millions of dollars from companies as they stop donating to more established (more *complicated*) projects.  From that page:

> [The Omarchy Doctrine](http://web.archive.org/web/20260911142433/https://omarchy.org/doctrine/) is a public, fascist creed: heritage as duty, hold the line, the will to power. It matches Roger Griffin's palingenetic populist ultranationalism and Robert Paxton's first stage of fascism: a creed, a leader, followers. And there's $18.5 million already pledged for the next stage.

Yep.  We finally have anti-woke---or what I like to propose that we call *Doze*---Linux.

### Why, Though

We have seen many attempts to explain this, from the fairly benign (the project lead has connections in Silicon Valley, and so can call people to solicit money as a publicity stunt) to the criminal (money laundering while cryptocurrency prices drop), but only one theory seems to fit the overall behavior, an attempt to [centralize authority in Free Software](https://tante.cc/2026/09/11/power-grab/) in the hands of someone who will happily make grants to other projects conditional on accepting fascist- and bigot-friendly policies.  From that post:

> DHH has used his connection to CEO’s of tech corporations and his position as influencer to collect up to now above 18 million dollars of funding for Omarchy. He uses that money not for himself (he has enough of that) but to fund certain pieces of Software he uses in his distribution. Like for example the window manager hyprland whose [main developer the Omarchy money now funds](https://omarchy.org/news/2026/08/omacom-foundation-to-be-exclusive-hyprland-sponsor/). What is “Vaxry” known for outside of a niche window manager? He runs a community based around LGBTQ discrimination and participates in that (see the short summary on Drew DeVault’s "[Weird Little Guys of FOSS](https://drewdevault.com/weird-guys/)" list). His bigotry is so consistent that he was banned from participating in the Freedesktop.org community where all the other developers of window managers and the free software desktop stack collaborate. And it was long before DHH funded him.

Expect to see a lot of offers of donations to projects **if** they can only delete the link to donate to Palestinians displaced by the war on their website, or tone down their DEI policies, or maybe add some telemetry to the product and weaken some encryption.

## Anti-Solidarity

And actually, I suppose that I lied, because we *do* have a concurrent concern in the broader Free Culture space:  Wikipedia hiring union-busting attorneys in reaction to the formation of [Wiki Workers United](https://wikiworkersunited.org/).  While they did ultimately issue an anonymous [grudging acceptance of the union](https://wikimediafoundation.org/news/2026/09/03/us-staff-unionization-vote/) after the vote that they forced, the fact that they fired good people and then fought the unionization of the remaining employees strikes a worrying tone for future relations.  And this has a non-zero chance of spreading to other foundations in the Free Culture space.

Specifically, the incident shows that even the Wikimedia Foundation---long committed to user-centric, consensus-based collaboration---sees a future in authoritarian decision-making, where worker solidarity worries them.  Again, I need to emphasize that this attempt backfired on them, the press coverage of their aggressive, seemingly irrational opposition to the union turning the vote for unionization into a landslide in favor.  But they have still set a precedent that, if people at the Free Software Foundation or wherever start to worry about supporting a project like Omarchy or using LLMs, Wikimedia has normalized the idea of trying to crush the dissent.

## The More Things Change

This adds to the existing problems, mind you.

We still need to contend with the success of the "Open Source" movement in turning the premise from ensuring that people (you and I) can always learn from and adapt software to our needs, to making sure that Big Business can harvest and exploit unpaid labor.  Or when companies "contribute" to the community, they invariably do so to either sabotage a competitor's business model or funnel customers to them, what business folks call "commoditizing your complements;" think of Google releasing web browsers and mobile operating systems that not-so-coincidentally coerce users into relying on their proprietary products and services, or Kubernetes to encourage developers to leave AWS for their cloud thing.  Even before that, we need to talk about the libertarian framing of "freedom," that they fight for the ability to *fork* projects, not to collaborate on them and so find the best solutions.

Or we could talk about the number of high-profile pioneers associated with the overall Free Culture movement who had strong connections to the {% wiki MIT Media Lab %} while Jeffrey Epstein (among other worrying people) lavished money on the group, leading to some wild defenses either of accepting the money or of Epstein himself.  And that says nothing about the [broader group of creeps](https://drewdevault.com/weird-guys/) who use their technical credentials to get away with predatory behavior and/or launder their far-right views into the mainstream.

## Retreat

I know for a fact that many of us have started the fight by looking for a new refuge.  Over the last couple of weeks, I have talked about starting to investigate [NetBSD](https://www.netbsd.org/), [ReactOS](https://reactos.org/), and [Guix](https://guix.gnu.org/)-with-the-[Hurd](https://www.gnu.org/software/hurd/advantages.html)-kernel as projects that definitely oppose LLM-generated contributions and *may* (I don't know, yet) refuse to indulge authoritarians and abusers.  Multiple people have suggested that [Haiku](https://www.haiku-os.org/) might also work.  Likewise, [Plan 9 from Bell Labs](https://p9f.org/) or [Inferno](https://www.vitanuova.com/inferno/) could suffice, too.

And while I certainly see the appeal, and even consider it necessary to have someplace safe to build, this won't solve any problems.  If we cede the ground to the intersection of AI and fascism, then they'll expand and eventually push their way into any project that requires outside support.  We'll need somewhere to take a stand, though.

Don't mistake this for the people pretending to take a stand on {% x %}, as if they'll magically overcome the biases and algorithms to change hearts and minds on the site.  Nobody will change the minds of people who outsource their thinking to linear algebra, and no "my followers there reliably pay me money" excuses.  I mean that we need to build something better, and we can't do that on compromised systems.

## Forks

If you actually read the Torvalds-tantrum above defending LLM-generated contributions to the Linux kernel, you'll notice that he makes a childish recommendation.

> ...if somebody has issues with that, they can do the open-source thing and fork it.

By "forking," he means creating a new version of the project based on a version that you can accept.  In some ways, it happens all the time.

I call this childish, because he of all people knows that Linux has grown large enough that it requires the millions of dollars worth of infrastructure that he makes sure gets donated to the project by major corporations.  It also needs many developers to keep up with problems.  You *can* create a fork of the source code and rename it "no-AI-ix" or whatever, but unless you have significant resources to burn on it---and systemd, and the work that Debian/Fedora do, and so forth---then your project doesn't differ substantially from "keep using the Linux kernel from 2025."

Some people will do that, because people who don't understand Free Software love to grab headlines by announcing forks of projects that probably won't go anywhere.  And I strongly recommend not taking them seriously until they've shown that they can survive for a while, create a community, and resolve problems, rather than jumping on board because you see a Codeberg repository and website with a fancy logo.

## Embrace the Politics

Since I invoked the Torvalds e-mail a couple of times, I should revisit something that I've pointed out a few times over the past couple of months:  He makes a point of saying---incorrectly, but whatever---that he doesn't see politics and social justice as having a part in Linux's future.

And thinking about that always strikes me as hilarious, because while it took me over a decade to treat Linux seriously, I remember those early days, when a young Torvalds announced that he had a barely functioning kernel that people might find interesting.  And unlike Torvalds, I remember him getting broadly criticized, because he had no sense of good software architecture, didn't product particularly good code, and didn't seem to have any vision beyond aping UNIX-like systems in a loosely interpreted way.

Why did Linux thrive when the contemporary systems became literal footnotes used to give context to that announcement post?  Unlike the other systems, Torvalds released this new kernel under the GPL, inviting whoever wanted to play with it to contribute or make it their own.  Contrast that with the clearest equivalent at the time, Andrew Tanenbaum's {% wiki MINIX %}, which has sufficiently well-designed code and complete feature set that many computer science courses still use it as an example of how to build a kernel for an operating system, but the then-proprietary license meant selling a busy Professor Tanenbaum on your idea.

In other words, Linux thrived because of progressive politics, or social justice if you prefer; by letting anybody contribute, it got the best ideas available to them, instead of the ideas that Torvalds himself found comfortable.  And if the Omarchy people have no problem with their fascist mission statement, then I see no reason for projects with less-troubling views of the world to make their views part of the conversation.  If Linux can prefer anti-labor technology over inclusion and Omarchy can make its brand about wanting people to see you as important, then they have normalized Free Culture/Software projects having DEI policies or otherwise urging solidarity.

Maybe we should start taking them up on their generous offer to center politics in this work.

## Rebuild

As you can probably guess by the fact that I've written tens of thousands of words so far on trying to [build a better definition of Free Culture]({% post_url 2025-09-21-define-free-culture-1 %}) (and Free Software, by association), I already think that the movement needs an overhaul.  In fact, this post partly branched off from those posts, as I noticed my "how should creators behave" post crossing the ten-thousand-word mark, so I'll need to break that up...

And even though I've given it a lot of thought, I don't think that I have complete answers on what the movement needs to look like overall, beyond my emerging ideas.  We need more people thinking about that, especially as we see the AI-boosters and the fascists openly exploit the problems in the existing movement to gain power.

## Organizing

The example of the Wikimedia Foundation shows that collective bargaining terrifies these people.  The *do the open-source thing and fork it* line underscores this, that authoritarian leaders, whether they lead Wikipedia, Linux, Omarchy, or anything else, want contributors to see themselves as "rugged individualists" who (coincidentally) have less power than the leaders.  They call this "meritocracy," because it lets them pretend that they got their position through their inherent superiority, rather than their connections to wealthy people.

At the moment, United States labor law appears to require a conventional, paid employment agreement to form a union, making it impossible to form a traditional labor union that can appeal to government agencies for support in forcing leadership to negotiate.  However, imagine volunteer contributors-of-conscience from across all Free Culture projects going on "strike" by redirecting their volunteer efforts to a pre-arranged group of smaller projects with strong ethical stances, to exert pressure on the overall system.

We don't have that now, not least because the movement really has deluded itself into believing in meritocracies and rugged individualism.  And many won't let that go.  But building a cross-project organization could show even those AI-worshiping, anti-DEI zealots what their chatbots and scabs accomplish without everybody else...*if* the work goes in to build that organization.

## Professionalizing

Likewise, software especially already feels overdue for becoming at least *mostly* a profession, the sort of career where you don't necessarily need a license to practice, but licensing comes with a guarantee of certain training and ethical standards.  And whether the industry does that as a whole, maybe Free Culture/Software should start considering that.

No, I can't even guess how that would look, but imagine a Debian-like scenario, where the project decides that developers can use AI, but must do so "responsibly."  Among professionals---engineers, architects, doctors, and so forth---you'll immediately get push-back on that, because they need a definition of responsibility that they can square with the research about the technology.  And that push benefits the entire team, because they have that definition and can know where the limits sit.

Now, imagine that for Free Culture, where a licensed individual understands how copyright and licenses work, why a leader might want to own the copyrights (because they intend to change the license in the future), how LLM contributions affect a project, and so forth, and have a duty (to keep their licenses) to maintain standards in those areas.  Would projects avoid such licensed individuals as "trouble," or would they embrace the idea as a way to show that people can trust them?

## New Blood

Part of the problem, I suspect, comes from the fact that Free Culture/Software doesn't have much outreach.  The people seen as leaders today---including some people specifically causing problems described in this post---positioned themselves as leaders more than *twenty years ago*.  We see new developers come in, but I suspect that they only stick to projects that welcome newcomers with disadvantaged backgrounds, because why would a new person want to volunteer their time anywhere else?

Maybe we need to start bringing people in.  I've mentioned before that only one major project that I can find, Wikipedia, openly positions their project as collaborative.  And even there, "the free encyclopedia that anyone can edit" doesn't really *invite* collaboration so much as acknowledge that they'll tolerate it, even though they have a more welcoming community than that.

Even [Software Freedom Day](https://digitalfreedoms.org/en/sfd)---mark your calendars for Saturday, I suppose---has events, but I don't think that anybody sees it as a huge public event showing the world what we can do in a world without proprietary software.  In fact, the organization itself now advises abandoning the "install party" model and shifting to only having fun with like-minded individuals, maybe pressure governments to recognize Free Software solutions.  And...OK, sure, but that makes us an aging demographic with few new ideas.

## Opening the Floor

Again, I apologize for writing this post as mostly setup and then a few half-baked ideas, but like I said, I wanted to get this discussion started *somewhere*, because I don't see it happening anywhere else.  Or rather, I see people looking for AI-free alternatives over here, and I see people looking to boycott Omarchy's supporters there, but I definitely don't see a *we need to fix the movement to make these people irrelevant and make it a more difficult target in the future* discussion anywhere.

Like Wikipedia's "anyone can edit" line, I always write posts with the expectation that people *may* reply if they want.  Here, though, I feel some urgency to actually seeing some of these problems solved.  As such, I make the [blog's Matrix room](https://matrix.to/#/#entropy-arbitrage:matrix.org) available as a place to workshop these or other ideas, and we can always branch off to other rooms, if it becomes more serious than brainstorming.  Comments on the post also work.  Or contacting me directly, we can hammer out ideas to propose in another forum, and branch out to bring more people in.

I think that we, as a community, *can* solve the problem, but we do need to see it all as a single problem, and need to stop thinking of it as a problem to solve by waiting out the attacks or walking away to a quieter space.
