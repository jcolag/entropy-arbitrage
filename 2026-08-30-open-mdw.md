## The Linux Foundation

Before we get going, I should note what it means for the Linux Foundation to do something.

The Foundation has, as its prominent [members](https://www.linuxfoundation.org/about/members)---careful if clicking through, by the way, because the page takes an absurd amount of resources to render some logos and links, enough that I needed to log in remotely to my own machine to kill the browser---Amazon, Ericsson, Fujitsu, Google, Hitachi, Huawei, Meta, Microsoft, NEC, Oracle, Qualcomm, IBM, Samsung, Anthropic, Dell, Future Wei, Honda, LY, Mitsubishi, Panasonic (multiple divisions), Renesas, Sony, Toshiba, and Toyota.  You might notice that their "movers and shakers" don't include any humans, but do include the majority of companies screaming at you to accept the inevitability of LLMs before you get left behind in...the mass layoffs, I guess.

This probably also goes a long way to explaining the recent tantrum from Linux Torvalds about how Linux doesn't engage in politics, but wholeheartedly embraces LLMs.  Yeah, somehow the Free Software ethos that turned Linux from a forgettable, flawed one-off into a behemoth in the market doesn't qualify as politics, nor does the extensive use of a technology created by oligarchs by harvesting labor to suppress wages.  Funny how that works.  Anyway, that outburst seems far less surprising when you see those exact oligarchs funding the organization.

Anyway, I point this out, because when "the Linux Foundation" does something, they do it on behalf of their large donor-members, not the community of Linux users or developers.  The Foundation wants to push the license because their members want that framework in place.  In previous years, they funded development of lightweight "cloud-native" Linux systems (only useful to cloud providers) and data center designs.

## The OSI

Because I sometimes use them as a bit of a punching bag, we should also talk about the [Open Source Initiative](http://opensource.org/), too.

The OSI launched in 1998 as a counter-balance to the Free Software Foundation.  As I have described in the posts about the Free Culture movement, they openly wanted to replace the "Free Software" term with something that would encourage large corporations to aggressively exploit it, without getting the stink of anti-commercialism on them.

Most prominently, they maintain their Open Source Definition, and a list of OSI-approved open source licenses.  Over the past couple of years, they have attempted to expand to the "Open Source AI Definition" (OSAID; maybe the most misleading acronym in the industry), with the presumed intent of landing here, judging "open source AI" licenses.

For all their faults---and they have *so many* faults---unlike the Linux Foundation, they have individual members (humans who do things) and [Affiliate Organizations](https://opensource.org/affiliates), who throw money at them on occasion.  And even there, their Affiliates include various foundations in the space, individual software projects, and universities, rather than Silicon Valley's Big Five.  And they do have (more or less) public debate on mailing lists.

## License Concept

Remember the license, after all that?

Yeah, the license purports to do...something.

> The Open MDW License Agreement v1.1 is a permissive license specifically crafted for machine‑learning models and their related artifacts, collectively referred to as "Model Materials." It aims to provide a single, easy‑to‑apply license agreement that enables openness, consistency and clarity across all components of an AI model release.

The first sentence sounds like an AI wrote it, and I have no idea what it thinks that it means.  The second sentence says "BSD, but for AI models."  Why not use BSD or a similar license?  Ahem.  Apparently we don't need to know that.

Anyway, I'll refer to their [about page](https://openmdw.ai/about/) in a couple of cases where it might---place your bets now...---clarify some point in the license itself.

## Model

To start with my concerns, do LLM models even qualify for copyright?

Using {% wiki GGUF %} as (almost certainly) representative of what constitutes a model, it contains seven parts.

- Literal ASCII text `GGUF`, as a "magic number" to identify the file.
- GGUF version number.
- Number of tensors in the model.
- Number of metadata elements.
- Key-value pairs that make up the metadata.
- List of tensors.
- Weights.

The metadata could include anything, but generally only identifies and describes the model.  It also doesn't affect the model's behavior.  The first two items, similarly, don't do anything but explain what kind of file that you have.

Tensors...well, I usually oversimplify and use the term "vector," because the odds increase that people will understand the term, but tensors amount to roughly the same thing.  A vector has a magnitude and direction.  A tensor describes linear relationships between vectors (and scalars, matrices, and so forth), describing how to transform one to the other, so they generally look fairly similar.  Generally, we represent tensors as multidimensional arrays of numbers between zero and one.

And the weights tell us (or our software, rather) how much to---no surprises, here---*weigh* the various tensors when choosing between them.

I assume that you can start to see the problem.  The United States Copyright Office has an entire circular, [Works Not Protected by Copyright](https://www.copyright.gov/circs/circ33.pdf).  At the top of their list, they identify "Ideas, Methods, or Systems."  What does that mean, beyond their hand-waving, there?  Well, they have an entirely separate circular on [Ideas, Methods, or Systems](https://www.copyright.gov/circs/circ31.pdf), where they say the following, emphasis mine.

> Copyright law does not protect ideas, methods, or systems. Copyright protection is therefore not available for ideas or procedures for doing, making, or building things; scientific or technical methods or discoveries; business operations or procedures; mathematical principles; **formulas or algorithms**; or any other concept, process, or method of operation.

I can't think of any way to understand lists of tensors and weights as something other than formulas, making it impossible to protect the model.  Arguably, you could protect the metadata, but again, it doesn't carry any of the value.

You might want to call the model a [database](https://copyright.gov/non-photographic-databases/) of those formulas, because copyright law apparently now allows for a database copyright, but they have a fairly strict definition.

> - The contents must be arranged in a systematic manner.
> - A query function must be the sole method used to access the contents of the database.
> - The database information retrieval program or system must yield a subset of the content, or it must organize the content based on the parameters specified in each query.

In fact, a model in this sense fails all three criteria.  The contents fall out of the training passes, and we can't predict what ended up where in the list.  Anybody can access the tensors and metadata however they please, with no query function provided.  And no standard "find tensors that match these criteria" methods exist.

That leaves one remaining possibility, thinking of it as a general creative work, like a painting or software.  The Copyright Office [describes copyright](https://www.copyright.gov/what-is-copyright/) as applying to *original works of authorship*, then defines that.

> Works are original when they are independently created by a human author and have a minimal degree of creativity.

Again, humans don't generally create AI models[^6oYyIg].  The training software does the work, independent of the human authors.  And since it encodes probabilistic descriptions of the training data, it also has no creativity, such that these training systems *should* produce the same model every time, given the same training data and parameters.

[^6oYyIg]:  I don't doubt that a person or group could go through training data by hand and build an "artisanal" model, but it doesn't happen, and the Linux Foundation certainly doesn't have that prospect in mind.

If a model doesn't rise to the level of having a valid copyright, then what rights can the license grant?

### Legality

Actually, this raises an interesting problem for the license, I think.  [17 USC §506(c)](https://www.copyright.gov/title17/92chap5.html#:~:text=Fraudulent%20Copyright%20Notice) says the following about applying fraudulent copyright notices.

> Any person who, with fraudulent intent, places on any article a notice of copyright or words of the same purport that such person knows to be false...shall be fined not more than $2,500.

If you can't get a copyright for the LLM, then doesn't offering the public a copyright license for it open them up to exactly this kind of lawsuit?  Granted, Meta doesn't care about a few thousand dollars; they wasted tens of billions of dollars on their creepy leg-less metaverse, only a couple of years ago.  But a hobbyist who would like to share their ethically sourced LLM probably can't afford it.

I'll get back to this.

## Change It As You Wish

Yeah, we haven't even cracked open the license, and it already seems broken.  But as we do start reading, we find problems almost immediately.

> As used in this agreement, "Model Materials" means the materials provided to you under this agreement, consisting of: (1) one or more machine learning models (including architecture and parameters); and (2) all related artifacts (including associated data, documentation and software) that are provided to you hereunder.

The second freedom that we need for a valid Free Software (Open Source) license, though, says this.

> The freedom to study how the program works, and change it to make it do what you wish.

We could also refer to the last of the freedoms, which also has some relevance, here.

> The freedom to improve the program, and release your improvements (and modified versions in general) to the public, so that the whole community benefits.

How does one sensibly change an AI model without the training data and all the training infrastructure?  You could theoretically hand-edit a GGUF file, yeah, but it has no sign-posts to identify what changes would have what effects.  In fact, quite the opposite, the mystique around the LLM industry says that we only have figurative black boxes with mysterious internal workings.  If you could pinpoint the tensor that chooses the likely mapping between "write me a Forth function that downloads a random Wikipedia page" and the resulting code, then we wouldn't have "AI" so much as a (shoddy) compiler.

*If* they committed to supplying the training data and method of training, that would allow for modification.  But poking at tables of numbers and hoping that something useful happens hardly qualifies as "free" or "open."

## Normal Stuff

We then have some boilerplate, almost certainly lifted from other licenses.

> Subject to your compliance with this agreement, permission is hereby granted, free of charge, to deal in the Model Materials without restriction, including under all copyright, patent, database, and trade secret rights included or embodied therein.
>
> If you distribute any portion of the Model Materials, you shall retain in your distribution (1) a copy of this agreement, and (2) all copyright notices and other notices of origin included in the Model Materials that are applicable to your distribution.

Yeah, we have permission to use and distribute the dingus, provided that the license and attribution comes along.  This at least references the first freedom required to qualify.

> The freedom to use the program for any purpose.

And...sure.  It does that, technically.

## Wait, What

And then we take a wild left turn.

> If you file, maintain, or voluntarily participate in a lawsuit against any person or entity asserting that the Model Materials directly or indirectly infringe any patent or copyright, then all rights and grants made to you hereunder are terminated, unless that lawsuit was in response to a corresponding lawsuit first brought against you.
>
> This agreement does not impose any restrictions or obligations with respect to any use, modification, or sharing of any outputs generated by using the Model Materials.

It doesn't impose any restrictions or obligations...except for the *entire paragraph* banning you from having any involvement in a lawsuit against anybody involved in the supply chain that brought the model to you.

And by the way, note the vagueness of "voluntarily participate."  They would expect us to read that as suing them, but providing testimony[^7d_8F3], serving as a juror, writing an amicus brief, and so forth---maybe even working with the defense---bars you from taking advantage of the license.  And note that this ban applies to the person, not the role, so even a juror, who only has the choice of stepping down to avoid voluntarily participating, could find themselves in a bad situation at a workplace that requires the use of an LLM supplied by a party in the case under the terms of the Open MDW license.

[^7d_8F3]:  My name doesn't appear in any case that I know of, but because I supplied my experience in examining LLMs to several lawyers pursuing such lawsuits, that could ban me from using various models.

Also, specifically, this seems to admit that they have every expectation that their models will violate copyright, if they prioritize protecting those vendors from copyright infringement lawsuits.

What motivates this?  Their about page has a section about it.

> Open MDW-1.1 incorporates a patent‑and copyright-litigation termination clause: if a licensee initiates litigation alleging that the Model Materials infringe their patents or copyrights---except as a defensive response to a suit first brought against them---all granted rights under Open MDW-1.1 are terminated. This provision serves to discourage aggressive patentor copyright assertions that could undermine open collaboration.

Ah.  Their over-broad exclusion derives from the idea that, calling them out for stealing from you *undermines open collaboration*.  Personally, I would say that the theft undermined open collaboration, but that wouldn't allow them to claim authority to decide how this shakes out.

And again, I want to point out that this consistently contradicts their claims about conditions.

> The only substantive condition imposed by Open MDW-1.1 is preservation of the license itself: any redistribution of Model Materials must include (1) a copy of the Open MDW-1.1 Agreement and (2) all original copyright and origin notices. There are no copyleft or share‑alike requirements, ensuring that derivative works and integrations remain as unconstrained as possible.

Maybe I overlooked something because I don't have a law degree, but "stay away from lawsuits against us" seems like an *extremely* "substantive condition," well beyond keeping a license file around.

Buried in the (mostly useless) [FAQ](https://openmdw.ai/faq/), by the way, it expands on this and...makes it worse.

> Although the scope is broader, this provision is intended to be comparable to "defensive termination" provisions relating to patents in other licenses such as Apache-2.0. Defensive termination provisions cannot ensure the absence of patent aggression, such as from "non-practicing entities" or other patent holders that don’t make use of the Open MDW-1.1 materials themselves. However, this provision aims to discourage aggressive patent or copyright assertions from among the users of the Model Materials that could hinder open collaboration.

Ah.  It works *exactly like* a patent provision in another license, but broadens it, again *to discourage litigation*, because calling them out might hinder their idea of working together.

Also, note their casual use of a term that Native American groups have *begged* people not to use[^8DZVKw] for an organization appropriating the name to evoke the nobility and romance of Geronimo and the "last days" of the Apache people, despite...you know, nine federally recognized Apache tribes in the American Southwest, plus assorted state-recognized groups and informal enclaves and people living on their own in the United States, Mexico, and Canada, in total numbering around two hundred thousand people.

[^8DZVKw]:  And yes, I still have about a dozen posts left that still refer to the organization by their original name instead of the ASF, often in referring to the license.  However, I didn't spot the issue until recently and have (slowly) worked to fix it, whereas the Linux Foundation *absolutely* knows better, but continues to use the offending name.

### Retaliation

Actually, I didn't notice it the first couple of times through that passage, but one point finally caught my notice.

> ...unless that lawsuit was in response to a corresponding lawsuit first brought against you.

Why would the vendor sue me for copyright infringement, exactly?  Doesn't a public license exist *specifically* and exclusively to assure me that the vendor won't sue me for doing what the license permits?

Even if we took the anti-litigation clause seriously, qualifying it so offhandedly means that the license doesn't actually grant us any permission.  The vendor can revoke that permission at any time without warning, meaning that we don't actually have a license at all.

In fact, you'll notice that the license *never* says that the copyright holder can't revoke the grant of rights, like the Creative Commons licenses do.

## More Boilerplate

We get some of the usual nonsense, adapted from the non-copyleft licenses.

> THE MODEL MATERIALS ARE PROVIDED "AS IS", WITHOUT WARRANTY OF ANY KIND, EXPRESS OR IMPLIED, INCLUDING BUT NOT LIMITED TO THE WARRANTIES OF MERCHANTABILITY, FITNESS FOR A PARTICULAR PURPOSE, TITLE, NONINFRINGEMENT, ACCURACY, OR THE ABSENCE OF LATENT OR OTHER DEFECTS OR ERRORS, WHETHER OR NOT DISCOVERABLE, ALL TO THE GREATEST EXTENT PERMISSIBLE UNDER APPLICABLE LAW.

And...I guess.  But for some reason, the bad guys always need to keep talking.  They could've stopped here, but nah.

## Free As in Mattress

We then veer into silliness again.  Don't ask about the stains or the smell.

> YOU ARE SOLELY RESPONSIBLE FOR (1) CLEARING RIGHTS OF OTHER PERSONS THAT MAY APPLY TO THE MODEL MATERIALS OR ANY USE THEREOF, INCLUDING WITHOUT LIMITATION ANY PERSON’S COPYRIGHTS OR OTHER RIGHTS INCLUDED OR EMBODIED IN THE MODEL MATERIALS; (2) OBTAINING ANY NECESSARY CONSENTS, PERMISSIONS OR OTHER RIGHTS REQUIRED FOR ANY USE OF THE MODEL MATERIALS; OR (3) PERFORMING ANY DUE DILIGENCE OR UNDERTAKING ANY OTHER INVESTIGATIONS INTO THE MODEL MATERIALS OR ANYTHING INCORPORATED OR EMBODIED THEREIN.

This actually goes back to the validity of an AI model's copyright.  They admit, here, that they don't expect model vendors to check the copyright status of the pieces that go into and ship with the model, expecting the users to deal with all copyright searches, consent, permission, and other legal issues, and the consequences of getting it wrong.

Or to put it another way, the license admits that the vendor probably doesn't have permission to give you the model.

I know, you want to interrupt me, here.  "But John," you say, "they must mean verifying that the output from using a model doesn't violate somebody's copyright."

And sure, maybe, but to that, I have two responses.

First, it *doesn't say that*.  It says that the user holds all responsibility for securing permission to use "the model materials."  If it meant the output, then it should have *said* some words referring to the output.  Legal documents need to mean what they say, or else you only ever have "handshake deals" where the more powerful party can claim that you agreed to different terms, as they remember it.

If I---hypothetically speaking, since I don't expect to traffic in AI models in the near future---need to walk into a court to defend myself based on this license, then I would absolutely lose the case if I started talking about what the license might have wanted to say instead of the literal text.  Licenses and contracts exist to prevent arguments by defining everything explicitly.

Second, and maybe more importantly, I challenge you to find any public license that YELLS AT YOU for a full paragraph about making sure that you don't use the tool to infringe on somebody else's copyright.  Imagine if your compiler said "we take no responsibility if you write code that looks exactly like Microsoft Excel."  Imagine if your file-copy utility warned you to secure permission and consent before copying any file, or if your paint program insisted that you shouldn't copy Banksy's work without permission.  Licenses don't say that, because everybody should already know that no license gives you permission to break the law.  Contracts invalidate themselves when they involve illegal subjects, in fact, so if the license *did* grant permission to copy Microsoft Excel or **A Catcher in the Rye** or whatever, then as far as the courts care, that contract doesn't exist, and won't protect you.

As a corollary to that, *if* they mean that you can't use their product to infringe on somebody else's copyright---despite saying that you can't sue them for infringing on your copyrights---then that seems like it would violate its own assertion that it "does not impose any restrictions or obligations with respect to any use, modification, or sharing of any outputs."  It can't *grant* you the right to violate somebody else's copyright, but a valid Open Source license also can't tell you not to, because laws change by jurisdiction and over time.

### Motivation

Note how they frame this in their about page, incidentally.

> Critically, Open MDW-1.1 clarifies that any outputs generated by using the Model Materials are entirely free of license restrictions or obligations---a distinction often absent in traditional software licenses. Moreover, the license disclaims all warranties and liabilities "to the greatest extent permissible under applicable law," placing responsibility for due diligence and rights clearance squarely on the licensee.

First, note the snotty swipe at other licenses.  They represent social progress, by granting you full permission of whatever the model vomits up, unlike all those other licenses.  And...OK, *technically* other licenses don't state this explicitly.  But does anybody think that the LibreOffice team secretly owns the copyright to your spreadsheets, or the Inkscape team secretly owns the copyright to your vector images?  At its most rigorous, a copyleft license *might* govern the output of software under the same license, but nobody worries about output file copyrights, for...actually, I want to talk about that next.

I initially saw the attack on other licenses as a red herring, something to deflect criticism away from this license by lashing out at other licenses first.  However, thinking about it, it seems like a part to a broader philosophical problem across the entire disclaimer and the thinking around it.  The model outputs have no "license restrictions or obligations," but...what does that matter?

The US Copyright Office, in its [Copyright and Artificial Intelligence](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/) section on [Copyrightability](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/Copyright-and-Artificial-Intelligence-Part-2-Copyrightability-Report.pdf), it provides a clear analogy.

> Although an AI-generated output cannot be considered a joint work with respect to the user and AI system, joint authorship provides a helpful analogy in assessing whether a party contributed sufficient expression to be considered an author. To be a joint author, one must make a copyrightable contribution. "A person who merely describes to an author what the commissioned work should do or look like is not a joint author for purposes of the Copyright Act."

In plain language, the model's output doesn't contribute to the copyright.  We all understand this intuitively, I think.  If you ask an LLM to write you a story about or produce a picture of {% wiki Tommy_and_Tuppence|Prudence "Tuppence" Cowley %} fending off {% wiki Dr. Mabuse %} with a {% wiki Runcible|runcible spoon %}, then you can't legally secure a copyright on it, because you only have the result of an algorithm shingling together other people's work.  If you have it clean up certain aspects of your work or suggest developments that you implement, it'll probably do a bad job, but it doesn't reduce your eligibility for a copyright on that work.

This ties *tightly* to the GPL's alleged "failure" to grant you copyright over output files, really:  If you treat any software tool that helps you make things---compilers, graphics software, office suites, or whatever---like a possible joint creator, then you can see that you control all the creative bits.  Yes, your spreadsheet program adds some boilerplate and does some math for you, but *you* did the work, not the splanguagereadsheet.  By claiming otherwise, the Linux Foundation tries to imbue software with humanity (as a requirement for receiving copyright), and wants to convince you that the trigonometry involved in drawing a pie chart from your data contributes at least as much creativity to your spreadsheet as you do, and that makes no sense...unless you run a business raising money off the fiction of software that might magically become conscious any day now.

Therefore, Open MDW's *generous* grant of rights on the model output grants you exactly nothing.

### Returning to Legality

By the way, do you remember 17 USC §506(c) warning everybody not to deliberately place a copyright notice or equivalent on any article "with fraudulent intent"?  This paragraph of the license---and, arguably, the provision against lawsuits---*establishes* the fraudulent intent for a prospective plaintiff.

Releasing the model under a public license (aspiring for recognition as an open source public license) at least strongly implies ownership.  Let's work through a silly example.  If I made a big production of uploading a *Star Wars* film somewhere, after adding a GPL notice to the credits, that action strongly implies that I claim ownership over it and the authority to release it under a Free Culture license, regardless of my actual right to do so.  If Disney sues me, or if someone who wanted to adapt the film does and realizes that I did something inappropriate sues me, then I could try to argue that I didn't realize that Disney had the copyright, in hopes of weaseling out of the fine.  And maybe a particularly gullible judge would decide that I really do look that stupid, and tell me to pull down my copy of the film, no fines necessary.

However, if I released the film under an "open source" public license that incidentally takes pains to explain that I take no responsibility for the film's release violating anybody's copyrights, then that license serves as an admission that I fully expect that I might have done something wrong, but choose to claim ownership anyway.  In other words, I have used the license to identify my claim as likely fraudulent, and that made the fraud public knowledge.

## Last Boilerplate

We have one more stock bit that they adapted from elsewhere.

> IN NO EVENT SHALL THE PROVIDERS OF THE MODEL MATERIALS BE LIABLE FOR ANY CLAIM, DAMAGES OR OTHER LIABILITY, WHETHER IN AN ACTION OF CONTRACT, TORT OR OTHERWISE, ARISING FROM, OUT OF OR IN CONNECTION WITH THE MODEL MATERIALS, THE USE THEREOF OR OTHER DEALINGS THEREIN.

I can only note, here, that this clause seems like it should cover what the anti-lawsuit clause earlier *should* intend, but they genuinely want to scare people away from suing them.

## Compatibility

To give you some idea of how closely the Linux Foundation reviewed their license, I want to point out something in the aforementioned FAQ---largely useless, because it only recycles verbatim text from the license or description---about compatibility between licenses.  It calls itself *probably* compatible with other licenses, under the following reasoning.

> Because it is permissive and imposes minimal conditions, you can generally **combine** Model Materials licensed under Open MDW‑1.1 with code under MIT, Apache-2.0, the BSD family of licenses, and similarly permissive licenses. Care should be taken if integrating with copyleft licenses (e.g. GPL-3.0), as their terms may impose additional requirements on the combined work.

First, note the repeat of the use of a tribal name to refer to the license.

Also, though, nobody asking about license compatibility wants to know if the files can live in the same folder without getting drunk and starting fights.  They want to know what, if anything, might prevent them from releasing a model in a project that announces that the team released it under an MIT license, the AGPL, or whatever.

In fact, the *next* question comes closer to asking that question---asking if an Open MDW-licensed project can include work released under different licenses---by rambling, before settling on this gem.

> In such a case, the model provider should be very clear in the primary documentation (e.g. the top-level README and/or LICENSE files) about the presence of content that is subject to other license terms. The other applicable license terms should be clearly stated, along with an easy-to-understand explanation about which files and content are subject to the non-Open-MDW terms.
>
> A model provider may want to consider distributing such non-Open MDW artifacts in a separate distribution or repository if feasible, to avoid confusion to downstream users about which portions are subject to Open MDW-1.1.

If you can *totally* combine projects under different licenses, provided that you carefully separate and annotate them so that nobody mistakes one piece for having the other license, then I assure you that you **do not** have compatible licenses.

## Conclusions

I don't know about you, but I read this as trying to create authority, not freedom.  And it probably fails to do that.

The license takes an absurd view of what it means "to use the [model] for any purpose," since you explicitly may not use it if you have any involvement in a lawsuit against them.  It takes a dangerously narrow view on "to study how the [model] works, and change it to make it do what you wish" and "to improve the [model]," in that it doesn't even suggest that you might get the information in a form that a reasonable person could change.  Along the way, it exposes the vendors to liability, by suggesting that they have fraudulently asserted copyright.  It also grants rights that don't exist, but also seems to reserve the right to revoke your permission at any time, if they would rather sue you.

The license *does* allow you to make copies, but without any concept of source code, the other freedoms have no meaning.  Honestly, if this license qualified as an open source license, I have trouble envisioning a public license that would fail to meet the same criteria that this does.

Meanwhile, it tries to manufacture consent for an "AI copyright" that it can grant you a license for, while claiming that AI output deserves copyright, and also asserting the right to infringe on *your* copyrights, but also warns you not to violate anybody else's copyrights.  Like I said, it has more interest in establishing authority than freedom.

One final thought boiling all that down?  If I needed an AI model that I could distribute as part of a larger Free Software project, then relying on this license would scare the heck out of me.
