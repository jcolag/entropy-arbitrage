Probably another short post.  We'll see how it goes.  I also didn't expect this to go out too late, honestly...

Some of you probably can guess that I've had parts of this post kicking around in my drafts for a long time, because I often find myself noting that many of the world's problems tend to stem from never having a discussion about what we want from our institutions.  Our lack of consensus, and often lack of any significant thought, about the goals allows grifters and partisans to manipulate these institutions to their benefit, making the situation progressively worse.

![A person standing on what looks like a giant root, staring at an endless forest](/blog/assets/48-rylvbs4.png "Why did I come in here again...?")

The issue came back to mind with the botched execution of {% wiki Christa Pike %}.  Nobody can reasonably defend what she did, but I also don't think that anybody can rationalize incarcerating her for almost thirty years, only to subject her to her current condition.  Personally, I don't believe in capital punishment *because* we can't eliminate the chance of a bogus conviction, exactly these technical problems, or other issues, even before we get to arguing over the degree to which we consider life precious.

Critically, though, it forces me to ask what we inexplicably never ask.

## Capital Punishment

What do we *do* this for?

Seriously, what purpose does it serve to execute convicted criminals?  Executions don't reduce crime; in fact, depending on the role of the deceased in their family or community, their deaths might push people into more desperate circumstances, thereby *increasing* crime.  A dead perpetrator doesn't help the victims.  You can't rehabilitate a corpse.  Presumably, we don't want any of those, because if we did, then we wouldn't have this system...if we stopped to ask, at least.

The system *does* inflict suffering in various degrees on people convicted of certain crimes---not discriminating between rightly or wrongly convicted---and doesn't seem to serve any purpose beyond that.  And we seem to *not* want that, hence the obsession with finding an alleged [*humane* death penalty](https://otherwords.org/theres-no-moral-death-penalty/).  In many ways, it feels like it only serves the purpose of [expressing outrage](https://theconversation.com/death-penalty-can-express-societys-outrage-but-biases-often-taint-the-verdict-169711), with no interest in the consequences, with [justice barely considered](https://theconversation.com/in-death-penalty-cases-the-quest-for-justice-is-not-americas-highest-value-256042).

Do we, as a society, want any of those, some combination, and/or something else?  Nobody knows, so we have no idea whether to change any particular policy or method.  And that feels like a heck of a problem, when you get to irrevocable actions such as execution; when they go wrong, you can't *fix* the problem.  And therefore, we nibble around the edges trying to make horrible acts like executions feel slightly less horrible.

We have done this for a long time.  In a firing squad, for example, few of the executioners have live ammunition loaded for them.  Everybody else has "blanks," with nobody knowing who does and who doesn't, the theory going that it softens the trauma of shooting someone to death if you had a low probability of actually firing a deadly shot.

## Incarceration

We could go broader, here, and ask many of the same questions about the criminal justice system in general.  What outcome do we want?

Because we can't say that we care specifically about reducing crime or helping victims or anything else, we end up with these obscene situations where we put people in prison for years.  This ends in releasing them back onto the streets with no support and a society that will happily and legally discriminate against them, *or* we force them to make an uninformed choice of their method of execution.

And we do all this so that...nobody knows why, as far as I can tell.  Like executions in the narrower view, incarceration doesn't reduce crime, doesn't help the victims, and only rehabilitates people accidentally.

It "gets them off the streets" for a few years, separating them from the general population, but we have more effective ways of doing such things that don't ruin lives.  And because we don't have that conversation on the purposes, I doubt that anybody sees that as the purpose of the system, only a sometimes-convenient side effect.

## Education

You might notice that, to a lot of people, we have a smooth transition from incarceration to schools.  *I* don't necessarily buy into it, because I think that I had a fairly good education, but I can also see where I sometimes got a good education in defiance of official policy, and where I almost certainly got extra help because I presented as a highly motivated student, even when I had no interest in a class and went through the motions.  In other words, I can understand why some-to-many students see school as an "outpatient prison."

You might want to push back on my having schools on this list, though, because *we all know* why schools exist:  We make schools to educate people.  But think a bit deeper about it, and you can start to see a lot of the disconnects that we have in the educational space.

For example, what do we want to educate students *for*?  If we go through these processes to create an industrial workforce, then those processes diverge greatly from what we'd do to enrich the lives of the students.  If we do it to expose students to enough fields that one might inspire their future career choices or hobbies, that would diverge greatly from how we'd give everybody a sense of succeeding.  And if we only want to keep kids off the streets for six hours per day, that diverges from visions of every student becoming a polymath who adores academic subjects.

Or we could ask how we know if we have successfully educated the students.  We have tests and essays, but as I've written about before, we create [tests designed to pass without learning]({% post_url 2026-04-05-reclaiming-education %}) while screaming that students use new technologies to cheat.  We have faster- and slower-paced classes, but they tend to break down more sharply by class than individual skill in separate fields.

And regardless of how we feel about our own educations, I think that we can all agree that school produces *bad outcomes*.  Schools often slow down some advanced students, while abandoning students who need more help.  Over-burdened educators find themselves "teaching to the test" instead of the actual interesting aspects.  Political organizations push out topics that they find controversial.  We could go on endlessly.  And those vulnerabilities exist because we don't have that idea of what schools should actually do.

## Free Culture

Let's also talk a bit about organizations in the Free Culture/Software space randomly lighting themselves on fire, these days.

We have always had the [FSF defending harassment-positive individuals]({% post_url 2021-05-09-fsf %}).  We have Creative Commons constantly coming up with ideas that amount to [stop complaining about the poor corporations]({% post_url 2025-06-29-signal-change %}).  Many critical projects insist that they can't worry about little things like quality, team cohesion, or reputation, because they want to compromise with people deluging them with low-quality LLM-generated "contributions."  Others have thrown support behind Omarchy, Arch Linux with a 1990s hacker aesthetic and an actual fascist manifesto.  Some groups have rejected all that thinking, but too few, and usually the smaller groups.

That doesn't even get to the traditional problems in the space.  We have always had the OSI pushing developers to make their work useful for exploitation by companies that could absolutely afford to pay for it.  Creative Commons has always tried to push the idea of using their licenses as advertising to sell the "real" content.  Too many projects have ignored the pleas of users with disabilities as they requested standard accessibility features, along with the art products with no interest in accessibility.

When someone does present a vision of what we have Free Culture *for*, it always pushes for more corporate-friendly, human-exclusionary policies.  "Fork it if you don't like it" ignores the infrastructure and labor required to run a large project to force people to accept bad policies.  Terms like "liberal license" treat non-copyleft licenses as "more free" than the licenses that protect long-term freedom.  Insistence on a "meritocracy" goes a level deeper on this discussion, because it always quietly avoids defining what qualifies as merit, and so means that the established team gets to exclude whoever they don't like.

## And So Forth

We could go on forever, here.  Democracy, the economy, and many other areas all constantly find themselves vulnerable to attacks and subversion, because when some powerful person says that "we should use this institution to make wealthy people even more wealthy," people show up to express their outrage, but we can't figuratively tap the sign, saying that no, we have this system for reasons X, Y, and Z.

And yeah, it didn't occur to me until now, but it does occur to me that you can think of this post as connected to last week's post on [customers]({% post_url 2026-09-27-customers %}), in that you need to get to the root of things to get anywhere.

Without those questions, without knowing what we collectively want out of a system, then we don't have that structure to protect those systems from going wrong.
