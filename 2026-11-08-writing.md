* Ignore for ToC
{:toc}

After pushing out my post on [elections and beyond]({% post_url 2026-11-01-voting %}) last weekend, somebody drew my attention to a small oversight in the post.  Well, I call it small in the context of that post, which mostly revolved around holding your nose and voting for the candidate who you have the best chance of pushing in the right direction, and that doesn't include the pushing part.

![A US Marine writing a letter home](/blog/assets/36670192026_1819e92ccd_o.png "Please vote yes on extra gravy for Thanksgiving...")

Yes, for all I talked about regularly writing your elected officials to nudge them in the right direction, I ignore *how* to do that.  Let's walk through that in a brief post now, so that you can get your letters ready.

## Get off My Lawn

I want to first talk a little about what this does *not* involve.

To the extent that you can manage, don't treat this message like coordinating lunch with your friends.  Don't try to dazzle anybody with your technical expertise.  That means the following.

- No e-mail
- No voice calls
- No SMS
- No Telegram bot or AI agent mob or whatever

They didn't create Congress in the twenty-first century.  At the moment, your *officials* probably predate the twenty-first century[^1CChHq], so don't get fancy.

[^1CChHq]:  You might have some young state or local officials.  Nationally, though, eligibility for the House of Representatives requires a minimum age of twenty-five years, so you *might* have someone born in 2001 in the remaining three districts counting their votes, but as I write this, the incoming (120th) Congress doesn't have anybody younger than Wisconsin Republican {% wiki Michael_Alfonso_(politician)|Michael Alfonso %} born in early 2000, technically not the twenty-first century.  After him, you have returning Florida Democrat {% wiki Maxwell Frost %} born in 1997.  Senators have a minimum age of thirty-five, so even Jon Ossoff remembers the Clinton administration.

Newer technologies present a convenience to you, but they also present a convenient opportunity to ignore you.  E-mails get "lost," and even when they go through, it doesn't take much effort to set up a "thank you for your concern" auto-responder that bumps a number on a spreadsheet.  Stop answering the phone, and voicemail fills up.  Anything else, you can't even guarantee that they have anybody on staff to look.

## Whatever Remains

I know, that excludes everything that you know how to use.  I get it.

Really, though, you need to think like an older professional working in a two-hundred-fifty-year-old institution.  You write them *on paper*.  You send it in an envelope with stamps.

Why?  They can't ignore paper.  An office worker can't delete paper.  Paranoid offices need to make sure that you didn't use the envelope to commit biological warfare.  Somebody needs to *file* paper, either literally or in constituent-management systems, which means actually reading it.  Oh, and if they don't process it fast enough, it piles up, threatening to push them out of the office.

Seriously, I mean it.  When you contact your elected officials, do it on paper.  They understand that language.

## Contents

Pull out your word processor and get moving.  You learned to write a business letter in school.  Your word processor knows how to format a business letter.  Use a business letter format.

### Salutation

You know how to do this, I assume, but for completeness, you open with **Dear**.  You address them as **Mr.**, **Mrs.**, or **Ms.** and their surname.

### Introduce Yourself

This takes more thought than you might imagine.  People have told me over the years that you open with a list.  It starts out **As a...** and the list has three sections.  First, the list identifies why they have some obligation to care what you think, specifically one of these two.

- As a constituent...
- As a constituent, donor to your campaign...

Only claim status as a donor if you actually donated to their campaign[^JSiAKu], because they have that list handy.

[^JSiAKu]:  Everybody with money who I've ever talked to about politics wouldn't shut up about how they donate some money to every candidate in every race, precisely so that they can pull the "as a donor" card.

Next, continue the list with what makes you an expert or why you have a stake in what you'll write about.  You own a small business, make art, have a child with a disability, work with transgender colleagues, work in a specific field, or whatever else (again, true) translates to you needing the problem solved, and that they might want to ask your advice.  The choices depend a lot on the issue under consideration, but make sure that you only have noun phrases that describe you.  Think in terms like "a small business owner, an artist, a parent of..."

Then, end the list puffing yourself up.  Specifically, you want to give a sense of the number of people who you could (plausibly) mobilize on the issue if you need to.  Maybe you attend regular religious services, teach classes, hold office in a club, or plenty of other options.  In other words, you can viably claim to speak for more than yourself, and you have influence.  Again, though, make sure that you only have those descriptive noun phrases.  You want "a churchgoer, educator, treasurer in a small organization," and so forth.

Finally, finish the sentence with your main reason for making contact.  In the roles that you list, you write to urge some action or because of your feelings on some issue.

### Explain

State your position in full and your reasons.  Back that up with whatever hard data that you can track down on the stakes of getting it wrong.

I had a professor in college who, when asked about the required lengths of submitted papers, would describe a viable paper as long enough to cover the topic, but short enough to not bore him.  Make your case, but keep it short enough that they don't start checking the news to distract them.

And keep it polite.

### Summarize and Close

Restate your preferred outcome.  Thank them for their time.  Avoid insipid threats about how, next time, you'll vote for the other candidate[^2LK-mW].  And, if you have expertise or direct experience with some aspect of the issue, you might offer to make yourself available to follow up, presenting yourself as a resource.

[^2LK-mW]:  After all, you mention the various groups where you have some sway in order to let *that* make the threat for you.  Only people in weak positions need to make threats out loud.

Finally, you sincerely or respectfully sign off with your full name.

### Addressing

Who does this go to?  Your letter goes to more inboxes than you might initially consider.

First, though, you address them on the envelope as *The Honorable* as a title before their...well, not necessarily their *full* name, but the full name that they use professionally.

The letter then goes to *both* their offices.  Except for local officials, everybody has a local office and an office in the legislature.  You want both, noting that a copy has gone to each.  This saves you the trouble of remembering whether they have an active session, and also doubles up on the filing for them.

I have also heard strong recommendations for also copying the officials one level down and one level up.  For example, your local member of the (in the United States) House of Representatives might not have a strong interest in paying attention to your specific issues, but if you can recruit your Senator and/or a couple of state legislators to the cause, then they have better access than you do.

Yes, send copies to both their offices, too.

## Possible Opportunity

As a quick aside, deep in the recesses of my older notes, I have a project idea that I'll almost certainly never bother with due to the logistical problems.  I called it "[Resist-Bot {% cc %}](https://resist.bot/) for Grown-ups."

The real, released project grossly misunderstands the problem that it wants to solve.  It makes contacting your officials convenient, but in ways that almost certainly don't make any impact, for reasons that I have mentioned.  Instead, if you wanted to do this right, you'd want to let users pick a bill or issue from a list of known concerns, use that to provide a list of identities to select that give weight to what you have to say, and so forth.

And then---this presumably explains why I didn't build it and probably never will---you need to find some way to get the generated letter into physical envelopes and in the mail.  Do you trust the user to print it and do the work?  Do you ask the users for money and put in the labor to stuff envelopes, which the users need to trust that you'll do immediately?

Neither of those sound workable.  But I do see a role for *something* like this, even though it doesn't remove the need for personal discipline.

## Urgency

Sometimes, an issue comes up that can't wait for the postal service, legislature mailrooms, and the specific offices to get an envelope from your hands to the desk of someone on the official's staff.  At that point, you can call.

That said, if you get a full-voicemail message and can't leave a message of your own, then that does *not* mean that your side has already won.  It means that they can't (or won't) hear from most constituents, and won't guess at the number of people who didn't get through or their opinions.  Keep calling.

The calls follow a similar structure to the letters, but shorter.  Explain why they care what you think.  State your position.  Explain the stakes.  Offer more specifics on your experience.  Thank them.

## Meetings

As I have mentioned before, you should also try to meet with them (with a group) as often as your schedule and theirs allows.  This helps make your case by putting a literal face (yours) on the issue, rather than abstract facts and figures.

Somebody once told me that, if an official doesn't audibly groan when your name comes up, then you probably don't see them often enough.

## Caveats

I should note that I have gotten uncomfortably out of practice on this, after a decent run years ago.  Specifically, I fell for the siren call of e-mail, then got disillusioned by the lack of results there.  As such, if one of my officials gives you access to his files, you probably won't find much in there from me.

And yes, this takes time and costs money.  In the United States, a stamp currently costs eighty-two cents, so if you send two letters on each issue to your two Senators, Representative, and their state counterparts, then---using New York as an example, since I have some sense of how it works---every issue that matters to you will cost you more than eight dollars, plus the costs of the paper, envelope, and ink/toner.  And that assumes that you don't donate a couple of bucks to campaigns so that you can present yourself as part of *their* movement.

In the short term, this also doesn't feel much different sending an e-mail, in that you'll get a form letter in response thanking you for your input on `%TOPIC`, and depending on their original position and who else has their ear, it may not change their vote.  Remember, opposing you, the worst people who you could imagine have spent decades throwing money and time at presenting themselves as the experts who represent the will of the voters and the money that they need to rely on for reelection.

You do this to build credibility of the movement in general.  If you can get a group writing these sorts of letters, and expand that group, and follow up with meetings, then you build a more truthful vision of reality, where we *don't* have some silent majority of AI-positive white Christian nationalists who happen to stay home on Election Day when liberal and progressive candidates win.  And when politicians can connect their wins with visible public support, that changes policy; I often cite the {% wiki Lucy Parsons %} quote that "governments never lead; they follow progress," and this illustrates the progress for government to follow.  And if you don't do it, you let the progress stay invisible.
