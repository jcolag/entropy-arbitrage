Ah, yes, election season has come to the United States again, with Election Day on Tuesday, for those who haven't voted early already.  The Sun shines a little brighter.  The fruit tastes a little sweeter.  Everybody acts somewhat kinder to each other.

![A small fence with diamond-shaped signs hanging saying to Vote Here, in different languages; on the left, a handicap-accessible sign also hangs](/blog/assets/291492623_a1d732eea2_o.png "I mean, don't actually vote HERE, because my blog can't do anything with your ballot, but you get the idea...")

OK, none of that actually happens.  Where I live, we have crossed the boundary to less than ten-and-a-half hours of sunlight per day.  Fruit gets bred for its stability in shipping, so nobody actually knows what any of it should taste like.  And people stop talking to each other, concerned that they'll find out that their friend loves (or hates) fascists.  I exaggerate a bit, but do you know what we *really* get this time of year?

- Biased reporting that only seems to want us to know what one party does, quietly ignoring the Blood and Soil types.
- The Blood and Soil types already telling us that their loss derives from massive fraud (which never seems to happen in races that they win), and not because they all pitch a vision of the country that looks like a cyberpunk novel without the fun parts.
- Journalists prefacing their think-pieces with phrases like "I don't support Trump, *but*..." and then go on to explain why they think that voting for the other party actually caused all our problems.
- Purity tests everywhere except among the fascists, who have already elected every example that people would usually use as the dregs, including people with fictional identities.

I want to focus on that purity nonsense, this time, because I increasingly see comments like "don't trust candidates willing to sacrifice a group."  And while that seems reasonable, I'd like to propose a counterpoint.

## Don't Trust Any Politician, Ever

If you don't personally bribe a politician more than anybody else does, and if you don't blackmail that politician, you should assume that---all things equal---they will eventually sell you out.  Honestly, even then, somebody can probably outbid you.

This dynamic can happen for any number of reasons.  Let me run through some possibilities, because I definitely do **not** mean this in some cynical nothing-matters sense.

### Lies

Let's start with the prototypical example, that the candidate says whatever it takes to get elected, but only wants the power of the office.  They offered to sell out some vulnerable group not out of hatred, but because they don't care.

### Money

We all know this one, right?  Somebody comes along to them this time of year, and says look, we see you behind in the polls.  You only have a few dozen hours left to make your case to voters.  Our consortium likes you, so we will funnel such-and-such dollars to your campaign strategists, so that you can run ads, get people knocking on doors, and maybe donate some food to shelters so that the local news covers you as a good person instead of a candidate.

In exchange, they continue, you'll owe us a couple of favors in a few months.

The politician might assume that they can turn down the request later, but everybody in office needs to raise money for the party, so they'll need an ongoing source.  And c'mon, people can get used to data centers and some weirdo patting you down as you enter a public restroom to guess your gender...

### Feedback

Do you know how, probably once every year or two, I write about how you make change by regularly writing letters to your elected representatives and showing up at their office to discuss the issues that matter to you?  You all roll your eyes and go back to filling out online petitions or posting on social media about how it seems so short-sighted of politicians to not follow the zeitgeist on social media.

And while you do that, everybody on the right, from the cranks to the wealthy---where I got my advice, by the way---writes letters and schedules regular meetings with your representatives.  They explain that, as constituents, parents, students, small-business owners, donors, churchgoers, and whatever other credentials they can stuff into their introduction, they represent a broad cross-section of the community, and want to make sure that the official understands their positions.

While they make their crisp, polite presentations, you get angry about some bill, and decide to send an e-mail to the effect that they had better vote your way, or you'll never vote for them again.  Oh, and you probably didn't bother to include your address, so for all they know, "you" only exist as a mail merge program in some data center in Eastern Europe.  Why would they think that you have *ever* voted for them?

Guess whose concerns they'll treat as more pressing.

### Media

Think of this as *Feedback Writ Large*, so I won't go into too much depth.  But when all the TV news outlets and newspapers come down on the same side of an issue, that makes it sound like the other side has no valid points.

### Strategy

We also occasionally get maybe-good people who abruptly decide that the universe has bigger plans for them, so they start making whatever trades they can, to build enough power that they can start aiming for bigger seats of power.

Often, this backfires and fails, but that doesn't make it less appealing to politicians who think that they know better.

### Other

I could go on, here.  We have volunteers and party workers making their voices heard directly, or a misunderstanding of what constituents care about.  Horse-trading comes up often, compromising on one issue to secure a beneficial compromise from someone else on a different issue.  You could imagine emergencies tricking them into thinking that they need to act fast, and more.

## Seeing the Future

Critically, I need to point out that, despite many people claiming this uncanny ability, you generally can't see this coming.  The people making these claims actually mean that they see the reporting on the changes and don't discount it, but it sounds far less uncanny when you put it like that.

Take the two former darlings in this space, Kyrsten Sinema and John Fetterman.  Both of them started their careers as progressive darlings, but of the sort that also appealed to moderate conservatives.  But both also came to see their broad appeal as making them likely good presidential candidates, and so started walking through *most* of the sorts of issues that I list, in hopes of raising their profile, and better positioning themselves for bigger runs.

You can't predict that, because nobody has a list of moral compromises that they plan to make.  In those two cases, they probably did have the best of intentions for most of theirs.

OK, sure, *sometimes* you get someone who falsely describes himself as working-class, claims to never have noticed his Nazi tattoo, and covers up domestic violence incidents.  You can probably spot them pretty early.  They usually don't make things that clear, though.  And you might notice that plenty of people who claim to have a "nose" for this sort of thing probably told you not to worry about the tattoo, because young people make mistakes...

## The Woke Mob

Longtime readers probably already know why I bring this all up.

Do you know when we had candidates who got progressive legislation and regulation through?  We got that during the late 1960s and early 1970s.  We had amazingly progressive presidents, for example, such as---let me double-check my notes, here...---noted racists Lyndon B. Johnson and noted racist and pardoned (alleged) criminal Richard M. Nixon.  The {% wiki Voting Rights Act of 1965 %}, the {% wiki Environmental Protection Agency %}, the {% wiki Federal Election Campaign Act %}, and much more happened on their watches.  And I won't even waste time digging into the sorts of people who voted the bills through Congress.

How odd, right?  Two chief executives deeply offended by Black liberation, anti-war protests, and women's liberation, to say nothing of queer liberation, supported legislation that massively transformed the country for the better, getting us closer to living up to our claimed ideals, while in the years since, we can barely maintain the idea that medical insurance should probably cover existing conditions.

They didn't do so because they secretly wanted to go down in history as titans of progressive thought.  And they didn't do so because their hearts grew three sizes[^zeQbG7].  They did so, because an engaged electorate wouldn't accept anything less.  Failing to enact these laws would have resulted in more surges of protests, possibly escalating.  They'd need to deal with mass work stoppages and boycotts.

[^zeQbG7]:  In fact, Nixon did everything that he could to undermine FECA starting as soon as he signed it, explaining why you've probably never heard of it.

When politicians talk about their worries of "the Woke Mob," they mean this.  They live in fear that the people will organize and stand up for progressive causes, because then none of those career-shifting moments that we talked about work.  You can't take a ~~bribe~~ *large campaign contribution to your favorite political action committee* to change policy if a thousand people might stage a sit-in alongside the media to slow your entry into your office.

By contrast, they have no problems with polite demonstrations at the local park on a Saturday afternoon that inconvenience nobody and barely make it to the nightly news.  And they love it even more when you grumble about them on the Internet, because that won't affect anything unless you have a ton of influence...and if you have that much influence, then you probably don't care about social progress.  They have a lot to keep in their heads, and anybody who they can ignore clears space; anybody who they can't ignore, that takes space that could go to something else.

## Babysitting

Political movements used to say (some variation of) *elect the person who you'd rather fight*.

They didn't mean to elect whoever has the most punchable face; if they did, then we'd live in utopia; mission accomplished.  Rather, they meant that, of the people in each race, you vote for the person who you can most likely pressure into enacting as much of your agenda as possible.  If you have the choice between a fascist and a pro-corporate "centrist" unsure of who deserves human rights, then one of them responds to pressure better than the other, making them the better choice.  One has less conceptual distance to bridge to do the right thing.

In the (undeservedly obscure) musical **How to Steal an Election**, *Comes the Right Man* has a character[^X6DMHU] earnestly singing about some ideal candidate who does everything right and requires trust.  I suspect that it satirizes what I have called the "fire and forget" model of politics, the idea that you wait for some ideal candidate, vote for that person, and then walk away, trusting that your new representative will fix everything.

[^X6DMHU]:  On the original cast recording, Carole Demas sings it.  You might recognize her as the original Sandy from **Grease**.  Or at my age and living in the broad New York metropolitan area, hosting the children's show **The Magic Garden** with her best friend and pink squirrel puppet might also ring a bell.  Anyway, I managed to snag a recording on vinyl, so good luck tracking down a listenable copy elsewhere...

It doesn't work that way, though.  Politicians don't travel in straight lines, for the reasons that we have already discussed.  They need care and feeding, and if *you* don't provide that care, the worst people who you can think of will happily do it and pretend to speak for you.

## Vote, Plus

I have almost certainly said this before, but you need to think of voting as the *first* step, a critical step, but not remotely the last step.  You vote for the person who you'd rather fight, then start fighting.  Write letters.  Show up to appointments and events.  Get involved in the party.  Organize people[^82_y-C] to join you.  Feed and water your politician, so that they can see the right positions on policies.

[^82_y-C]:  It seems so strange that solving problems keeps coming back to organizing, no?  You'd almost believe that we have more powerful together than stomping around in rugged individualism.

That gets results.  Sitting back and then, when they do something wrong, refusing to ever support them again---because you now believe in retributive justice for some reason---*also* gets results, but always the opposite results that you want, because it installs someone in the office who cares even less what you have to say.  Declaring that "the lesser evil still looks evil" and refusing to engage also produces results, and not the results that you want.

If you treat elections like having a gig worker do your grocery shopping, don't act surprised when your "diamond-level" shopper makes a bunch of unpleasant substitutions on your behalf.  Go vote.  Then micromanage whoever wins.
