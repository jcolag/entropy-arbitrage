* Ignore for ToC
{:toc}

I recently discovered that the [BBC requested proposals for a presumably post-Disney **Doctor Who** {% cc %}](https://www.bbc.co.uk/commissioning/news/doctor-who-invitation-to-tender-september-2026/).  The deadline passed on October second, so I can't do much about that, and presumably they don't want more American interlopers, especially of the sort that won't shut up about *Star Trek*.

![A star-field with swooping lines leading towards a large bright spot](/blog/assets/15937475583_c4e1e15201_o.png "We'll get back to the military adventurism disguised as fun science fiction some other day.")

Instead, I'd like to think about what a Free Culture version of the *Doctor Who* franchise might look like, something that feels somewhat like the series and its offshoots, but where anybody can get involved or branch off.

## Core Conceit

First, we need that big tent-pole, the driving idea for the franchise.  This makes the exercise interesting to me, and I think would make the stories comprehensible to fans of the original.

Let me explain:  I mean that we could spend hours or weeks running down characters from works in the public domain with some sort of reincarnation-on-demand power, or time travel attached to telephones, protagonists with no known names, or whatever else.  But whenever someone does all that without getting to the heart of the property, you end up in the world of the all-style-no-substance knock-off.

To me, *Doctor Who* has---appropriately, maybe---two sometimes-related such "hearts," at least at its best.

- We live in a universe of horrors, which we often deal with through humor.
- The Doctor recruits disciples of a certain personality type, building their resilience through (seemingly accidental) lessons.

I exclude the earliest years, which work well as their own thing, but you can see that they designed the show as an educational vehicle, traveling through time to reconstruct ancient cultures for the audience.  That has value, but the property hasn't worked that way in over half a century.

### Ooh, Scary

What do I mean by horror?  Think about the major recurring villains.

If you squint, you can find various Lovecraft-inspired gods, disinterested in humanity as they try to make the universe suitable for their return.  You can find zombies, even if usually encased in metal or plastic.  You can find a vampire, vaguely continental and preying on people in the prime of their lives while carrying out revenge plots.  The universe teems with (pardon the expression) mad scientists, werewolves, doppelgängers, gremlins, trolls, killer toys, and more.  The Nazi-robots make regular appearances, sure, feeling like they buck the trend, but they have little Cthulhu-looking drivers.  And not only do we have many horror archetypes on display regularly, but you *don't* get many bug-eyed monsters with ray guns, planet-killers, cackling imperial forces, or any of the usual science fiction antagonist archetypes.  The main series especially likes to laugh off existential threats to humanity by making it all seem ridiculous and (especially) keeping the actual deaths far off-screen.

And I say that these ideas often connect, because maybe the type of humor that recurs most---especially in the original run---derives from the Doctor promising the companion(s) peace and quiet, but instead dumping them into a Hellscape where they'll need to fight for survival.  They then spend the rest of the arc pointing out that this dangerous spacial rift or unethical medical experiment would never have happened at Piccadilly Circus or the French Riviera or wherever British people spend their leisure time.

This, I think, becomes more explicit in the modern revival---after all, many seasons end with the companion (or some other side character) finding some inner strength to make some heroic gesture to save the Earth or universe, occasionally moving on after to build something---but you can see episodes throughout where the resolution of a story only works because of what a companion learned.

It seems worth contrasting this to how they largely bill the franchise, and how many groups try to draw inspiration from it, that you have a mildly anachronistic British person who bumbles around in space and time using his fancy widget, with nothing else to say.

## Rebuilding

To the extent that I think about getting the idea moving---at least for the purposes of this post, quite a lot---whether the official version produced at least in part by the BBC or a Free Culture work taking inspiration from the original, I see it as leaning into the former and treating the latter as largely irrelevant.

In fact, I say that you drop the fixed protagonist, for two reasons tied to the official work.  First, as many people like to point out, the companions traditionally do most of the protagonist work of growth and reaction, while the Doctor acts like a plot device.  That has changed a bit in the modern versions, and might account for its increased popularity outside of Britain, but the *Doctor Who* concept doesn't *need* to center the Doctor.

Second, consider that the Doctor, as a concept, inherently defies the idea of a singular protagonist by periodically changing.  The performer, the personality, and sometimes even the memories change.  Occasionally, a script will insert the idea that "regeneration" strongly resembles death and new life, which would cement the idea of having an entirely new character, more like the {% wiki Dalai Lama %}---minus needing to search for the next reincarnation---than James Bond.

## Archetypes

From here, I think that we go full Free Culture, because this at least strikes me as a fairly compelling concept independent of anything that the BBC might want to do.  After all, we have monster-hunters crisscrossing time and space, recruiting allies and successors.  To get a sense of the "shape" of the world, we can start with public domain characters that fit the profile.

- In the United States, the first five {% wiki Solomon Kane %} stories have lost copyright, with the sixth lapsing in the new year.
- From **{% wiki Dracula %}**, we might borrow Abraham Van Helsing and/or Mina Harker.
- We might as well also consider their off-brand counterparts from {% wiki Nosferatu %}, Professor Bulwer and Ellen (or Nina) Hutter.
- {% wiki Randolph Carter %} not only deals with (arguable) monsters, but does so on different worlds.
- {% wiki E. E. Smith %}'s Tedric stories feature a medieval metalworker who a time traveler teaches more advanced metallurgy, which he uses to fight a human-sacrificing cult among others, which certainly fits where we want to go, and lets us shift from monster-hunters *to* those time travelers.
- Tedric got his advanced knowledge from Skandos, someone deliberately altering history in hopes of averting the mass of problems developing into a world-destroying war, getting him into fights with alternate-timeline versions of himself who want other outcomes.
- H. G. Wells prototyped ideas that got deeper treatment in **The Time Machine** in *{% wiki The Chronic Argonauts %}*, but this has a named time traveler, Dr. Moses Nebogipfel.
- Andre Norton wrote several *{% wiki The Time Traders %}* novels, involving time travel to get an edge against the Soviets *and* a belligerent alien race.

We could almost certainly find more---**{% wiki El anacronópete %}**'s don Sindulfo García, *{% wiki The Clock That Went Backward %}*'s van der Werff family, and so forth---and I could probably dig up some Free Culture characters who'd fit, but I wanted to use this as to illustrate the types of characters who might serve as models, rather than to exhaustively detail a cast.  In fact, like Skandos "recruits" Tedric, one might expect the "famous" characters serve more as inspirations to the characters than protagonists, whereas you'd probably want original characters at the forefront, several "generations" of recruitment deep.

Anyway, now that we have a broad premise, they crisscross time and space through some means, able to read each other's records, which supplies some continuity.  As an organization, they spend some time quietly recruiting and training more of their kind of personality, poised to protect the universe from some invasion/rampage/whatever by some cosmic horror.

## Rites of Passage

As an homage to the regeneration cycles, maybe assistants only come into their ability to travel in some sort of death-and-rebirth ceremony.  This would allow them to end one part of their story by returning to their prior, mundane lives (if changed), but then get called back into the fray.  Rather than an inherent ability or a vehicle, I can see it as some token that "wakes up" when recruits find themselves in mortal danger, whisking them away to some Axis Mundi that serves as a common and timeless meeting-place.

I can think of three (and a half) tokens from public domain works from which we might draw some inspiration.

- For the half-item, I already mentioned *The Clock That Went Backwards*, and while we probably can't expect these people to cart around grandfather clocks everywhere, the idea of a time-traveling timepiece does appeal.
- Similarly, Lewis Carroll's **{% wiki Sylvie and Bruno %}** introduces the *Outlandish Watch*---as in a watch from the Outland---which serves as a time machine.  And the Outland could work as the setting for that aforementioned Axis Mundi.
- I mentioned Randolph Carter, and he talks about the *key to the gate of dreams*, not really meant as a physical object, but which allows him to transcend time and space.
- Further afield, E. E. Smith's *{% wiki Lensmen %}* books---about half of the series and side-stories in the public domain---includes a policing organization with a pseudo-magical badge of office powered by the life-force of the wielder and granting the wielder various power.

I don't want to suggest that this idea combines in any way with the *Lensmen* stories or fictional organization, but rather that we might draw inspiration from the Lens itself, especially the point that it doesn't work without the person it bonds to.  And/or we might imagine a secondary device, the multi-tool that translates, teaches, detects, analyzes, heals, records, and offers other features possible to authenticate from fiction in the public domain.

And that feels like it does most of the high-level work, a secret society dispersed throughout all societies, waiting for some signal to rise up and act in defense of the universe.  Most of the time, they can do what they please, though presumably have some guidelines.

## Thumbnails

I assume that can see the sorts of stories that this core enables.  You can have the meandering do-gooder with their apprentice(s), but you can also sit with former apprentices, who know what threatens the universe and assemble their own teams to fend off smaller intrusions in their area.  You can dig into the workings of the organization, or people without the ability to travel trying to find ways to coordinate across time to stop an antagonistic time traveler.  It can shift quickly between science fiction, fantasy, and horror.  They can get involved in almost any sort of problem, from the mundane to intergalactic scales.  It lacks a central narrative, but so does its inspiration.

### Rumors and Legends

We might also imagine mythology or folklore hiding in corners of the organization, about a first time-traveler---some Prime Guardian, for lack of a better term---who always returns after death with a different face, an ancient scientist or healer or scholar who started the program with his young students.  Some might believe that this person anonymously waits for the time and place that needs them most before revealing themselves, fated to endlessly sacrifice themselves to stop the universe's biggest threats.  I like the idea that this forms some growing cult that slowly and subtly tries to proselytize and recruit from among the ranks of the fresh recruits, possibly requiring a story to infiltrate their ranks to prevent some overt action that would endanger everybody.

Or maybe it requires pushing the faction into working, even as they dismiss a crisis because their role model hasn't appeared to them.  You could also have imposters playing on the legend to gain power.  And that doesn't even get to the factions that want to recruit or destroy them.

## Scaling Up

Another aspect might draw heavily on Fritz Leiber's {% wiki The_Big_Time_(novel)|**The Big Time** %}, which involves a war to adjust the time-stream to prevent the enemy from existing.  Adjusting this to work with the horror concepts works well, because you have a war against creatures striving to make the universe more horror-friendly and less amenable to our sort of life.

We might imagine the Outland as one of the first theaters of the war, its native life completely erased from history, and so used as a relatively safe zone of little other strategic value to either side.  Maybe the devastation created a "plateau in time"---something like the "Super-Sargasso Sea" that {% wiki Charles Fort %} talked about---a place that catches clumsy time-travelers, analogous to how almost anybody carelessly traveling through Earth history will somehow eventually manage to land during World War II.

And we could flesh out more of this, if we wanted.  **The Big Time**'s spiders and snakes could almost certainly map to mythologies and horror stories, including more Lovecraft.  That could further suggest the nature of the Outland, and so forth.

## Remind Me Who

Back to where we came in and stripping out the adornments, the solicitation period has ended, and the BBC has no reason to listen to me.  If someone put me in charge of *Doctor Who* for a few years, though, I would start with that recruitment concept.  You bring back companions (with performers still around), and give them a time machine of their own and the title of Doctor.

They don't need to take focus, but it expands the regeneration idea into something more interesting.

That said, having thought this through, I like my full version better.  In my head, I call them something like (not firmed up, yet) the Continuum Guardians.
