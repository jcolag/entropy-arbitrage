## The Quest for Mittens

Once upon a time, there were three little witches who lost their mittens, so they began to cry:  "Oh, mother dear, we sadly fear, bubble, bubble, toil, and trouble, our mittens we have lost."  And so they went out into the woods to find them.

They never found their mittens, though, and feeling too proud to go home empty-handed, decided to build homes for themselves in the woods, so that they could seek their fortunes, especially to buy yarn and hire somebody to knit new mittens.  The scraggly witch went to a candy shop and said to the proprietor:

"Please, my good confectioner, give me that candy to build me a house."

Which the little confectioner did, and the scraggly witch built her house with it.

The bedraggled witch went to a bakery and said to the baker:

"Please, my good baker, give me that gingerbread to build me a house."

Which the little baker did, and the bedraggled witch built her house with it.

The uncouth witch met a construction crew and said to the mason:

"Please, my good mason, give me some bricks to build me a house," for she suspected that food was for eating, not for building.

Which the little mason did, and the uncouth witch built her house with it.

The following winter, the scraggly witch made herself a bowl of porridge for breakfast.  The porridge was too hot, so she decided to go for a walk in the woods while it cooled.  The witch was unaware that a mother sent her three children out into the woods to forage for food for the family, and so a hungry Hänsel, Gretel, and Kunegunde went out with their baskets to collect food.  But imagine their surprise when they came upon a house made entirely of candy.

They immediately began to nibble on the house.

"These gutters are too sticky, Hänsel," Gretel said.

"These shingles are too sweet, Kunegunde," said Hänsel, rubbing his stomach.

"This nut-filled doorknob is just right," Kunegunde declared, and the door opened.  "Also, I hate it when people call me Kunegunde.  Can you call me Goldilocks?"

"It seems weird since you have brown hair," Hänsel observed, "but it seems fair.  Only Mother refers to Gretel as Margarethe or me as Johannes."

"That works for me, Goldilocks," Gretel added.

Inside, the children saw the scraggly witch's breakfast waiting.

"This porridge is too hot," Gretel said.

"This porridge is also only porridge, and also not candy," Hänsel replied.

"This table is just right," Goldilocks declared, and soon the children had eaten the entire house and wandered off, leaving nothing but a steaming bowl of porridge lying on the ground.

The scraggly witch returned home, but there was no home to return to.  "By all the hairs on my chinny-chin-chin, who could have done such a thing," she asked herself as she ate her now-cooled ground-porridge.  "My sister, the bedraggled witch might know," and so she rushed off to visit the gingerbread house.  While she walked, she happened across a cat, who began following her as she sang.

When the scraggly witch reached the gingerbread house, the bedraggled witch was on her way out.  "I just made myself some porridge, but it is too hot, so I am going for a walk," she told her sister.  "You can join me."

"Thank you, sister," the scraggly witch replied, and they went for a walk---the cat following along---and talked about her missing candy house.

While they were away, though, the smell of the gingerbread house attracted the somehow-still-hungry children.

"Fee, fie, fo, fum, I smell a thing that could be yum," Goldilocks said as they all approached to investigate, even if Gretel found the rhyme a bit forced.

"We little pigs must get in," said Hänsel.

"These shingles have too much icing on them," Gretel said, as she bit into one.

"These shutters have too little icing on them," Hänsel replied.

"These windows are just right, unless you wanted to look through them," Goldilocks insisted as they made their way inside.

Inside, the saw the scraggly witch's breakfast, waiting.

"This porridge is too cold," Gretel said.

"This porridge is too bland, especially with gingerbread around," Hänsel replied.

"These chairs are just right," Goldilocks indicated, and soon the children had eaten the entire house and moved on, leaving only the bowl of cold porridge sitting on a stump and a man-shaped scrap of a shingle that ran away from this mess of a tale as fast as it could.

Meanwhile, without any idea of what could have happened to the candy house, the scraggly witch and the bedraggled witch began talking about their mitten-less lives alone, until the cat began to play with a pig, which also joined them.  But when they returned home, there was no gingerbread house remaining.

"By all the hairs on my chinny-chin-chin, who could have done such a thing," the bedraggled witch asked the scraggly witch as she ate her perfectly cold stump-porridge.  "Our sister, the uncouth witch should know," and so they rushed off to visit the brick house.  While they walked, a fox joined them.

When the scraggly witch and the bedraggled witch reached the brick house with their new animal companions, the uncouth witch was on her way out.  "I just made some porridge, but it is just right, so I am going for a walk until it is too cold or too warm," she told her sisters.  "I don't much care which.  You may join me."

"Thank you, sister," the scraggly witch and bedraggled witch replied, and they went for a walk---followed by the cat, the pig, and the fox---and they talked about the missing candy house and the missing gingerbread house.

"I suspected that something like this might soon happen, sisters," the uncouth witch said.  "The omens foretold it.  Also, candy and gingerbread are for eating, so surely, someone has eaten your houses."

"But they left the porridge.  Who would do such a thing," the bedraggled witch asked.

"Perhaps someone who does not like porridge, or someone with a stomach full of candy and gingerbread.  Now, let's go back to my house and decide what to do," the uncouth witch said, as she picked up a rooster walking through the woods.

It was getting dark, by now, so the witches let the animals graze in the brick house's yard while they went inside to sleep, making a pot of stew for morning, since porridge seemed too finicky, temperature-wise.  The scraggly witch pulled out the recipe.

> Double, double toil and trouble;  
> Fire burn and caldron bubble.  
> Fillet of a fenny snake,  
> In the caldron boil and bake;  
> Eye of newt and toe of frog,  
> Wool of bat and tongue of dog,  
> Adder's fork and blind-worm's sting,  
> Lizard's leg and howlet's wing,  
> For a charm of powerful trouble,  
> Like a hell-broth boil and bubble.
>
> Double, double toil and trouble;  
> Fire burn and caldron bubble.  
> Cool it with a baboon's blood,  
> Then the charm is firm and good.

And they left the pot of stew in the fire's embers for morning.

It might not have proven a good night's sleep, for Gretel, Goldilocks, and Hänsel appeared in the bushes.

"It is nearly night, and we have still found no food for Mother," Gretel said.

"But our bellies are full," Hänsel pointed out, satisfied.

"My brother and sister, I spy another house ahead.  Perhaps it is as tasty as the others.  I smell something."

"It might be meat," Hänsel shouted.  "A meat house would make a perfect dessert, and could feed Mother."

They ran ahead and immediately began biting at the brick house.

"These bricks are too hard," Gretel complained, her teeth aching from gnawing the wall.

"These doorknobs are made of metal," Hänsel agreed, his teeth aching from trying to bite through brass fixtures, or maybe his cavities.

"The windows are glass," Goldilocks grumped.  "But we can look through them, and oh, do look through them.  I spy a pot of delicious stew."

"But look further.  In that corner, I also spy three little witches sleeping," Gretel announced.

"They must take pity on us and our mother, for we are but adorable urchins," Hänsel said.  He began pounding on the doors.  "Little witches, little witches, let us come in!"

"Not by the hairs of our chinny-chin-chins," the woken witches replied.  "It's late.  Go home.  We need our sleep."

"We cannot go home without some of your stew for our mother," Goldilocks said.

"I recognize you," the scraggly witch said.  "Does your mother not have great big eyes?"

"The better to see you with," Gretel responded.

"And does your mother not have great big ears," the bedraggled witch asked.

"The better to hear you with," Hänsel responded.  "And you don't hear us asking about the hair on your chinny-chin-chins, do you?"

"And does your mother not have great big teeth," the uncouth witch asked.

"The better to eat your stew," Goldilocks screamed, perhaps not understanding why people make stew, as she tried to break through the brick wall.  The wall chose not to cooperate.

"Well, we don't like your mother, and we already feel quite tired after a day of losing my sisters' homes.  Come back in the morning, and we'll think about it," the uncouth witch said.  "Or make your own stew."

"Bah, surely we can get that stew without waiting," Gretel muttered to nobody in particular.

"We should wait until they're asleep," Hänsel said.

"What if we climb down the chimney after they have fallen asleep," Goldilocks asked.

The children agreed and withdrew to the woods to wait in the dark shadows of the forest.

"Surely, the witches must be asleep by now," Hänsel suggested, wondering if perhaps hiding made their criminal enterprise more difficult.

"Let's check," Gretel agreed.  They approached the brick house again, feeling their way ahead of them.  When they reached the house, they peered into the window to be sure the three little witches had fallen sound asleep.

"Good," said Goldilocks.  "We should be able to drop down and take their cauldron of stew right out the front door before they even notice."

However, the children didn't realize that the animals noticed them.  Wanting to protect their new bipedal companions, they attacked as the children reached the roof.  The cat scratched Gretel with her claws.  The fox nipped at Hänsel's foot.  The pig kicked Goldilocks with his hooves.  And the rooster leapt to the chimney to crow, scaring the children in addition to the pain, possibly adding insult to injury, though nobody took the opportunity to translate what the rooster crowed.

Running way, Gretel shouted "it must have been the witches, with their long fingernails.  They must have planned to cook us into that stew all along, now that their houses fattened us up."

Following close behind with a limp, Hänsel corrected her.  "No, this could have only been the work of a masked vigilante witch with a knife.  She must have planned to kidnap us and hold us for ransom, and fattening us up on houses only served to lure us farther from home."

Finally, Goldilocks caught up, a bit dizzy from her ordeal, adding, "you both have it wrong.  Only an ogre with a giant club could have done this, summoned by the witches.  He must have planned to pound us into the ground."

Gretel worried, "the judge that screamed that he had caught us, do you think he will identify and arrest us?  And did the witches summon him, too?"

Biting his fingernails, Hänsel said, "I don't know, especially given the traditional animosity between witches and government institutions, but I can tell you that I refuse to eat another house as long as I live."

"Maybe we should bring nuts and berries home to mother," Goldilocks concluded, and so they started home.  "The witches had a point about possibly making our own stew, too."

"Maybe," Hänsel sulked, "but that seems too much like work.  Impish children such as ourselves tend not to enjoy work."

Gretel looked up, suggesting "in the distance, I see a giant stalk climbing to the clouds.  Perhaps we could bring mother some giant beans instead of nuts and berries."  The other children agreed that the plan sounded legitimate, and set out for the beanstalk instead of home, because they agreed that nothing bad could possibly happen there.

"Surely not relevant," Hänsel mentioned, "but I recently learned that Hans equates to the English name John, so Hänsel would equate to Johnny or Jack."  He stuck a feather in his cap to emphasize his point, hoping that it looked like a macaroni wig.

"Fascinating," said Goldilocks, though her tone implied that she might have found it otherwise.

Back at the house, the rooster's crow woke the witches, who found the cat, the fox, the pig, and the rooster playing music in the uncouth witch's brick shed.

"I think that I foresee the solution to all our problems," the scraggly witch said.

The witches walked their new animal friends to Bremen, where everybody agreed that the animals played terrible music, but animals playing any music still seemed at least mildly interesting.  And by convincing the animals to play for several days, they earned enough money to buy eight pairs of mittens:  One for the scraggly witch, one for the bedraggled witch, one for the uncouth witch, one for the cat, one for the pig, one for the fox, one for the rooster, and one extra pair, plus one cabbage.

After successfully crossing a river in small boats without anybody tempted to eat anybody else---the system they devised quietly skipped for brevity---and avoiding a crowd near someplace called St. Ives (the uncouth witch used to date one of them, and it didn't end well), the proud little witches returned home, singing "oh, mother dear, see here, see here, our mittens we have found and/or replaced."  And they gave the extra pair of mittens to their mother, who wore them always, but especially when feeding the cat, the pig, the fox, and the rooster, and grumping about how her little witches never even visited her, the surplus mittens, or their weird musical pets anymore.

For complete closure, the children returned home, exhausted and with strained backs and aching teeth, hauling one giant bean and one golden egg back for their mother.  The egg dented instead of cracking and seemed inedible, though, so the mother threw it away and focused on cooking the bean, trying to ignore the animal-like cries about the sky falling from out the window.  "Now, if I can only find where that dish and spoon run off to," she considered aloud, "this would be just right."

The end.

## Commentary

I assume that we all have enough familiarity with Western fairy tales and nursery rhymes[^VrKoFe] that you don't need me to walk you through the references.  Instead, I'll point out that---especially looking at the file history---I seem to have planned to center this on the recurring number three in fairy tales, then abandoned it after a while.

[^VrKoFe]:  OK, and one of Shakespeare's plays and a mediocre-at-best song from the American Revolution, but you probably recognize those, too...

Part of me wonders if I originally planned to post it in celebration of the blog's third anniversary.  That would fit, especially with a provisional title of *The Rule of Three*, except that "calling my shot" like that almost two years in advance, especially with the blog still unproven in my eyes, doesn't sound like me at all.  And when I added *Town Musicians of Bremen*, with some animals adjusted to fit the other stories, I no longer needed to adhere to the triads.

Oh, and yes, [Hans](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Hans#German), a shortened nickname for Johannes, made it to English as *John*, most likely via the Dutch *Jan*.  [Hänsel](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Hansel), a diminutive of Hans and Johannes then, maps to Johnny or [Jack](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Jack).  And through the same routes, the name *may* have also evolved into [Yankee](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Yankee#English).  I checked when I added the beanstalk and wondered if Hans might ask his sisters to call him Jack, an idea that got reintroduced when I realized that one of the three kids doesn't have an actual name.

Actually, I should probably point out one inspiration that might veer too far into the obscure, these days, especially since I only stumbled into it accidentally:  Kunegunde may hate her name for reasons similar to Voltaire finding it funny to name **Candide**'s love interest the Gallicized {% wiki Cunégonde %}, coinciding with the reason that *you* might have snorted a bit when you first saw the name.

Speaking of whom, I almost renamed Goldilocks in such a way that the kids' initials would've spelled something out or some other progression.  Initially, I went with Isentrud for G-H-I or Anna---I know, not obscure enough---for HAG, until realizing that neither Hänsel nor Gretel probably represents their given names.  Once I realized that we actually had Johannes and Margarethe, we don't have a valid word other than JAM to work with, and so I went for J-K-L-M with a letter missing.  Without a need to make everything a triad, does all this mean that they have a brother Lamprecht who we never see to fill the progression of Johannes, Kunegunde, and Margarethe?  Sure.  Why not?  I spent more time looking for appropriate period names than I did on world-building, except for the brief question of whether I could get away ending this with the story of a witchy lady, bringing up three very witchy girls, all of them had knotted hair like their mother, the youngest one all gnarled.  And you also have the story of a woman named Sophie, busy with three kids of her own...

OK, no, I didn't *really* consider that until writing this background.  Though I don't know, maybe it'll inspire a sequel where the two mothers have married each other and weird cousin Osprant comes to stay with the blended family, to the chagrin of their maid, Alusch.
