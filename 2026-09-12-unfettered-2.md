## Fantastique Unfettered #2 (Unless)

Before we get into this, I do want to reiterate that---as far as I can find---the project only exists as the four "dead tree" issues of the magazine.  To read along, you'll need to have your favorite book store grab a copy.

The first issue (page 66) provides us with a mission statement for the overall project.

> Fantastique Unfettered, a Periodical of Liberated Literature exists to provide well written, compellingly readable, original stories of fantasist fiction to readers. We will publish both established and new writers alike, and intend no certain genre as transcendent over another, though Science Fiction should be sent to our sister publication, M-Brane SF. Some stories might look like SF or Fantasy depending on one's perspective (stories that follow in the tradition of Philip K. Dick, for instance). We are interested in such tales. There are also specific areas of overlap that are outlined in our Writers Guidelines.
>
> Fantastique Unfettered will continually seek ways to generate revenue with the end goal of paying creative talent the best rate possible.
>
> Fantastique Unfettered is not a magazine. It takes many forms. Those forms include the near-anachronistic magazine. What forms will follow, we look forward to discovering.
>
> Fantastique Unfettered is guided by a transcendent purpose and the concept that the Periodical, whatever its Form, is at heart an idea factory.

For reference, Christopher Fletcher's [M-Brane SF](http://www.mbranesf.com/) seems to stand alone as the only part of this overall project that still has an Internet presence, though I don't see any evidence that it released any of its work under a Creative Commons license.  It also holds out hope for a relaunch in 2013.

I might also point you to the piece that editor Brandon H. Bell---whose name you might recall from his editorial work on [**The Aether Age:  Helios**]({% post_url 2024-07-20-helios-1 %}) that we talked about a couple of years ago---[wrote for Question Copyright](https://jcolag.codeberg.page/qco/208-small-press.html) around when these magazine issues came out.  It actually comes from the second issue of the magazine, which we'll talk about next week.

> Our alignment cover of Fantastique Unfettered, Issue 1is not indie against corporate, small against large, or fan against pro. Those are foolish stances. Our alignment is one against obscurity, expressed via a pragmatism that acknowledges money may or may not follow our good karma. We certainly hope it does: our goal, after providing quality fiction to our readers, is to pay writers professional rates.
>

>
> I’m not an ideologue; I’ve stated in blog posts that I don’t know how well CC-BY-SA scales, and for the Stephen Kings of the world, traditional copyright may be the only reasonable default for their work. Creative Commons is a tool, in a toolbox that includes traditional copyright, and I have no prohibition against the latter (though even if I reach “rock-star” level, I would ensure my work returns to the culture at some point).
>

> Writers deserve to be paid for their work, and we hope that you, dear reader, will take an active interest in supporting short fiction. If not _FU_, then some other venue. As a writer, I hope to someday make loads of cash at my craft and to have people bemoan my place on the NYT list. _That hack_, they’ll complain as I laugh my way to the bank. (Yeah, it’s a _writer thing_.) So, a final reminder that our use of Creative Commons licensing is not purely ideological or a revolt against traditional publishing.

>
> We have come not to praise Caesar, but to bury him.

As a tangent, I find it funny to see it in the context of Question Copyright, because they include a big "we don't agree with everything here" disclaimer.  I can't help wonder if that writer's bigger concern comes from the suggestion that copyright might have some validity, since they don't mean "question" in the sense of exploring to find the good and bad parts.  Alternatively, because they fully believed that since "information wants to be free," that art shouldn't cost anything except through the [cost of manufacturing and distributing physical media](https://questioncopyright.org/understanding_free_content.html), so the idea that writers deserve payment for their work might have struck them as heretical.

For this issue, we'll specifically read *Every Mother's Child* by Therese Arkenberg, *The Dollhouse* by Magen Toole, *Glorious Madness* by Jude-Marie Green, and *Then Cried Arthur* by Peter Chiykowski.  You might want to note that this issue includes a "final"---I guess, until somebody writes more---**Aether Age** story, *The Kemetian Husesen Craze*, which I won't engage with, since that'd continue those other posts.

## What Works Well?

While I didn't really care as much for the stories that I read, this time through, I did need to respect the quality of the writing.  I never had a moment where I had to stop and try to reconstruct the situation that the story had gotten itself into.  While I didn't read everything, I'd call *The Dollhouse* and *Glorious Madness* the best of this batch, both having clever twists on more typical stories.

I should also note that this issue gets more experimental.  It includes some (fairly oddball) "letters to the editor," largely from authors---including authors who had stories in the first issue---talking specifically about their adjustment to (what I now call) Free Culture licenses.  It also includes reviews for contemporary issues of a different (and non-Free Culture) literary magazine and interview with a poet associated with it.  And it includes multiple essays from people involved with production.  I don't know if anybody reading this today will *like* the experiment or find it useful, but we should appreciate the attempt.

The poem (*Then Cried Arthur*)...I'd call it approachable, if nothing else, and it has ties to a storytelling tradition that most readers will feel comfortable with.

And since I brought up the relative numerical success, I should mention that this issue gives us ten stories by ten (more) authors, one who contributed a story to **The Aether Age** related to the one here.  We also have one poem, the non-fiction bits, and art, all released under a Free Culture license and seemingly compensated for their work.

## What Works...Less Well?

{% include lesswell.md %}

This almost certainly comes down to taste, but I have to admit that *Every Mother's Child* felt woefully out of place, here, with what we might charitably call its "traditionalist" fantasy world.  Other than acknowledging the existence of---and it presents this as a surprise, so I apologize if anybody feels that I spoiled the story---gay men, it almost feels like a one-shot D&D adventure, complete with occupations like "swordsman" (or woman, but their work gets more complicated when they have babies {% emoji eyeroll %}) and "magic user."  Likewise, because of the need to escape from whatever-the-pit-does, don't bother caring about all the killing on the way out.  Oddly, *Glorious Madness* follows suit, though it has an actual justification.

## Six Years Hence:  Book Dash

It pleases me to tell everybody that [Book Dash]({% post_url 2020-09-12-bookdash %}) continues to plug along.  Every few months, I get an e-mail announcing another hackathon to get people together and announce more books.  Every year, they announce a new print run, and I wonder if I could find enough takers to share a bulk order like that---if I remember correctly, the only sell units of one hundred copies of each book in that run---and whether they'd ship to the United States.

At a quick glance, newer books that look interesting include [**Mom's Hands**](https://bookdash.org/books/moms-hands-sasl/) teaching ASL and [**Graca's Dream**](https://bookdash.org/books/gracas-dream/) about Mozambican independence, and more.  The organization has also branched into audio and video, for many books.
