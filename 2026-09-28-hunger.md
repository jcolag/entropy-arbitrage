## Recipes

My makeshift incubator finally got a decent test, and I'd call it successful, because I now have tempeh with no soy[^spJIcx].

[^spJIcx]:  I actually like most soy products, but it doesn't particularly agree with me.  Don't take my choice to suggest any endorsement of the often-racist conspiracy theories telling you not to eat soy.  If it works for you, so much the better.

{% cook 1|Sunflower Seed Tempeh %}
Boil @sunflower seeds{2%cup} until softened, at least ~{1%hour}.  Let cool to around 90°F.

Mix the softened seeds with @white vinegar{2%Tbsp}, then mix in @tempeh spores{1%tsp}.  Spread in container(s) less than one inch thick.

Incubate at (roughly) 90°F for ~{24–48%hours}, checking after the first 12 hours to make sure that the tempeh fungus has started growing.  Consider it done when it forms a single mass, but before too many black/gray patches appear.
{% endcook %}

For the last bit first, I gather that the dark patches come from the mold beginning to sporulate.

Instead of boiling the seeds on the stove-top, I actually dumped them into the rice cooker to run through the "oatmeal" cycle (why does a rice cooker have an oatmeal setting...?) and keep it warm overnight, since that required far less attention and probably not much more energy.

Every recipe---if you can call that a recipe---that I saw online talks about using a sealable sandwich bag as a container, ventilated with pin-picks to aerate it while protecting it from the outside.  Since I doubt that my oven gets much dust and the like building up in it, I opted for bowls without much curvature along the base, and left them uncovered; I could probably throw a sheet of parchment paper over the tops, if the open-air approach ends up seeming like an issue.  Apparently, Indonesians use banana leaves.  The thin shape makes sure that it doesn't cut off any of the block from air.

It lasts a few days raw, refrigerated.  Steamed for twenty minutes, it'll last for a couple of weeks.

The results?

![One of the (three) discs of tempeh, about four inches in diameter](/blog/assets/first-tempeh.png "Acceptable, I think...")

It didn't become as solid as I expected, so it has a crumbling texture rather than something to slice or fry.  I don't know if I can steam this without most falling in the water, as I handle it.  However, I ate about half of this one "raw" with breakfast[^SNb4zB], and it tasted fine, a bit mild-to-bland, if anything.

[^SNb4zB]:  Making something that might go with the tempeh, I made a bowl with black beans, quinoa, some leftover roasted butternut squash, and the like.

Next time that I do this, I *probably* want to deliberately pack the seeds-and-spores mixture more tightly into the container(s), to give the mycelia less work pulling together.  Corroborating that idea, I got some *crisp* spots around the edges, where the mycelia would've gotten a good grip off the nearby seeds, and the piece in the glass bowl, a couple of inches further from the light also has a nice solid (but small) white patch covering some seeds along the glass.  Likewise, that would help explain the abject failure of my first batch a few weeks ago:  Yes, temperatures had started to drop by that time, but I also didn't do more than shovel the softened seeds into a sandwich bag and ventilate it, so only ended up with soft seeds (with a couple of white spots) in a bag.

## Mini-Server, Part 34

While I don't have anything *new* to talk about, I should mention that this weekend and the storm coming up the East Coast provided a decent test of the servers.  While I didn't need to replace any desktop software, Sunday morning with no Internet connection made sure that I worked locally.

I didn't use any of them for long, because I mostly listened to podcasts while I worked instead of consuming media, but [Audio Book Shelf](https://audiobookshelf.org/), [Jellyfin](https://jellyfin.org/), and [Kavita](https://www.kavitareader.com/) all worked perfectly for media.  As you can guess, I couldn't use Jellyfin to stream live television, with no access to the stream sources, but even the *Live TV* part of the application knew what I could watch, because it still had the Electronic Program Guide (EPG) information.

[Fresh RSS](https://freshrss.org/) didn't have access to new posts anywhere, but had the previous night's reading ready, and the API worked for my usual weekend scripts.  Likewise with [Shiori](https://github.com/go-shiori/shiori) for my bookmarks.

[Radicale](https://radicale.org/v3.html) and [Inf Cloud](https://inf-it.com/open-source/clients/infcloud/) worked fine as a calendar.  Similarly, [Cryptpad](https://cryptpad.fr/) and [Forgejo](https://forgejo.org/) also worked fine, though I didn't need much out of either of them.

Only [Snappy Mail](https://snappymail.eu/) fell apart, understandably since it really only connects to mail servers and present the results cleanly.  It doesn't store anything locally, so it announces a bad connection then throws a not-found error on reload.  I don't think that anybody would call it an elegant failure, but I don't know that gracefulness would've helped.

Related to the outage, if not useful to the post, they sent me the following message every two hours *since* restoring my connection.

> We’re still working to resolve the outage. We apologize for the delay.

And yet, somehow, they managed to get me a reminder to pay the bill, too...

## Kabang!

{% codeberg jcolag/kabang %}

As mentioned [last week]({% post_url 2026-09-21-yom-kippur %}), replacing the old AI-generated header image for my [**Kabang!** post]({% post_url 2023-08-13-kabang %}) shortly before discovering that [TIC-80](https://tic80.com/) had released a new version---not to mention finally releasing [my old novel on Itch](https://jcolag.itch.io/) and discovering that it has TIC-80 support built in---convinced me to revisit the project for more polish.

First, it now has a trimmed-down version of the new (hypothetical) arcade cabinet art as a label for their simulated cartridges.  Like some other "fantasy computers," the TIC-80 now supports game files ("cartridges") embedded in PNG-format images.  Because modern operating systems now show previews of images in folders, this means that you can have a collection of games that looks vaguely like a physical collection.

Next, in addition to the title screen with instructions to start the game, it also now has a splash screen, open for a second and a half, showing my logo.  That screen also debuts my new "sound logo," a five-note jingle that I wanted in my back-pocket for exactly projects like this.  See below for more on that.

After that, the music tempo increases slightly as each game progresses, adding some (minor) tension as the player's actions become more unwieldy.  And speaking of the music, I have finally replaced the bland AI-generated music that I used as a placeholder.  You'll now hear it play an approximation of [RottenMage SpaceJacked JINGLE 01](https://freemusicarchive.org/music/sawsquarenoise/RottenMage_SpaceJacked/15_1678/) by [sawsquarenoise](https://freemusicarchive.org/music/sawsquarenoise/), which only runs for a few seconds (and so can fit inside sixty-four beats) and loops somewhat better than the generated garbage. Anyway, I also updated the *About* screen to credit the music and adjusted some formatting.

I believe that wraps up the project, "only" three years later, since I can't think of anything else to change that wouldn't fundamentally change the game.

### Digression on the Sound Logo

Not really relevant---though I did spend a surprising amount of time deciding what I wanted, here---but it does seem worth noting that I have liked the idea of a *sound logo*, often referred to as an "earcon" as a pun on *icon*, starting from when the Wikimedia Foundation announced the [winner of their contest](https://wikimediafoundation.org/news/2023/03/28/wikipedias-new-sound-logo-winner/) for one, then primarily for use with audio assistants.  I didn't see a use for me at the time, but it sat in the back of my mind as a fun kind of project, more than decades of hearing the NBC chimes and equivalent audio trademarks.

Over the summer, the idea for the melody struck me:  While I don't know from composing music, I *have* sat on a secret weapon for decades, especially for short fanfares like this.  In 1827, Jean-François Sudre released one of the first attempts at a universal language, {% wiki Solresol %}.  Unlike traditional languages (natural or constructed), Solresol doesn't like to make assumptions about the ability to pronounce sounds.  Instead of complicated pronunciation rules, Sudre decided to use seven well-known syllables, and they may have a variety of representations.  Primarily, though, the syllables map to musical notes in one octave, as in "do re me fa sol la ti/si."

I find this idea remarkably clever, because using seven notes already provides a bunch of alternative representations.  If you can't speak the seven "solfège" syllables, then you could use the notes by whatever means you have available.  Or you could use the note letters (C--G, A, B).  Or you have numbers one through seven[^i7YcWi].  If you look further, you can find hand signals, which someone (maybe Sudre) adapted into characters, which we can approximate in Unicode `❍ | ◠ \ — c /`.  Music also has Braille and dozens of other representations.  And if you don't like any of those, we all remember rainbows having seven colors, which map as well.  Nice, right?  In almost any medium, it takes little to no context to start communicating, regardless of technical capacity.  It feels a bit silly if you think about using it to tra-la-la your way through a conversation, but it starts with a fairly clever conceit.

[^i7YcWi]:  Actual numerical music notation uses one through eleven, to account for sharp and flat notes in the scale, but you get the general idea.

Anyway, I've had a scan of Sudre's original book forever, though I've never given it as much attention as I'd like.  Looking through it, I didn't care for their approach to transliteration, as far too cumbersome.  For example, my name alone would look/sound like **la**-sol-**re** **fa**-sol-la-la-do-**la**-fa-sol-fa-do, where the bolded notes get emphasized in some way that the book opts not to describe; it also sounds miserable in a musical sense.

Sticking with my name, though, as a type of signature, the next step seemed like working through etymology.  *Colagioia* doesn't have enough representation to look up a meaning directly, but multiple independent people who have studied Italian have volunteered that it means "with joy."  I don't know if I *believe* that, because it sounds awfully simplistic, but [Gioia](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Gioia#Italian) does mean "joy," and a contraction of [con](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/con#Italian) and [la](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/la#Italian)/[li](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/li#Italian) doesn't seem impossible; plus, it doesn't *not* suit my usual aims[^PqJb21].  And it makes a heck of a lot more sense than assuming a diminutive of *Nicola* (the equivalent of Nicholas) got attached to many Italian surnames, whereas this doesn't seem to have happened with *any* other name.

[^PqJb21]:  Contrast all that with *John*, a transliterated and truncated version of Hebrew יְהוֹחָנָן (*Yahohanan*), meaning "God is gracious."  That feels cumbersome, *gracious* doesn't have a convenient Solresol equivalent (yet?), and while I don't have an objection to religion, centering it definitely doesn't fit my persona.

And that, I can pull right out of the dictionary.  *With* gives us fa-re.  *Joy* gives us sol-ti-re.  I played with the rhythm and volume of each note to approximate a quasi-authentic pronunciation of the name (Co-la-gi-o-ia; I don't pronounce it that way), and I mostly like the result.

**Question** for anybody who has read this far down:  Does a rhythm-based language exist?  This happened to work out well for me by using a natural/existing rhythm, but it doesn't generalize.  If I wanted to do this for a company name or similar, it probably wouldn't have the right number of syllables to match the notes.  Morse code sounds like a valid answer, but it only has two note-lengths, and provides too long a sequence.

### Making Music

Back to that detour that I mentioned on the music:  As you might have noticed if you clicked through, the creator of the track that I used only uploaded an audio recording.  That doesn't help when working with the TIC-80, since it only has a stripped-down synthesizer, a {% wiki music tracker %} that allows for listing the notes in order.

To get music pre-recorded as audio into an instrumental-only format, one needs to either handle it by ear---not much of an option for me, if I wanted the work done anytime this year---or some tool to pick out the frequencies and estimate the notes played.  In the past, we've seen that sort of tool as more hypothetical than off-the-shelf, because no real musical instrument plays clean sine waves, complicating what notes you actually hear.

These days, things have gotten better---probably applying neural networks in reasonable (non-generative) ways, though I don't know---producing a handful of tools and libraries that have a decent chance of getting it to work.  In my case, I handed the work off to [Muse Score](https://musescore.org/en), which hands it off to their servers.  That probably doesn't make this a fully Free Software solution, but it *did* produce sheet music.

Now, I "read music" a lot like I read Greek or Russian beyond a few words---identifying each letter and trying to sound it out, hoping that I recognize the result, like a toddler---but I *can* manage the whole E-G-B-D-F deal and octaves.  And from there, I  can plug the song into the tracker, if slower than someone else might.  And once that process wrapped up, probably with a couple of errors between the automated transcription and my own ineptness, it sounded fine if uninteresting.  Changing the "instrument" that plays on the main melody and bass pushed it more in a science fiction direction, and what you can hear now.

## KDE for People

{% codeberg cyrneko/kde-for-people %}

After the [Debian](https://www.debian.org/vote/2026/vote_002) LLM policy fiasco---on top of similar decisions for the [Linux kernel](https://lore.kernel.org/linux-media/CAHk-=wi4zC+Ze8e+p3tMv8TtG_80KzsZ1syL9anBtmEh5Z40vg@mail.gmail.com/), [systemd](https://github.com/systemd/systemd/blob/main/AGENTS.md), and [Fedora](https://docs.fedoraproject.org/en-US/council/policy/ai-contribution-policy/)---allowing the Grok Jocks to undermine the project and Software Freedom in general, I think that we all knew that it wouldn't stop there.

This week, KDE started its own "debate" on the issue, while [making unclear moderation moves](https://raphus.social/@MaddieM4/117320340004129375)[^ufxREb] about authoritarians versus people outing the authoritarians and maybe treating Omarchy as yet another distribution that needs more polish instead of an openly fascist project.  When scrutiny on their budding LLM policy grew, they appear to have taken it private.

[^ufxREb]:  You might notice, as you read the official KDE response, that they never state *which* person got permanently banned for violating their Code of Conduct, while also insisting that they need a complete lack of transparency when following their rules.

When the [KDE for People](https://kdeforpeople.com/) link slid across my Mastodon feed, it seemed worth adding my name to it, as I have tried to do with similar causes.  KDE may choose to ignore the statement or take everything private until LLM contributions pile up, and they declare that they can't reasonably enforce anything, but at least this attempts to sway them to do the right thing.

I also note the [insider perspective {% cc %}](https://pointieststick.com/2026/09/23/kde-and-ai-and-you-and-me/), here, that KDE had a plan to *compromise*---as in, accept LLM-generated "contributions," but make some vague claim about personal responsibility---but dirty non-KDE developers dare to have *opinions* on what should go into critical software, and we derailed the process...somehow.

Oh, and for the people who don't spend much time with UNIX-like terminology, KDE creates one or more (depending on how you count) "desktop environments," the part of the operating system that you can actually see, and which contains all your applications.  You might remember I went into somewhat more (but still oversimplified) detail in another post, but for a typical Linux-based operating system you can think of it as the Linux kernel managing access to your hardware, the GNU tools and libraries managing the kernel, something like Debian or Fedora managing GNU, and the desktop environment letting you manage Debian/Fedora/whatever.

I tend to use [GNOME](https://www.gnome.org/) (the competition) as the desktop environment on my systems, but I still want KDE to succeed and get things right.  And I also do occasionally install KDE on a machine.

By the way, speaking of GNOME, one of the developers [proposed an LLM policy](https://blogs.gnome.org/alatiera/2026/09/23/the-gnome-llm-policy-that-i-want/) that they hope to see, and I agree that large projects should use it:  You can't use LLMs to make anything under the GNOME banner, and the project might ban you for trying to smuggle LLM-generated code into a project.
