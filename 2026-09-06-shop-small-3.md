When I started this "series" of posts, I mentioned that for all the time that I spend *implying* that you should probably walk away from big companies (technology-wise and not) for independent and especially Free Culture counterparts, I haven't laid out the whys and hows.  From there, I introduced the idea of [distancing ourselves from large companies]({% post_url 2026-07-19-shop-small-1 %}), which all increasingly seem to want to make our lives miserable, with links to a tiny fraction of the evidence for that perspective.

![Young witch Pepper falling, with her cat Carrot on her back and broom in hand, as she frantically reads a user manual](/blog/assets/user-manual-revoy.png "OK, sometimes things don't quite go right...")

I explained that this necessary distance does *you* good, not necessarily the world unless it becomes a huge movement.  That came with my own example, looking to move off the Roku platform to watch media on my television.  One of the few devices running proprietary software---technically still built on the Linux kernel, but with mostly proprietary software on top of it, and no convenient ways of accessing any internals---now not only embraces surveillance-based advertising, but now reports to a massive company with its own film studio, right-wing propaganda network, gambling contracts, and now channels of AI garbage that it'll want to connect and push.

Then, I started talking about the process of actually getting away from these abusive companies, specifically outlining the requirements.  Whatever you want to replace, you need to make three lists:

- what a prospective replacement **must** do, in order to fill the niche in your life,
- what the replacement **should** do, in order to make the switch satisfying, and
- what the replacement **may** do, which represents your ideal case.

After that, with lists in hand, I talked about gathering and assessing the options based on the three lists, and making sure that you [don't expect the same level of convenience and polish]({% post_url 2026-08-09-shop-small-2 %}) as the better-funded-but-abusive solutions.  Along the way, I walked through an example from my personal life, that I want to have a plan in place for when I need to replace my Roku device, now that Fox Corporation owns the formerly independent company.

I'd largely call that discussion finished, except for one piece.

## My Not Quite Recommendations

This time, largely to provoke some possible ideas and also to point to people who I think (currently and mostly) get it right, this post will end the little series by walking through what I currently use to keep big companies at figurative arm's length.  Since I urged you to start your process by building your requirement and nice-to-have lists, which probably won't match mine, I can't give clean you a list of recommendations, here.  I don't know---nor do I particularly care, in most cases---what you need to live your life.  Instead, I can't manage much better than giving you an idea of what I did or would move to.

Also, note that this list could change at any time.  Especially in software, the last few years have increasingly exposed projects as unreliable because project leadership openly supports authoritarians and/or perpetrators of abuse and/or "contributors" wasting everybody's time with LLM-generated code.  Add in licensing considerations, usability, leaders who made their reputation on money from (or sometimes proximity to) Jeffrey Epstein, and so forth, and the ground can shift quickly.  And yes, I put in this effort partly because I'd like to get away from that entire mess.

With any luck, though, some of this might spark an idea, especially with the process that I've outlined over the past couple of posts.  And when I launched the blog, I always had the idea that I'd spotlight the Free Software tools that I use, but never see a good opportunity to do that; this hits most of them at once, plus some other ideas.

## Operating System

I have told the story at least once on the blog, but after about twenty years of defending Microsoft Windows as a platform that largely let you do whatever you wanted, Microsoft started floating the idea that the Windows 8 roll-out might include only certifying hardware for Windows if the manufacturer's {% wiki UEFI %} implementation provided no way of installing alternate operating systems.  That worried me, and I figured that only declining market-share would stop them, so after a good Linux experience on a job, I installed [Ubuntu](https://ubuntu.com/) Linux on a disused netbook[^CVSHvP] to see how much I would actually use Linux at home.  And within a few weeks, the Windows machine had mostly become a glorified file server, showing me that I absolutely could work with Linux.  It probably helps that I could bring the netbook to the living room or back stoop instead of always working at my little bedroom desk, but it still made the point.

[^CVSHvP]:  I miss netbooks.  Presumably nobody else does, because they didn't sell well enough to keep the product-type in production, but you got extreme portability on a fairly usable machine, often for less than two hundred dollars.  You couldn't fix much, and they didn't dissipate heat well, but I really liked having them as an option for emergencies.

Currently, I use [Pop!_OS](https://system76.com/pop) on my laptop, Ubuntu on the much older spare machine that I mostly use as a white noise generator and as a "station" to note things in the dark while I sleep.  On my servers, I use [Debian](https://www.debian.org/).  On the media server, Debian uses [KDE](https://kde.org/)'s desktop environment, though the rest use [GNOME](https://www.gnome.org/).

### Does It Work, Though

You might have heard that Linux takes an excessive amount of work to get running and keep running.  It has that reputation for a few reasons.

- Proprietary software vendors like to perpetuate the idea, regardless of the validity, since it directs attention to their "it just works" products...that usually don't work.
- In the early days during the 1990s especially, Linux software had almost no hardware support, unless you happened to have a system nearly identical to what a major developer used.  For everything else, you needed to develop or find patches to build your own custom version.
- If you use specialty (or cutting-edge) hardware, then you might still need occasional custom work.
- A vocal faction of Linux users enjoy the system's old reputation as a "hobbyist operating system" that requires constant your personal attention, and shout down any attempt to make it seem approachable to casual users.  For example, you'll find them objecting to things like graphical configuration tools, because "everybody knows" that you configure Linux by repeatedly editing text files and rebooting the computer to see if that worked.

By contrast to the reputation, over the past (rough calculation) sixteen years, I have had *exactly one* case where I needed to patch Linux.  At the time, I used a recycled Chromebook---back when you could replace Google's operating system---with a more standard Linux.  For reasons that I can't recall, Ubuntu stopped supporting that brand of touchpad, so after every major software update, I needed to run a script that would get a copy of the patch and rebuild that module.  Yes, even the tedious "hobbyist" work barely requires any effort, for most cases.

Similarly, early in that sixteen years, I had one incident where something broke in the configuration and Ubuntu wouldn't start its graphical interface.  I ended up reinstalling the operating system from a USB stick, and having it install around my existing user information.  That replaced whatever broke, taking ten minutes or so to fix, only because it took me a while to remember that as an option.

Those problems certainly exist, sure.  But again, I had one instance of each kind of problem over sixteen years, one in a fringe condition.  Today, you can mostly get away with installing the operating system---after a couple of hours of trying it out on the USB drive, to make sure that you don't hate it---and use it exactly as installed.

And in terms of stability, my laptop usually runs out of resources and locks up after a couple of weeks running, requiring a reboot.  The servers usually run for months without any problems.  Really, I've had many more problems with the power connectors on the hardware---plugs that came loose or otherwise broke, poorly cooled CPUs, and so forth, which would've happened no matter the operating system---than I have had problems with Linux.

### Or Not

Notice that I said that I "currently" use these systems, though.  Do you remember when I talked about things changing fast a couple of sections back?  Well, over only the past few weeks, Linus Torvalds [defended accepting LLM contributions in the Linux kernel](https://lore.kernel.org/linux-media/CAHk-=wi4zC+Ze8e+p3tMv8TtG_80KzsZ1syL9anBtmEh5Z40vg@mail.gmail.com/)[^e9-XxL], while also berating the community for daring to see a Free Software project as *political*, let alone progressive.  Meanwhile, Debian---which Pop and Ubuntu rely on, by the way---held a biased vote[^5Zm5zT] among developers to [accept LLM-based "contributions"](https://www.debian.org/vote/2026/vote_002) in the project, on top of what comes to them from the kernel.

[^e9-XxL]:  The kernel, as the term might suggest, sits at the core of the operating system.  It provides ways for software to connect to the hardware, and schedules access to hardware in ways that (unless something goes wrong) prevent conflicts or locking something out for too long.

[^5Zm5zT]:  I don't participate in the community, so I didn't pay attention to the vote until afterward.  But looking at the evidence, I note that the ballot only disclosed that the option to limit contributions written by non-humans would require a 75% super-majority---because it would require amending the project's foundational documents---in fine print wedged between the page footer and the disclaimer about potentially collecting data about voting behavior.  And they only had that single "no" option.  They probably didn't do this *in order to* skew the vote, but they certainly didn't take any pains to make sure that voters understood their choices.

As such, I have started looking at alternatives, especially because I finally managed to revive one older computer---see below, because [shopping](#shopping) figured into getting my old Pinebook working again---that now needs a fresh installation of *something*.  Let's take a quick tour to give you some idea of the breadth, and I'll comment where I can.

I have far less experience with these, but let's go through what I know.  My Linux systems have all sat on the Debian side of the ecosystem, but [Fedora](https://fedoraproject.org/) has its own variation on the same with more interest in integrating updates faster, though they justify using LLMs because it speeds things up, so maybe don't trust that anymore.  Meanwhile [Nix](https://nixos.org/) and [Guix](https://guix.gnu.org/manual/1.5.0/en/html_node/System-Installation.html) focus on reproducible systems; [Aeryn](https://aerynos.com/) seems to sit in a similar space.  [Gentoo](https://www.gentoo.org/) mostly only ships as the source code, which the system then automatically builds optimized for your system, and which has an explicit ban on LLM work.  I have heard good things about [Elementary](https://elementary.io/) (which has a ban on LLM-generated code) and [Solus](https://getsol.us/) and their fast on-boarding for new users, but haven't seen either "in the wild."  If we trust the Linux kernel despite the LLMs (and Torvalds fearmongering about social justice), then one of these options might work.

Meanwhile, {% wiki Berkeley_Software_Distribution|BSD %} versions carry on one version of UNIX code of the same name, with [Open Indiana](https://www.openindiana.org/) one of the systems carrying on for another UNIX branch (Sun OS/Solaris).  I would imagine that you can find other descendants of different UNIX versions, if you have a preference---I know about Open Indiana, for example, because I used Solaris through most of college, and even have a Solaris-for-PC installation disk somewhere that I gave a brief try---but I haven't looked to see if anybody has Free Software derivative of HP-UX or the like.

For me (and I don't expect anybody else to draw these same boundaries), the BSD family seems out of the question, mostly because I believe that the reciprocal/copyleft licensing (the GPL) has done Linux a lot more good than the libertarian ethos of the BSD side has done for them, given that Apple built macOS on Free BSD.  However, their teams also seem slow to make decisions about LLM-based contributions, even though [NetBSD](https://www.netbsd.org/) (which has its own distinct kernel) has officially refused such contributions as muddying the strength of the copyright.

Other than that, I haven't generally seen the difference between BSD and Linux or different distributions[^0Djzib] of either as particularly meaningful for what I do---as long as I have a shell and can get or compile the software that I need, that'll work---but I also haven't seen much reason to change my own setup significantly to dig into any of them until recently.

[^0Djzib]:  Well, I don't see a significant-to-me difference, except for Omarchy, which you couldn't pay me to work with.  Not only does it only add a couple of script and configuration files to another Linux distribution, and not only does the project founder call violent anti-immigrant demonstrations "heartwarming," but every technical analysis of the system turns up the worst policies and security in the industry.  I have no idea how this mess has raised millions of dollars, except to the extent that millionaire fascists like to stick together and try to normalize one another.

If you want to go further afield than the UNIX-like world, [React OS](https://reactos.org/) has a (mostly) Windows-compatible environment.  [Haiku](https://www.haiku-os.org/) does the same for the short-lived Be OS.  [GNU Hurd](https://www.gnu.org/software/hurd/advantages.html) or [Redox](https://redox-os.org/) could replace the Linux kernel in distributions, and each has some interesting properties, such as Hurd not needing a reboot to change the system, which could improve the overall experience.  [Dusk OS](http://duskos.org/) can run on fairly low-powered machines (for people intending to become familiar with its internals), and its predecessor [Collapse OS](http://collapseos.org/) runs on substantially older machines on a scale that you or I could probably build by hand without too much trouble.  And you can find still more, if you poke around.

However, the further that you get from the more popular operating systems, the more work you'll probably need to do.  If the Redox kernel team seems like they match your vibe, for example, I don't believe that anybody has a full Redox "distribution," with the system utilities, user utilities, and graphical environment packaged together.  As such, you'll probably need to build a lot of that for Redox or whatever system, and figure out how to connect it all up.

I would love something like Elementary with either a Hurd or Redox kernel.  In fact, if you follow me on Mastodon, you might've even noticed me asking the Elementary team about the [prospects of replacing Linux with a kernel closer to their other ethical stances](https://mastodon.social/@jcolag/117209502760002272), since I realized that I probably don't want to do that on my own; short version, they confirmed my assumption that it'll take a lot of work, so don't count on it showing up soon.  And as I unearth old computers, I may even give Dusk OS a chance, though I don't expect it to become a system that I use everywhere.

## Web Browser

As you can probably guess, I use [Firefox](https://www.firefox.com/en-US/) for most work.  In fact, I used Firefox before it got the name, using the pre-release versions of Phoenix in 2002, then Firebird shortly after.  I don't keep using it out of inertia or because I have brand loyalty, but because I find it the most sensible choice.

Chrome-based browsers---and I do keep one or two around for testing code under different models or when a site makes it necessary, mind you; more on that, later---feel like trouble waiting to happen.  Because of the market-share, Alphabet has rammed through changes to HTML and HTTP without much resistance, because they implemented it and insisted that everybody else keep up.  Something about its rendering bothers me in a way that even Internet Explorer didn't.  And, petty as it sounds, I have never liked Chrome's user interface.  Upcoming versions of Chrome will block ad-blockers, and if that succeeds, bet on them integrating that more tightly so that derivative browsers (Edge, Vivaldi, etc.) have trouble allowing them again.

Because of Mozilla's odd priorities, I *could* shift to one of the Firefox derivatives, such as [Waterfox](https://www.waterfox.com/), but each of those projects relies on a tiny team to integrate changes (security patches, for example) to Firefox quickly.  I don't think that I can make that choice exclusively because I don't like that Mozilla didn't make the AI features opt-in instead of opt-out...especially considering that I opted out before they shipped the AI features?  Changing to a smaller version feels like a lot of added risk for me with no benefit.  Note that this may change, should the [Base Browser Project](https://codeberg.org/basebrowserproject/organization) get enough traction.  If those various Firefox derivatives shift to that common base so that they can combine their efforts before adding whatever makes them distinctive from the others, then I'll consider them more serious prospects.

Until then, Firefox doesn't act alone, though, to nobody's surprise, I'd imagine.  I have a pile of add-ons that make it (and the web) behave a bit better.  I'll touch on the interesting items on my list.  Pardon the loose categories, but I did say that I had quite a few, and didn't even bother to list the boring bits.

### Data Extraction

- [Copy Tables](https://addons.mozilla.org/en-US/firefox/addon/copywebtables/) - When a page has a table on it, this allows me to select and copy specific rows and/or columns, making it far easier to move what I need to a spreadsheet for other processing.
- [Form History Control](https://addons.mozilla.org/en-US/firefox/addon/form-history-control/) - This keeps track of form submissions, which I sometimes use for both the intended purpose of filling in a similar form *and* sometimes pulling out an idea for a blog post from something that I might have left as a comment somewhere.
- [Mark Download](https://addons.mozilla.org/en-US/firefox/addon/markdownload/) - A "Markdown Web Clipper," which when I select text (HTML) on a page, it gives me the Markdown that I can copy and drop in my notes or a blog post.  It does other things that I don't care about, too, but which could interest you.

### Exploration

- [Add custom search engine](https://addons.mozilla.org/en-US/firefox/addon/add-custom-search-engine/) - You can guess that I don't love the commercial search engines; a bit more on that, later.  This allows not only adding smaller competitors as the search-from-address-bar provider, but also narrower searches on individual sites.
- [Awesome RSS](https://addons.mozilla.org/en-US/firefox/addon/awesome-rss/) - In its early days, Firefox had a dedicated "this page points to an RSS feed" button in the address bar, but they dropped it when RSS fell out of fashion in the mainstream.  This creates a new button to grab the feed.
- [Blog Quest](https://addons.mozilla.org/en-US/firefox/addon/blog-quest/) - Think of a more aggressive version of the RSS button, because rather than flag that an RSS feed exists, this quietly records every RSS feed that it sees, so if I wonder later if a site had one, I can look through its list.
- [Consumer Rights Wiki](https://addons.mozilla.org/en-US/firefox/addon/consumer-rights-wiki/) - When opening a (large) corporate website, this pops up a list of the major controversies, with an option to never show it again (for a site that you need to use anyway, I guess) or only show it again when it has new information.  Particularly appropriate to this post, it nags me every time I open a page from Amazon or Alphabet page or wherever, helping remind me why I should go somewhere else.
- [Library Extension](https://addons.mozilla.org/en-US/firefox/addon/libraryextension/) - When looking at a shop's website, this flags if it can find the product at my local library.
- [Street Pass for Mastodon](https://addons.mozilla.org/en-US/firefox/addon/streetpass-for-mastodon/) - When a website has a link to the author's Mastodon account (with an often-invisible `rel="me"` link), this lights up so that I can follow up if I'd like.
- [Verify Me](https://addons.mozilla.org/en-US/firefox/addon/verify-me/) - This confirms that every one of those "you can also find me here" `rel="me"` links that I mentioned goes somewhere that actually points back to the website.  I mostly use it to validate my own pages, but it'd also help if I found a random page claiming to represent somebody on one of the platforms.

### Privacy

- [Canvas Blocker](https://addons.mozilla.org/en-US/firefox/addon/canvasblocker/) - Companies now use HTML's `canvas` element (which allows a page to draw arbitrary images) to identify users, and this blocks the most common approaches.
- [Clear URLs](https://addons.mozilla.org/en-US/firefox/addon/clearurls/) - Many writers and companies stuff their links with tracking information.  This removes it when copying the link.
- [Consent-O-Matic](https://addons.mozilla.org/en-US/firefox/addon/consent-o-matic/) - This doesn't work as well as it once did, but identifies cookie pop-ups---the kind that maliciously comply with the GDPR, for example---and automatically clicks through to tell them that I only consent to the minimum.
- [Decentral eyes](https://addons.mozilla.org/en-US/firefox/addon/decentraleyes/) - While not critical and almost never visible, when a website uses a library that sits on some big service, this quietly redirects to a copy of the same version of the file somewhere else to disrupt tracking.
- [Disconnect](https://addons.mozilla.org/en-US/firefox/addon/disconnect/) - This directly blocks a wide variety of known trackers.
- [DuckDuckGo Search & Tracker Protection](https://addons.mozilla.org/en-US/firefox/addon/duckduckgo-for-firefox/) - You can figure this one out for yourselves, I'd bet.
- [Facebook Container](https://addons.mozilla.org/en-US/firefox/addon/facebook-container/) - I believe that this ships with Firefox by default, pulling Meta websites into their own alternate universe where they can't connect your other browsing activity with what they know about you.
- [Firefox Multi-Account Containers](https://addons.mozilla.org/en-US/firefox/addon/multi-account-containers/) - This does *not* ship by default, for some reason, but generalizes the Facebook Container thing---I believe it uses the same code---for any arbitrary group of websites that you care to classify.
- [Privacy Badger](https://addons.mozilla.org/en-US/firefox/addon/privacy-badger17/) - The EFF's take on blocking trackers on websites.
- [uBlock Origin](https://addons.mozilla.org/en-US/firefox/addon/ublock-origin/) - This blocks a lot of garbage on sites, mostly automatically, since people have carefully built lists to import.

While it hasn't caused me any problems over the years, I should note that multiple layers of blocking trackers *probably* slows the browser down without much benefit.

### Readability

- [Activate Reader View](https://addons.mozilla.org/en-US/firefox/addon/activate-reader-view/) - Firefox *has* a minimal-CSS reader view, but many pages don't register as needing it.  This forces it on.
- [De-Arrow - Better Titles and Thumbnails on YouTube](https://addons.mozilla.org/en-US/firefox/addon/dearrow/) - I don't visit YouTube much anymore, but when I need to hit the site, this replaces the clickbait titles and preview images of a shocked face and the like with (I believe) crowdsourced versions that actually give an idea of what the video has to say.
- [Disable AI](https://addons.mozilla.org/en-US/firefox/addon/disable-ai/) - I don't spend much time on the big search engines, but when I do, this does what it can to prevent the site from kicking off the AI search/overview/assistance nonsense that will absolutely never help.
- [Indie Wiki Buddy](https://addons.mozilla.org/en-US/firefox/addon/indie-wiki-buddy/) - While the infrastructure behind it doesn't work reliably, and so I often need to shut it off for stretches of time, this bounces `fandom.com` requests to an independent clone.
- [JavaScript Toggle On and Off](https://addons.mozilla.org/en-US/firefox/addon/javascript-toggler/) - This creates a handy switch to turn JavaScript off on a page.
- [Lib Redirect](https://addons.mozilla.org/en-US/firefox/addon/libredirect/) - This redirects (mostly social media) links to web interfaces that don't send the company any information on me, so that I can click (for example) a Twitter or YouTube link without their nonsense, like Indie Wiki Buddy, but for a bunch of bigger services.  And like that plugin, the sites available for redirection sometimes vanish or stop working without notice, so prepare yourself to turn the redirection off a lot.
- [Right-Click Borescope](https://addons.mozilla.org/en-US/firefox/addon/right-click-borescope/) - This creates a context menu option that gives me a view of all images under the mouse pointer, which I usually use to snag image URLs for the Friday posts.
- [Rotate and Zoom Image](https://addons.mozilla.org/en-US/firefox/addon/rotate-and-zoom-image/) - This does what it says, zoom doing most of the work...

### Utility

- [Pass FF](https://addons.mozilla.org/en-US/firefox/addon/passff/) - I use [pass](https://www.passwordstore.org/) as my password manager, so connecting it to the browser helps a lot.  Word of warning, though, it requires a bit of setup outside the browser.
- [Shiori](https://addons.mozilla.org/en-US/firefox/addon/shiori_ext/) - I use [Shiori](https://github.com/go-shiori/shiori) as my bookmark manager, partly to generate that section of the newsletter, so the add-on connects the browser's bookmarks to it.

And there you go...

## Search Engine

Since I mentioned independent search engines earlier, I have to admit that I don't have a great solution, here.

Since they launched, I have used [Duck Duck Go](https://duckduckgo.com) almost exclusively.  As far as I could tell, at least for the sorts of things that I generally search for, it gave me significantly better results than Google trying to outsmart me.  However, since it relies on big-corporate search indices for its results, its quality has declined as the bigger search engines have.

Similarly, for about a year or two recently, I used a [Searx](https://docs.searxng.org/) installation regularly, a Free Software meta-search engine, but those have both Duck Duck Go's problem of relying on corporate indices *and* API throttling by those same corporations, meaning that they increasingly don't get many results at all.  Few and low-quality results don't make for a good solution.

I *want* to like [YaCy](https://yacy.net/), as Free Software and maybe the only independent search index---and definitely the only *distributed* index---so I'd accept lower quality results in exchange for independence, but the project keeps throwing up red flags.  Leaving it running on my server, in crashes for no clear reason; I originally imagined that I gave it a computer with too little power, but even on a beefier server, it craps out with little opportunity for recovery.  Then the developer(s) seem highly enamored with LLMs, using it for technical support, even though (by their own admission in the forum) it doesn't actually work.  They have also spent a ton of time trying to build an AI assistant into the software, *and* they've started "vibe-coding" the search engine itself.  All that makes it difficult to take seriously, to my irritation.

[Start Page](http://startpage.com/) combines searches from Google and Bing, so you probably know how this will go.  It actually seems to do slightly better than Duck Duck Go, but it also has many of the same problems.

[Mwmbl](https://mwmbl.org/), meanwhile, looks promising, but definitely only in an "early days" kind of way, especially when it doesn't make any clear statements about how it searches.  I get a lot of mostly relevant results for most searches, but they seem poorly prioritized.

Finally, [Mojeek](https://www.mojeek.com/) also looks interesting.  It apparently has its own index, and has survived for a while.  I haven't had much time to check them out despite over two decades out there, but I notice that they also have a dedicated Substack---as in the "we support the free speech of Nazis and help them monetize their hate speech" newsletter site---search *and* maintain their own blog there, which makes me uncomfortable.  For the moment, I have landed here despite my concerns, because they do at least have an independent search index and...well, it seems to mostly work.

No, I haven't paid any attention to Kagi, nor do I have much interest in paying for access to a search engine that doesn't even have its own index.  Also, like YaCy, [Kagi seems to raise too many red flags](https://d-shoot.net/kagi.html) for my tastes.  If you ask me (nobody did), everything about them smells like they want Alphabet to acquire them, because they seem to want to do everything that Alphabet does in the same ways.

I wonder if YaCy has documented their protocol well enough to build a simplified application that could take advantage of (and contribute to) the indexing that other users have done over the years.  It provides surprisingly good results, but also has the aforementioned problems working and their vibe-coding obsession doesn't seem likely to fix it soon.  Therefore, if a lighter-weight "peer" could get on the network, then that would probably solve my problems.

## Graphical Work

I regularly use the aforementioned unpleasantly named [GIMP](https://www.gimp.org/) and the better-at-names [Inkscape](https://inkscape.org/).  When I feel like a change, I have the fancier-looking [Krita](https://krita.org/en/) installed, designed more for painting than the sort of technical manipulation that I usually need.  And if I hit another situation where I can't use my laptop, I also have [Graphite](https://graphite.art/) running on one of my servers for use in a web browser.

Over the years, I have *tried* on occasion to get myself using [Scribus](https://www.scribus.net/), but never stuck with it.  If I had to guess, I'd say that the problem comes from my writing getting updated frequently, meaning that I would need a desktop publishing system to update the contents of a document when the source updates, and most desktop publishing doesn't have that idea.  Instead, I have settled on [Quarkdown](https://quarkdown.com/), which you can think of as a minimal programming language to write programs that convert Markdown (what I write almost everything in, these days, anyway) to different document types.  I have heard good things about [Typst](https://typst.app/)---and they have a policy against LLM use, if that matters to you for desktop publishing---but I don't need its features enough to consider converting my documents.

How do they compare to commercial systems?  I couldn't really tell you.  Unless I've forgotten something, I haven't used a commercial image-editing system since {% wiki Microsoft Paint %}, and {% wiki The Print Shop %} on the desktop publishing side, except for *one* time that I needed to export a PDF file...

That said, I can point you to a recent test suggesting that [Krita has ninety percent feature parity](https://blazetrends.com/open-source-krita-hits-90-of-photoshops-capability-as-creators-abandon-adobe-subscriptions) or so with Photoshop.  Maybe you use the ten percent of features that it can't match, though I would generally doubt it.

## Other Local Software

While I have almost certainly mentioned this before, I use [pass](https://www.passwordstore.org/) as my password manager, really not much more than a shell script that manages PGP-encrypted files.

The structure provides it with a few convenient features.  First, if something breaks the software, then I only need to decrypt the file(s) that I need to get my password(s), since it uses standard encryption and I have everything locally.  Or I could build my own client application.  The encryption also means that (barring a mathematical advance) I could leave my entire password repository somewhere public and not need to worry that someone will get the passwords.  And maybe most importantly, I don't ever need to worry about [corporate data breaches {% cc %}](https://www.wired.com/2015/06/hack-brief-password-manager-lastpass-got-breached-hard/) or [companies giving money to fascists](https://brennan.day/normalized-fascism-in-open-source-12-million-given-to-dhh/).

It mainly runs on the command-line, but you can find plenty of graphical interfaces.  And as I mentioned earlier, it takes some extra work to get it to work with web browsers, but it does work fairly well.

Related to password management, I use [GNOME Authenticator](https://apps.gnome.org/Authenticator/) for two-factor authentication codes.  I also have [2F Auth](https://2fauth.app/) running on a server (in-house, not connected to the Internet) as a backup, if the local software breaks somehow.  The password manager actually has a plugin to handle 2FA, but if you store your 2FA information with your passwords, then you no longer have *two*-factor authentication, only the superficial simulation of it.

For podcasts, I use [gPodder](https://gpodder.github.io/).  In case that breaks, I also have [Audio Book Shelf](https://audiobookshelf.org/) running on a server, with most podcasts set to only download manually, so that they don't sit there while I listen elsewhere.

Like almost anybody using a Free Software operating system, I use [LibreOffice](https://www.libreoffice.org/) on my desktop.  However, because I need office software so infrequently, I increasingly rely on [Crypt Pad](https://cryptpad.fr/) installed on a server.

I have plenty of other software, but most of it does something novel, rather than standing in for something normally provided by a large company.

## News

Separate from the rest of the media, I should talk about where I get my news, even though most people have probably figured that out.

I use an RSS feed reader.  [Fresh RSS](https://freshrss.org/) sits on one of my servers at home.  It checks for the latest articles and posts on any site that I add with an RSS feed---see [Exploration](#exploration) under web browsers for how to make them more discoverable---and I check periodically for whatever updates come in.  Any RSS feed reader allows you or me to curate to sources that have more of an interest in serving the public than selling ads against an angry audience.

The post has surely fallen out of date by now as I lose interest in some sites and find others, but in late-2024, I wrote an entire post on [what my RSS feed reads]({% post_url 2024-11-17-blogs %}), if you want a broad sense of how that looked.

If you want to do the same sort of thing, you don't need a web-based setup.  If you poke around, you can find many RSS feed readers for any mobile device that you happen to prefer.  Your e-mail client (if you use one) might have a reader already in it.  And I had some success with [RSS Guard](https://github.com/martinrotter/rssguard) on the desktop; under [Releases](https://github.com/martinrotter/rssguard/releases), you might find a build that'll run on your computer.

Oh, and if you pick a reader that you end up disliking, (almost) every RSS feed reader can import and export your information in a format called <abbr title="Outline Processor Markup Language">OPML</abbr>.  If you set up RSS Guard and decide that it won't work for you, then export your information from it, find the next reader that looks interesting, and import that file.  And then you can mostly continue reading as if you have always used this new reader.

Until around mid-2025, I also watched the [PBS News Hour {% cc %}](https://www.pbs.org/show/newshour/) most nights, probably the only television news that actually *covers* the news instead of putting a journalist in front of a giant screen to happily report that an obnoxious wealth-hoarder said something horrible.  Eventually I dropped the News Hour, though, because Donald Trump's doddering nonsense became every story, who he met with, what horrible thing that he said, what experts say will result from the action, and so forth.  And while that has a lot of importance, especially when you run a newsroom in Washington, DC, it grows exhausting quickly.

## Other

I have probably mentioned some of this a few times over the years, but probably not recently, other services that I use...

Shortly after the 2008 financial crisis, I moved my accounts from one of the major banks---where, by the way, they only ended up because that bank bought out the small local bank that I used for a decade or so before that, or rather they bought out the big bank that bought them out---to the local credit union.  I *highly* recommend this...moving to the credit union, I mean, not big banks gobbling up smaller banks.  The credit unions take care of everything quickly and professionally, and frequently follow up on visits.

Really, I only have one objection, which has only reared its head recently:  Certain organizations, never employers, use some third-party service for direct deposit, and that service (or those services) refuse to work with my credit union's computers, so they always need to cut me a check when they send me money.

I put changing off, as you might have, because credit unions have strict rules about who has permission to join.  But at some point, the criteria for the local credit union broadened from living in specific towns and/or working at certain companies to living anywhere in the areas that they serve.

And I also get my (home/VoIP) phone service through [Ooma](https://www.ooma.com/), which sells the device and then only charges the required state taxes.  I actually spring for the upgraded account that gets me a second line, a wider calling range, improvements to the voicemail and blocking, and so forth.  It replaces the (POTS) line that I had for many years that the phone company actively sabotaged to get me to cancel it so that they could abandon the network...

You probably also have mobile lines to worry about, but I can't really help you there.  I use a couple of Android devices---which would probably get a fresh operating system to get me away from Alphabet if they support it, maybe not, considering that I got them cheap---for various purposes, but not phones.
