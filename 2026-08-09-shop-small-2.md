When I started this "series" of posts, I mentioned that for all the time that I spend *implying* that you should probably walk away from big companies (technology-wise and not) for independent and especially Free Culture counterparts, I haven't laid out the whys and hows.  From there, I introduced the idea of [distancing ourselves from large companies]({% post_url 2026-07-19-shop-small-1 %}), which all increasingly seem to want to make our lives miserable, with links to a tiny fraction of the evidence for that perspective.

![The mascots for Mastodon, Pleroma, and Misskey watching a self-immolating blue bird running around in a panic](/blog/assets/twitter-on-fire.png "We get to feel smug because we get to watch exactly this show on a regular basis...")

I explained that this necessary distance does *you* good, not necessarily the world unless it becomes a huge movement.  That came with my own example, looking to move off the Roku platform to watch media on my television.  By the way, since that post last month, [Roku launched a channel to dump AI-generated trash {% cc %}](https://www.theverge.com/entertainment/976939/roku-fairground-ai-fast-channel) into televisions around the world, yet another reason to get out of there.

Finally, I started talking about the process of actually getting away from these abusive companies, specifically outlining the requirements.  Whatever you want to replace, you need to make three lists:

- what a prospective replacement **must** do, in order to fill the niche in your life,
- what the replacement **should** do, in order to make the switch satisfying, and
- what the replacement **may** do, which represents your ideal case.

I have my lists, so we can now look at the next steps in the process.

## Example's Quick Update

Before we go forward, I do want to mention two items that I've mentioned elsewhere.

First, in the previous post last month, I mentioned that Jellyfin has an IPTV feature that could potentially provide me with <dfn title="Free Ad-Supported Television">FAST</dfn> channels without needing to worry about access to a specific app.  I have since validated that theory, setting up a list of a dozen or so streaming "channels" that I'd likely watch, and even managed to track down a script that pulls Electronic Program Guide (EPG) information for the channels.

I can now confirm that it does work, and that I no longer look at the Roku Channel, Pluto TV, or similar apps at all, though I suppose that I still might if I want to watch something from their library.

In addition, as I mentioned in a recent developer diary post, I watch it so little that I neglected to list YouTube as a **should**-level requirement, but I do occasionally remember to follow a couple of creators there.  I could have abandoned them, but would like to make watching them more reasonable.  And about two weeks ago, I discovered a utility to [download from YouTube channels]({% post_url 2026-07-27-robespierre %}) in a way that Jellyfin accepts as a normal television show, while also phasing out (deleting) old videos.

Having set that up, Jellyfin now *also* now replaces YouTube for most of my purposes.

OK, with those updates, *now* we can get back on track.

## Assess the Options

Once you know what you need, then you can start looking for any available products and projects that might fill the role.

Think of this a lot like brainstorming, with "no bad ideas."  If you ask in public, especially, then *state your requirements* instead of asking for "possible replacements for XYZ."  And---again, brainstorming rules---tamp down your desire to shout down anybody who doesn't read your mind before replying with a perfect solution for your needs.  You don't even need to reply to people if you think that they've answered in bad faith, but if you show yourself as the person who'll scream at anybody who doesn't do all the work for you, then why would you ever expect help?

And once you have your prospective options, evaluate each one against your requirements.  And I shouldn't need to say this, but if it doesn't meet every requirement on your **must** list, discard it.  Of the surviving candidates, organize them based on how well they meet the **should** list, with the **may** list breaking any "ties" that you might run across.

### Media Box Options

In my case, I can already guess some likely possibilities, though I'll need to conduct a more rigorous search later and evaluate them all.

#### Use the Web

First, as mentioned, I may not *want* the option, but a web browser will absolutely work.  Every (audio or video) streaming service that I have ever used seemed to work fine under Firefox, at least with their DRM code enabled.  Therefore, if I have one of my servers plugged into the television's HDMI port, then the Debian desktop and browser could show everything as full-screen video.  This comes *pretty* close, except for the inconvenience of trying to control it from standing eight feet away without much space available.

And sure, I could probably spend time modifying the web browser, developing some bare-bones user interface that I could control with something like a cheap Bluetooth game controller with the directions mapped to something useful[^3zPP0V].  That technically solves the problem, though at the expense of making that computer useless as a conventional desktop, and the further expense of my needing to write code and work out the design.  KDE's upcoming [Plasma Bigscreen](https://plasma-bigscreen.org/) might help a bit, but that alters the operating system shell to fill in with apps, rather than adjusting the browser to better work with media on a large television.  But even then, maybe the links to the streaming services would become the apps, opening them in the default browser.

[^3zPP0V]:  For example, while I don't yet know how to do half of what I describe, imagine starting with the default browser opening with each streaming service of interest open in a tab.  Now imagine growing the tabs so that the tab bar fills the window, with each tab taking a significant cut of that.  The user, at least in my imagination, can arrow around between the tabs, with Enter/Space/whatever switching to that tab.  That causes problems navigating the individual page, but I've literally only spent five minutes on this idea.  And actually, a bit more research shows that Chrome-derivatives (including Chromium and the like) have an `--enable-spatial-navigation` command-line option on startup, which uses the arrow keys to navigate between links.  It doesn't simplify the site, but it could do part of the job.

#### Meet the New Overlords, Same As the Old Overlords

I should also look at the existing commercial solutions, even if I don't see them as a step-up.  Alphabet, Amazon, Apple, and Walmart all have boxes and/or dongles with similar app ecosystems and a similar user interface.  I can't guarantee that they all support the necessary apps, but I shouldn't discount them even if that really only changes one right-wing corporate overlord for a slightly quieter right-wing corporate overlord with much more money.

They *might* serve the purpose.  Or rather, like the web browser, most options here will surely work, and I only need to decide if I can stomach getting into their ecosystems.  And if one or more of the four required services doesn't have an app for one or more of the lines of devices, then I can remove it from consideration completely and never wonder if I should've taken that path.

#### Overlord with a Muzzle

Similarly, I have often *considered*, but haven't gone through with installing something like [Pi Hole](https://pi-hole.net/) to block telemetry and continue on with Roku devices.  It *feels* like an ugly solution, because I would then need to worry about the in-house DNS server running to use the television, but it admittedly could solve about half my objections to using Fox-Roku, at least until I need to pay for a new device.

People even maintain Roku-specific block-lists for the software, so it shouldn't need much testing.

I should also note that running Pi Hole would allow for broader ad-blocking for every device on the network, if I wanted to do that.

#### Dreams of Electric Sheep

Then, the third point on the metaphorical triangle---using a web browser or continuing to use an existing commercial device as the first two, with the other ideas so far variations on those---especially because of my choice of apps, the possibility exists that I could potentially run a de-Googled Android derivative, such as [/e/](https://e.foundation/e-os/) or [Graphene OS](https://grapheneos.org/)[^ur2rrM], on another small computer and run the relevant apps there.  If the apps work, then I'd have (something pretty close to) the required 10-foot UI, taking the apps as exported from the Google Play Store, a part which other people have surely done or know how to do.

[^ur2rrM]:  I call them out by name because they both have regular and recent updates and each takes a different approach to dealing with apps that try to use Google Play Services.  /e/ has a Free Software layer called Micro-G that tries to mimic the official API responses, while Graphene puts those apps in a sandbox so that they can contact Google without leaking any other information about the device.  The former takes a purer approach but risks providing the wrong kind of response.  The latter has a better chance of working (until Alphabet starts locking Android down), but creates that new reliance on a corporate overlord.

Under ordinary circumstances, I'd dismiss this quickly, because the modern DRM/{% wiki Widevine %} ecosystem has software-only components that can live in a browser, app, or operating system, but also has hardware and firmware aspects that some companies latch onto, meaning that only "real" Android on Alphabet-approved hardware has a chance of working, at which point I might as well use a dedicated device from Google.  However, because two of my key apps (Jellyfin and PBS) don't use DRM, and non-profits run the other two, with little interest in goosing subscriber numbers in the name of fighting piracy, I might have a decent chance of getting this to work.  Peacock doesn't have much of a chance in this scenario, but I already declared that an acceptable loss, one of the more important reasons to start with the three lists that I described earlier.

Oh, by the way, if this de-Googled Android idea does work, that actually creates *new* opportunities, as in features that appeal to me that didn't make it to my lists because I don't have them now.  For example, Hoopla's TV app doesn't support comics---even though I learned from the DC Universe app (back when it existed as a broader service than today's comics-only DC Universe Infinite) that comics look fairly good on a big screen, moving panel-by-panel---but their Android app does.  Similarly, Android would almost certainly give access to audiobooks from [Libby](https://libbyapp.com/).  Right now, I get the comics on the website and audiobooks on a small tablet, but if I expect to spend time in the living room anyway, I see no reason not to take advantage of the larger screen and better audio quality if I can.  Plus, the apps should synchronize my progress, meaning that I could listen to part of an audiobook on television on a rainy day, then continue it on the tablet outside in nicer weather.

#### The Grandparent

Finally, somewhere in the middle of that triangle, and older than everything here other than the web browser, we have [Kodi](https://kodi.tv/) or any of the similar software projects.  A "media center" which has a variety of add-ons, some of which do resemble typical TV apps, might fill the role.  I don't see any of those add-ons that I would need other than Jellyfin, but I may need to look in places other than the official list.

Admittedly, I don't even know if a "media center" actually fits the requirements of a digital media player.  To me, it looks like it competes more with Jellyfin than Roku as it tries to manage media libraries, but it also looks close, does run as a user interface on the computer (which would show over HDMI), and always features in these sorts of conversations, making me think that I have misunderstood some part of what it wants to do.

#### TBD

Now, I haven't gotten further than this in my own process, other than sending a request to Hoopla and Kanopy asking if they had made or would consider making sure that their apps worked on a non-Google Android, explaining that doing so would play nicely with their broad public library philosophy of making sure that everybody has access.

At some point, I'll need to evaluate these five---"use the web browser" doesn't count, since we all know that and how it works, and mostly serves as the plan of last resort---choices, setting up Pi Hole on one of my existing servers to see how it pans out, looking at the difficulty of changing Firefox's user interface and navigating the relevant websites, seeing if I can stomach any of the commercial boxes, and testing Kodi and non-Google Android setups for compatibility.

## Adjust Your Expectations

OK, now we need to talk about the big issue, probably the idea that actually inspired this post.  Prepare yourself.

**Stop making excuses** and learn to work with what you get.  And if you must make excuses, at least come up with something original that actually presents a new problem for you.  Let's talk examples, since we have so many, yet my phrasing sounds oddly coy.  Consider the Fediverse.

You know the Fediverse, I hope, the upstart social network not owned or managed by a corporation, despite Meta's attempt to muscle in with Threads.  Using a common protocol, {% wiki ActivityPub %}, dozens of software projects power thousands of servers in a way that lets (almost) any of millions of us communicate with (almost) any of the rest of us, with little trouble.  Everybody pretty much knows, at this point, that they shouldn't trust Facebook, YouTube, WhatsApp, Instagram, TikTok, Reddit, {% x %}, and so forth.  Some might feel that they need one or two of them to stay in contact with specific people or groups, but they know that they should at least stake out space somewhere not run by a gigantic corporation lusting after making you fight with people so that they can sell more ads and harvest your data.  The Fediverse feels like the solution, then, with no profit motive and no ads[^pUQ6ur] unless you somehow count people posting about their own work.  I mean, I send out announcements to blog posts, and you might consider that advertising.

[^pUQ6ur]:  I vaguely remember hearing about *one* server experimenting with selling ad space to cover costs, years ago, but nobody ever mentioned it again, so I assume that they gave up.  The possibility of doing so certainly exists, but most servers run fine on user donations (a Mastodon server with not-many users apparently runs comfortably on one of those three-Euro-per-month virtual servers), so I guess that I should say that you **probably** won't find any ads pushed into your line of sight because they paid for access to your eyeballs.

Raise the Fediverse as an option when people talk about leaving "Big Social," though, and people reject it.  Why?

- Choosing a server takes far too much effort.  People describe the network as *unusable* because of this.
- Some servers take an arbitrary approach to moderation.
- If a server owner stops paying the bills or throws a fit and the server goes down, or if the server crashes, then you lose everything that you (or the owner) didn't back up.
- If you change servers, then your post history doesn't come with you to your new server.
- Direct messages don't have end-to-end encryption; your server owner can read what you say from the database.

These complaints all strike me---and I selected the Fediverse specifically since the most common objections directly reflect my "at least come up with something new" directive---as disingenuous, because I can't imagine what service we need to compare the Fediverse to, where any of these makes for a legitimate complaint.  The big sites have the *worst* reputation for far worse moderation policies than the worst corners of the Fediverse; [Facebook enabled ethnic cleansing campaigns](https://www.asc.upenn.edu/research/centers/milton-wolf-seminar-media-and-diplomacy/blog/road-hell-paved-good-intentions-role-facebook-fuelling-ethnic-violence) and [{% x %} made CSAM a feature](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cvg1mzlryxeo), after all.  When a centralized social media site shuts down, you similarly lose your content and contacts, unless you had the foresight to export your data *and* make arrangements with your friends to meet up somewhere else; you don't have the option of moving to another server.  And over the years, multiple people from Silicon Valley companies have told me that managers will sometimes use direct access to the database as a selling point of the job, that employees can quietly "check up on" people no matter their privacy settings.

And sure, you need to invest a bit of time in picking a server, but...I don't know, have you people never gone *grocery shopping*?  You could have multiple birthdays pass while you try to figure out which bottle of olive oil you should buy.  And we haven't even looked at the other oils, the condiments, the breads, the cheeses, and on and on.  Forget about insurance or any number of other products and services.  Most adults probably go through and survive that sort of nonsense on a regular basis, probably by *picking one* and living with any disappointment until you need to replace the thing.  You want to tell me that you can't do exactly that with a list of servers...?  Pick something and deal with it, changing servers if you decide that you picked wrong.

Oh, and I forgot the other big complaint/excuse.

- I don't have any friends there, and my friends probably won't move.

And OK, maybe.  But your friends on other platforms can say the same thing, because *you* won't move.  You don't need to move everybody at once.  You don't need to commit to a single platform for the rest of your life.  And you don't need to already have a community in-place wherever you log in.

The complaints change depending on the replacements and the needs, but the general idea doesn't, that a certain kind of person latches onto any perceived defect as an excuse to not change from the abusive corporate nonsense.  In other words, no matter if you need a replacement for your graphics program ("calling it *the GIMP* feels icky"), web browser ("Mozilla seems too excited about AI"), a chat app ("Signal requires[^KFVgtf] a telephone number"), or anything else, expect and accept some things that you won't love.  I assure you that you did exactly that with the commercial versions that you used before it, and got accustomed to those quirks quickly.

[^KFVgtf]:  You can use a Google Voice number, by the way; I did exactly that.  They also have tools to hide the number from other users, and I believe that they have a change coming where you can register without a number on their desktop app soon.

And always keep in mind that these projects don't have billions of dollars and an army of developers behind them.  They need to prioritize what they work on, based on what makes the few developers happy, because they don't really have another metric to judge by.

Now, sometimes, you might find a deal-breaker, and I don't want to play that down.  For example, because of the few people involved and a lack of expertise, many small projects fail to consider accessibility at all.  If you need that and can't contribute expertise, I don't mean this section to say that you should go figure it all out on your own.  In a perfect world, they would prioritize ease of use for everybody, but in a world where not much money comes in (especially in Free Software), a small group of happy users outweighs a larger group of users getting by, and that frequently puts an unfair burden on people who already have more work in using software.

However, if you have an excuse that I can repurpose for whatever *you* already deal with, though, then maybe pipe down and manage your expectations.  You won't get a solution that does everything perfectly.  Since requirements often conflict, you probably *can't* get such a solution.  For a classic example, you won't find an Amazon alternative with the same selection and will drop everything off on your doorstep in a few hours, but mysteriously without the horrible billionaire exploiting every step in the supply chain.

And notably, sometimes you might *need* that, but you shouldn't use that occasional need for a service that only terrible people provide as a reason to immerse yourself in their toxic ecosystem.

### But Don't Say THAT One

Seriously, cut that out.

If you want people to take you seriously, you don't get to outline requirements that read like the literal marketing for an existing project, then insist on ruling that out.  The fact that you carve out that exception says that you already know the best---the *only*---answer, but would rather endure the privacy breaches, the manipulation, and other abuses than take it.

Providing that you don't mind the coarse language for dramatic effect, somebody wrote up the [correct response for people avoiding Mastodon {% cc by-nc-sa %}](https://giants-club.net/articles/just-use-mastodon/) in the social media space.

I use Mastodon[^Ir1KjY]---twice, no less---as an example because it comes up so frequently as the solution that people avoid for the most insipid reasons, but the same goes for every other category.  When you know to look for it, you'll constantly see people looking for a non-corporate operating system with features X, Y, and Z, *but not Linux*, a web browser with whatever features and without Google's fingers in it, *but not Firefox*, a minimal-onboarding chat app *but not Matrix*, and so forth.

[^Ir1KjY]:  And by the way, everybody already knows the other half of this speech, right?  If you don't like Mastodon, then head over to Friendica, Pixelfed, Pleroma, Lemmy, PeerTube, Misskey, Write Freely, or any of the [more-than-a-hundred other applications](https://fediverse.party/en/miscellaneous/) running ActivityPub.  In most cases, you can still communicate with the rest of us.

It *sounds* like you want one of those well-known solutions, but backed by a right-wing billionaire who'll sell you out in five years and exploit the system to turn it into a conduit for right-wing content.

If you'd like an analogy instead of Mastodon, consider the big pitfalls in eating healthy, or vegetarian, or vegan, or any other attempt to "clean up" your diet.  People often start by trying to find identical drop-in replacements for their favorite junk foods.  They diligently replace chicken with eggplant, chocolate with carob, portabello mushroom caps for burger patties, and so forth, preparing it (or having it prepared) exactly as they would some traditional dish that they like.  And no surprise, it tastes terrible and has an awful texture, making the diet more difficult to tolerate and giving the entire idea a bad name.  Instead, you want to make dishes that highlight the new ingredients, so that you'll actually *enjoy* the food.

In the same way, lean into the parts of new systems and companies that you can enjoy, and stop complaining that the new thing doesn't solve every problem or that some nice-to-have on your list works, or does, but not in the specific way that you'd prefer.  Humans adapt, so you can, too.

### Ugh, the Community

OK, look, I get it.  Often, part of the "don't tell me about the only valid solution" problem stems from it feeling like the people who love it want to pressure you into following along.  And yes, whenever Microsoft or some social media site does something incredibly dangerous for users, Linux and Mastodon people show up asking if people will finally listen.

And sure, I get that many people on the Internet act like jerks.  Don't engage with them, or kick them in their digital teeth, I don't care.

However, let's look at some context for when you complain about Windows and someone "smugly" suggests Linux, or whatever social media and Mastodon, or whatever browser and Firefox, or whatever.

The big thing to keep in mind, here?  The Free Software people, especially the Linux people, have had this discussion for over *thirty years*.  It doesn't matter whether you start with Windows, Facebook, Twitter, Chrome, Adobe, or anything else:

- The company changes their product and makes the users angry.
- Those angry users vent about how this needs to stop and everybody should boycott the company.
- Those angry users ask for alternatives.
- Advocates suggest free alternatives.
- The angry users encapsulate the entire structure that I've outlined that, no, you can *only* answer with a polished drop-in replacement that does everything the users want, with absolutely no work or education on their part.  And also, the advocates shouldn't act so smug for making choices that allow them to consistently ignore these problems.
- The angry users write about how none of the advocates have offered them free labor, and that secretly stops them from making the change.

Everybody has seen this conversation before.  I already talked about the specific objections, and this entire post really goes through the full conversation.

Again, if someone harasses you, don't stand for that.  And every community has huge problems, Free Software included.  But if someone *only* suggests that you experiment with taking action on the thing that you've (legitimately) ranted about, maybe that person hasn't offered to help because the interaction has given the impression that you won't put in any effort of your own.  Nobody wants to help someone like that, and everybody in this space has needed to deal with people like that over and over again.

And critically, any "smugness" comes from the repeated vindication of the movement even as people refuse to learn any lessons.  Especially when you show resistance, it gives the impression that maybe they've dealt with exactly you before, and only now, after much mockery, you want help, but only help on your narrow terms.

In other words, before you wish that the Linux (or Mastodon, or...) community would treat you with more empathy, take a moment to remember what your interaction looks like on the other side of the screen to someone who has warned of exactly your problems for decades, only for your predecessors (or maybe even you, in a previous iteration of the problem) to laugh at them.

### Media Box Expectations

I mostly already know how this will go, no matter how I end up resolving the previous section.  The fact that Hoopla, Kanopy, PBS, and Peacock all have proprietary apps will bother me.  The fact that Hoopla, Kanopy, and Peacock all use DRM of one sort or another will bother me.

It will annoy me, but I need to remember that I *already* make that tradeoff today, and that no fully Free Software solution for Hoopla, Kanopy, or Peacock app exists, let alone the server.  They all use DRM on some level, too.

If I want to still take advantage of the public library and only run Free Software, then I'd need to regularly visit the library to check media out.  And even then, that will require accepting DRM on video, even if people have cracked it for most cases, allowing me to run a DVD through the media server.

On top of that, even if I go to the extreme of only watching or listening to media if I physically carried it out of the library, that still has more than a bit of hypocrisy involved.  After all, don't I keep telling everybody that they should look at more Free Culture media instead of slogging through the same parade of big-corporate franchises?

Alternatively, if I use another corporate product, then I should expect pretty much the same problems that I see now, but with a different name attached.  And if I get away from corporate products entirely, such as my web browser idea, then the solution will have some ugly and rough edges, and probably not the slick "arrow over to the app, which loads in a couple of seconds and works flawlessly" experience that I have today.
