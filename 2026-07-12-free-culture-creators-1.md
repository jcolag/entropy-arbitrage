## Recap

I kicked off that process of defining the idea back in September with an [elevator pitch for Free Culture]({% post_url 2025-09-21-define-free-culture-1 %}), defining the idea in terms of the four freedoms from the Free Software movement.

- The freedom to experience the work as you wish, without restriction
- The freedom to study the work, and to create derivative works that say what you want
- The freedom to redistribute copies so you can help others
- The freedom to distribute copies of your modified versions to others without restriction

I also talked a bit about why this might matter to us, especially in an age of constant media consolidation.  After that, I tried to use historical quotes about art and cooperation to outline a modern [philosophy of Free Culture]({% post_url 2025-10-05-free-culture-philosophy %}) not reliant on opposition to copyright or other individualistic, libertarian positions that coincidentally uphold tropes like abusive lone geniuses.  I summarized the results with this minimal statement.

> Humans need to create and share, creating culture; Free Culture sees this as an inalienable right.

This oversimplifies away the idea that we make art to connect with others---our inspirations, our peers/audience, and those who we ultimately inspire---because everybody has something to contribute to the culture, but for less than a hundred characters, it probably passes muster.

After that, I tried to outline the [history of Culture Freedom]({% post_url 2025-10-26-define-free-culture-history %}) from what we now call fan-fiction, to fights over copyright, to the movements that set up the mechanisms by which we can ensure that at least *our* parts of the culture remain free.

Then, before puttering out before the holidays, I started digging into the [mechanisms of Free Culture]({% post_url 2025-11-16-free-culture-mechanics %}) and a deeper [look at licensing]({% post_url 2025-11-23-free-culture-licensing-2 %}).  You can think of those as the current and aspirational (respectively) mechanisms *of* the mechanisms of Free Culture.

Finally, we had the flip-side of the (I hope) next post---think of this post as the transition---looking into the [ways to support Free Culture]({% post_url 2026-03-22-free-culture-responsibilities-1 %}), from sharing it to making the project your own.

## Taking Responsibility, Again

OK, in a literal, letter-of-the-law sense, we as Free Culture creators don't really have many responsibilities.

- Create.
- Publish it under the terms of an appropriate license that offers the four freedoms in some way.
- Avoid suing people for copyright infringement, provided that they abide by the conditions listed in the license.

Or if you'll pardon the massive abridgment, let's let Fodongo's (tongue-in-cheek) [A Practical Guide to Making Free Culture](https://geesegoose.ca/product/fodongo-presents-a-practical-guide-to-making-free-culture-download/) explain the traditional approach.

![Three pages of the zine. Step 2 says Make the Thing (anything). Step 4 says Put That License Visibly on Your Work. Step 5 says Share, indicating that you can optionally charge money. The frog mascot does the work at each step](/blog/assets/Fodongo-practical-free-culture.png "Go get the entire thing; if you've read this far, then you'll enjoy it")

Technically, that approach---text or comic---satisfies the definition.  And really, you can drop the last item from the list, because contract law, barring some fringe condition, says that our hypothetical creator will lose, since they granted public, irrevocable permission for the thing that they want to forbid *and* received their "consideration" in the form of the creator of the derivative work providing attribution and/or making their work available under the appropriate license.

In the grand scheme, I don't really have a problem with this, because it does make works available for other people to take on, and we never want to shame people doing their best to share.  If you can't go beyond this, it'll do.  And if you need to explain the idea to new people, as the zine does, better to focus on the mechanics than tens of thousands of words of trying to make this all more rigorous and cohesive.

However, you might notice that this gives us an art or a skill, but not a *culture*, and not really "culture freedom" in the sense that I have sketched out in prior posts.  Any cooperation gets pushed off as somebody else's problem, because the creator has largely moved on.  And indeed, if you look at the Free Culture space, you'll find all these little islands of (usually) unrelated projects, most "thrown over the wall" with nothing to do but consume or engage in a one-sided collaboration where one participant already did their part.

Interestingly, this even happens in crowdsourced Free Culture works, where they mostly build their little community around their project and have no engagement with or interest in other projects or seeing where their work goes.

Likewise, because the organizations that I've talked about in prior posts would rather "lead" on those mechanical bits instead of building a movement, it creates this idea that Free Culture needs yet another lesson about what license does what.  I don't think that a week goes by on the Fediverse without someone talking about their blog post or conference talk about which Creative Commons license does what, with the same "all licenses have equal social validity" attitude as all the others.

And this post, as a lead-in to bringing the practice of Free Culture closer to our ideals, let's look at what that "leadership" has done to the movement that we could argue separates the practice from principles.

Yeah, I went the long way around the barn to get here.  Now you understand why the original version of this post grew too big to make sense as a single post...

### The Four Freedoms Again

I talked about this a bit at the start of this series of posts, but let's expand a bit.  Back in my post about [the mechanics of Free Culture]({% post_url 2025-11-16-free-culture-mechanics %}), I pointed out that the Four Freedoms...

- The freedom to experience the work as you wish, without restriction
- The freedom to study the work, and to create derivative works that say what you want
- The freedom to redistribute copies so you can help others
- The freedom to distribute copies of your modified versions to others without restriction

...They interrelate.  Without the first, you don't really get the others.  If you don't have the second or third, then you don't get the fourth.

We can build on that initial idea of "publish under the terms of a Free Culture public license, then don't attack people for taking you up on your offer" as Free Culture, by specifying that the license must unconditionally grant the Four Freedoms, and that a creator must also *respect* those freedoms once granted.  That doesn't get us too much further along, though.  In fact, in some ways, it only restates what we already have.

Instead, we should think about what actions best respect and enforce those freedoms.  And critically, we need to think about what actions best respect and enforce those freedoms *even in the face of large-scale attacks*.

## Libertarians at the Gates

I should note that Free Culture---considered the broader field that includes Free Software and similar, since I think that we all see software as cultural works---has always faced the sorts of attacks that I mean, from both inside and outside the movement.

### Digital Microbiomes

From the start, people attacked the GPL as a "viral" license, infecting everything it gets inside.  Then-Microsoft executive Steve Ballmer---the same weirdo who spent an uncomfortably long time stomping around a stage repeatedly screaming the word "developers," if you remember that nonsense---went further, in possibly the first quote in this series of posts where I need to rely on Fair Use to copy it...

> Open source is not available to commercial companies. The way the license is written, if you use any open-source software, you have to make the rest of your software open source. If the government wants to put something in the public domain, it should. Linux is not in the public domain. Linux is a cancer that attaches itself in an intellectual property sense to everything it touches. That's the way that the license works.

No, I picked up on how the inclusion of a tiny work under copyright has called into question the freedoms that you have regarding this post, attaching itself in an intellectual property sense to everything it touches, almost like a...virus, maybe.

Anyway, in response to this fearmongering, including the outright lies about the inaccessibility of Free Software to poor perpetually disadvantaged corporations, the Free Software community banded together and said *damn straight, the pod people have come for your pathetic walled gardens*.  They made this the centerpiece of a campaign that convinced...

Ha, kidding.  Sorry, I couldn't even finish that fiction with a straight face.

No, I couldn't find anything more assertive from the period than Georg C. F. Greve's [**Brave GNU World** #10](https://www.gnu.org/brave-gnu-world/issue-10.html).

> The GNU General Public License has been called "viral" by people opposed to it for some time now. It occurred to me that this derogatory term has been accepted way too easily.
>
> In this context people talk about the GNU GPL "infecting" the code it licenses. The virus analogy implies the GPL then spreads uncontrollably and to the harm of the public.

...and it[^XiqF79] goes on to the *absolutely savage* insistence that "only people who want to take away the rights of other users may feel harmed by it."  And by "savage," I mean hilariously weak, and worryingly comparable to the [if you have nothing to hide, then you have nothing to fear]({% post_url 2026-03-01-nothing-hide %}) line that he'd presumably object to in ways that provide the argument structure to contradict him.  The only person who I can find in the movement talking about the characterization at all doesn't have anything more to say than words hurt, and idle hopes that people won't buy into the idea.

[^XiqF79]: Copyright (C) 1999 Georg C. F. Greve, German version published in the [Linux-Magazin](http://www.linux-magazin.de/), since "Permission is granted to make and distribute verbatim copies of this transcript as long as the copyright and this permission notice appear."

I should note that I have vague memories of Free Software figures also carefully outlining all the ways that companies could happily exploit[^Xwpqv7] Free Software without ever needing to contribute anything back, but I can't find any evidence of it, so may have mistaken something else for that.  I know that *many* people wrote about how businesses could make a fast buck off Free Software, such as charging to write new features, technical support, or even distribution, though the last comes with the caveat that somebody could always undercut your prices.

[^Xwpqv7]:  This goes back to the recurring theme in this series of posts, that the libertarian thinking that has motivated these movements has also corroded them, in this case by positioning everything for corporate harvesting instead of fighting exploitation.

Anyway, looking back, this seems like a massive strategic error, when they could have said yes, they deliberately built the license to spread, because the "virus" **protects the hosts** from the far worse disease of multinational corporations stealing people's labor, more akin to [bacteriophages](https://theconversation.com/viruses-arent-all-nasty-some-can-actually-protect-our-health-117678) that prey on exploitative practices.  Today, we also have (same article) the example of the asymptomatic Pegivirus C, which binds to the same receptors used by HIV, slowing the progression of the deadly disease.

### Opportunistic Schism

However, the people pushing the "Open Source" rebranding definitely had things to say, by positioning their vision as [more benign](https://web.archive.org/web/20021204155022/http://www.opensource.org/advocacy/case_for_hackers.php#marketing) (to exploitation enterprises) than Free Software.

> The case that needs to be made to most techies isn't about the concept of open source, but the _name_. Why not call it, as we traditionally have, _free software_?
>
> One direct reason is that the term "free software" is easily misunderstood in ways that lead to conflict. You can read an [extended discussion](https://web.archive.org/web/20021204155022/http://www.opensource.org/advocacy/case_for_hackers.php#marketingfree-notfree.php) of this problem.
>
> But the real reason for the re-labeling is a marketing one. We're trying to pitch our concept to the corporate world now. We have a winning product, but our positioning, in the past, has been awful. The term "free software" has been misunderstood by business persons, who mistake the desire to share with anti-commercialism, or worse, theft.
>
> Mainstream corporate CEOs and CTOs will never buy "free software." But if we take the very same tradition, the same people, and the same free-software licenses and change the label to "open source" – _that_, they'll buy.
>
> Some hackers find this hard to believe, but that's because they're techies who think in concrete, substantial terms and don't understand how important image is when you're selling something.
>
> In marketing, appearance is reality. The appearance that we're willing to climb down off the barricades and work with the corporate world counts for as much as the reality of our behavior, our convictions, and our software.

You got all that, right?  Officially, they'll say that they mostly objected to the multiple meanings of [free](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/free) in English.  But really, they openly and officially wanted to strip the ethics and *anti-commercialism* from the rhetoric, so that big corporations would start taking advantage of the community.  And all you peons, you get too wrapped up in the details to not see the opportunities of---let me check my notes, here---having a billionaire profit from your labor without paying you.

And especially check out that last paragraph.  **Appearance is reality**, they literally say.  If they can scrub the ethical considerations away from Free Software, then they'll probably go away, because propping up corporations means more than having principles.

And there you go.  The two branches of the movement divided themselves between "sticks and stones will break my bones..." and "we should appease the attackers."  Here, the firebrand of the story, [abuse-positive transphobe Richard Stallman](https://stallman-report.org/) finally woke up and...asked people [not to use the Open Source term](https://web.archive.org/web/20000815083642/http://www.fsf.org/philosophy/free-software-for-freedom.html), and also to re-litigate that people need to mention the GNU Project when talking about Linux, because why make this about principles when you can quibble about semantics and hold grudges.  He quietly omits the brief period when he tried to swallow his pride and use the more popular term, because taking responsibility for one's actions rates even lower than principle, especially when you need to write something reminding people that you still don't see a problem with powerful men sexually exploiting children.

And then we wonder how [Heartbleed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heartbleed)-like incidents happen to software (Open SSL) with billions of users.  It couldn't have anything to do with the movement "leadership" approving of exploitation...

### Licensing Gaps

I won't go into as much detail, here, because I have [written about the FSF's failures before]({% post_url 2021-05-09-fsf %}#history-lesson), but people saw looming threats to the GPL, only for the FSF to reply to the criticism with comments such as *Richard only cares about code running on his personal computer*, a reply that I saw on a mailing list in the '90s that I wish I would've saved.

When people pointed out that you could take software released under the terms of the GPL, put it behind a "gate" of a web page, thus never *distributing* the modified version and so not triggering the obligations assigned under the license, the Foundation considered that a deranged fantasy well beyond their scope---nobody would rationally put `gcc` behind a website, I believe they used as their example---and ignored it, until companies started using Free Software as part of web services.  When people pointed out that you could build a computer "appliance" with no direct user interface running Free Software and so dodge the obligations that way, the Foundation insisted that nobody would ever do such a thing and declared it beyond their scope, until TiVo did exactly that, releasing a Linux box (with GNU tools) and refusing to release their changes, because the license didn't actually oblige them to do it.  They shrugged their shoulders on firmware until smartphones needed "binary blobs" to access key parts of the hardware and the vendors wouldn't share the source code with Linux developers.

They ignored opportunities to embrace artists and writers, rejecting the idea that anybody should use the GPL for anything other than code, leaving that space open until Creative Commons launched with its poorly edited licenses.  As I recall, they even tried to convince [FLOSS Manuals](https://flossmanuals.org.uk/) to abandon their (early days?) approach of releasing their books under the terms of the GPL, so that the software the books talked about could include them directly if desired.

Lest it seem like I want to spend all the time talking about software or complaining about the FSF, Creative Commons has had similar problems, as I have mentioned over the years.  From the start, they poisoned their entire premise by creating incompatible licenses---the non-commercial and no-derivatives licenses---and haven't budged on keeping them around, even though many have found the [NC/ND license defenses lacking](https://jcolag.codeberg.page/qco/323-nc-and-nd.html) and [more lacking](https://freedomdefined.org/Licenses/NC).  And, in fact, their entire premise sounds more like the Open Source argument than anything else, that creators need flexibility to convince them to share, and besides, they [added a badge to the Free licenses](https://creativecommons.org/2012/12/17/next-steps-noncommercial-and-noderivatives-discussion/), since removed, by the way, assuming that they ever actually added them.  They keep [promising to keep the conversation going](https://creativecommons.org/2012/08/29/ongoing-discussions-noncommercial-and-noderivatives/), at the expense of ever finishing the conversation to *do* something about the problems.

Similarly, I'll talk about this more later, but when people kept coming to them for help or advice on the mass exploitation of "AI" companies hammering every server that they could find, so that they could sell shoddy replicas of other people's work, they first shrugged their shoulders and declared [LLM training Fair Use](https://creativecommons.org/2023/02/17/fair-use-training-generative-ai/), a conclusion that I agree with, but *ignores the problem* in a rush to tell us all to relax and learn to love the exploitation.  And they also chose to ignore that *training* may not violate copyright, but selling the ability to create copies of material with the serial numbers filed off seems pretty directly infringing.  When their response---actually *responses*, because they'd come back to defend their Fair Use analysis a couple of times, as if readers didn't understand the nuance the first times---met with anger, they concocted CC Signals, a non-binding request to AI scrapers, should they choose to read it, to credit or pay you (but not both) for your work as they indiscriminately slurp it down, on the chance that their hearts grow three sizes.

And more like the FSF, early licenses had gaping holes in them, such as their original poorly worded [termination clause](https://pluralistic.net/2022/01/24/a-bug-in-early-creative-commons-licenses-has-enabled-a-new-breed-of-superpredator/#more-6135:~:text=Crossing%20the%20Tees) that allowed people acting in bad faith to either try to extort money from creators or demand that creators [redirect attribution URLs as work changes hands]({% post_url 2022-02-20-shakedown %}).

For both organizations, it doesn't really matter what gaps the licenses have or how long it took them to respond to allies noticing the problems, though neither performed well on those fronts.  Rather, it matters that licenses *do* have gaps in coverage, and their lawyers don't proactively try to find the potential conflict points and abuses before adversaries might, putting us in danger.

Hell, Creative Commons still insists on using a "we all know it when we see it" definition of commercial activity, meaning that *any use* of material released under non-commercial terms carries the risk that the copyright holder might sue you for what they see as commercial activity.  And even their signaling about licenses implies a [linear spectrum of rights](https://creativecommons.org/cc-licenses/) "from most to least permissive," ignoring the fact that licenses only ever give permission, and those permissions don't necessarily make sense as more and less.

When I talk about the movement(s) under attack, the philosophical divisions mostly don't exist anymore---because everybody with sway over the movement has apparently decided to cede ground to the people who described *themselves* as not interested in principles---so we now have this never-ending stream of scammers looking for loopholes in licenses.  They want to buy the underlying copyrights to projects that have gotten some traction, so that they can make demands of people creating derivative works by exploiting those loopholes.  Or they want to coast through in general, such as the big nuisance, these days.

### (New) Ancient History

After all, I all but went dumpster-diving for what the OSI said almost twenty-five years ago.  Surely, none of this history lesson could have any bearing on the current state of affairs, right?

Also, kiddo, nobody has non-ironically used the term "Social Justice Warrior" in something like a decade.  And who gets it *wrong*?

Actually, I know who gets it wrong, the person who claims that we can't dispute AI's usefulness on the basis that it increases *maintainer workloads*.  In other words, he doesn't care that this loads up his fairly literal legacy with technical debt that'll take decades to sort out.  In that sense, it makes perfect sense that he can't even get his "you people actually care about making the world better" insult right.

I don't point this out to indicate some deep disappointment---though I admittedly don't know if I can trust an operating system kernel that prioritizes code quantity over code quality---but rather to point out that the *stop worrying about ethics* faction of Free Culture still influences people to make incredibly short-sighted decisions in public.

> Linux is not one of those anti-AI projects, and if somebody has issues with that, they can do the open-source thing and fork it.
>
> Or just walk away.

If you don't like the self-destructive policy, you can always run your own version of the project (without his funding) or give up on Linux entirely, according to him.  I suppose that we should credit him for personal growth, since he used to wish harm on people who disagreed with him.

## Artificial Inconvenience

As hinted above, in many ways, the industry behind Large Language Models combines both the other two types of attacks that I've talked about.

We can all see the institutional-types failing to meet the moment, for one.  Creative Commons started out with their technically correct but deeply unsatisfying training an AI qualifies as Fair Use, nothing to see, move along, and stop worrying about billionaires hammering your servers to harvest content.  When they saw people changing which licenses they use to avoid associating their names with the organization, they tried to appease everybody with [CC Signals]({% post_url 2025-06-29-signal-change %}), an even bigger flop putting the blame on the artists for not asking the billionaires nicely enough to not completely stomp on everything.  And right as it starts to look like the AI bubble might burst soon, they want [another six months](https://creativecommons.org/2026/05/13/from-signals-to-infrastructure-strengthening-the-commons-for-the-ai-era/) to help us make informed decisions while asking the AI companies to provide attribution, absolutely refusing to admit their misread on the situation.

For their part in the resistance, the [FSF sends people to conferences](https://www.fsf.org/news/fsf-serves-on-nist) on AI Safety, to "ensure the free software perspective is adequately represented."  For those who don't recognize the term, "AI safety" almost always means the questions of whether the LLM will respond in a way that follows corporate standards, and whether they can censor illegal or distasteful output before the user can see it, not anything related to Software Freedom, or really software at all.  Likewise, they have [concepts of a plan for Software Freedom in AI](https://www.fsf.org/news/fsf-is-working-on-freedom-in-machine-learning-applications), noting in an actual heading that *freedom may not equal justice*.  Way to stand up for principles, people...

Meanwhile, less visible, we have a similar schism that we saw with the Open Source folks.  In this case, we have people who see Culture Freedom as something to protect in the face of a system designed to grind up cultural artifacts and extrude them into answers.  But we also have people who see this destruction as *good*, because if Sam Altman and his ilk can make the case that copyright doesn't matter, they believe[^FthAfT] that will bring down the entire legal concept of intellectual property, so that we can all live in anarchy selling patched versions of Microsoft Word and *Star Wars* fan fiction to one another.  As I have seen it put, the licenses exist to work around copyright (a mischaracterization, I think), therefore we should want to destroy copyright more than we should want to create and share.

[^FthAfT]:  They believe this incorrectly.  Not only does training an LLM differ substantially from selling the specific output of an LLM in a Fair Use analysis, but I can't think of a single time in history where a large company has successfully broken a law and allowed a loosening of that law for we peons.

We also have a *third* group, though.  These people want [anti-AI licenses]({% post_url 2024-03-03-ai-licenses %}), licenses that will allow them to forbid the use of their work, and wanting stronger copyright law so that they can...I think that they believe that they can sue these enormous companies to get them to remove their work from the training data.  Anyway, they now want a public license to share their work, but where they have oversight over the sharing.

### Between the Cracks

And I should note that this all interacts.  Because everybody claiming a leadership role in the movement has spent a lot of its time ignoring threats, bickering over terminology, asking us to ignore silly issues like principles and justice, and [manipulating election results](https://codeberg.org/OSI-Concerns/election-results-2025), it has left plenty of room for the "source available" and "ethical" licenses, which play at Culture Freedom (usually Software Freedom), but don't actually extend or protect it, because they make a show of excluding people from who can share or why.

After all, if the OSI doesn't have any ethics, the FSF would rather yell at you for not using the term "GNU/Linux," and Creative Commons still thinks that the world has a use for a license that allows fans to do little more than look at a work, then what difference does it make if a license says that you can't compete with this company or another license says that you can't share if[^BppruJ] you believe that one can perform labor---such as creating Free Cultural Works---and not need payment for it?  What, do you object to the license on the grounds that your precious OSI won't add it to their list of approved licenses when they won't even announce full election results?  The awful behavior of these organizations turns their rejection of a non-Free license into a badge of honor.

[^BppruJ]:  The Hippocratic License---what I pick on, there---doesn't say so explicitly, but because nobody ever reads the documents that they reference, for some reason, their license demands that you abide by a basket of United Nations declarations.  And that sounds fine and even laudable, unless you actually *read through* those declarations and notice that the UN sees payment for all labor as a human right (ICESCR, Article 7) and demands protection from communal activity (Article 11).  Articles 18 and 23 ignore domestic abuse, wanting to protect families and give them wide latitude on what their children get taught.  The Universal Declaration of Human Rights says that you can't request asylum for identity-based prosecution or to get out of the path of a disaster.  If you don't comply with all that mess, then you *may not* use works made available under the terms of the Hippocratic License.  And that doesn't even go near their optional clauses that allow you to refuse to share with someone doing business with occupied Gaza but not land taken from Native Americans, and similar hypocrisies.

These represent more attacks, wearing away at the premise of Free Culture by suggesting that it either means whatever you need it to mean at any given time, or that it means nothing, and we should all move on from the concept.

## Coming Soon

Like with the [previous history post]({% post_url 2025-10-26-define-free-culture-history %}), I might have missed or---because this mostly happened in my adulthood, as opposed to the broader history---misremembered, but it should give some idea of how the current situation persists.  We keep looking for leaders to help us deal with the outcomes of licenses conflicting with our common values (which we can all see, even though nobody has published a chart or given a talk), and many feel betrayed by those organizations as they beg for donations while telling us (in effect) to embrace the bad outcomes.

It may also explain why I don't look to them (or anybody, really) as leaders of the movement.  They each have their uses, and we should probably engage with them on those uses.  But expecting them to identify future (or even present) threats and protect the community from them seems like a losing game.  Asking them (or anybody) to lead seems like a losing game.

And once again, we'll end here, so that this doesn't become some unhinged manifesto, as if that didn't already happen, here.  But at some point---I hope not in another four months, but definitely not too soon, because these increasingly take time to think through---we'll talk about the next layer in, what we as creators can do for our audiences, despite the organizations not seeming to move forward.  Or maybe I'll change my mind and go in a different direction.  As I said, these posts have started to take more work to pull together as they stretch further into the hypothetical.
