* Ignore for ToC
{:toc}

It occurs to me that, for all the time that I spend *implying* that you should probably walk away from big companies (technology-wise and not) for independent and especially Free Culture counterparts, I haven't laid out the whys and hows.  I vaguely got into this in my post recounting [my own move to Linux]({% post_url 2024-02-18-linux %}) and broad advice based on that, and tried guiding people through [Mastodon]({% post_url 2022-11-20-mastodon %}) and then [Mastodon again]({% post_url 2024-10-20-most-mastodon %})---leaning more heavily in the latter on improving the experience after you get there---but I haven't pushed over to the broader idea, even though I may gesture at it often.

![A small fox with a sword and shield standing at the edge of a rocky protrusion as a giant Chromium icon approaches](/blog/assets/fighting-open-web.png "Probably the wrong color scheme for this context, but it seems close enough...")

This time---and most of my regular readers can probably already see this coming and might even have at least one foot in this world---I want to get more explicit and talk things through in more detail than "maybe try it."

## The Radioactive Kaiju in the Room

Brace yourselves.

*You should walk away from doing business with big companies*, at least to the extent that you can.

Yeah, I know, {% emoji exploding head %} we have the shock of the millennium, here that I would ever say such a thing.

{% cw Too many links incoming to sensibly annotate copyright licensing without ending up with a sea of copyright symbols.  Assume that applies from here on... %}

Regardless of the predictability, we can all see why I suggest this, right?  Big Tech openly wants to [replace their entire range of software offerings](https://techcrunch.com/2026/05/19/google-search-as-you-know-it-is-over/) with an LLM chatbot with a [for entertainment purposes only](https://www.tomshardware.com/tech-industry/artificial-intelligence/microsoft-says-copilot-is-for-entertainment-purposes-only-not-serious-use-firm-pushing-ai-hard-to-consumers-tells-users-not-to-rely-on-it-for-important-advice) disclaimer while trying to get us [addicted to the technology](https://www.404media.co/microsoft-wants-to-make-people-addicted-to-scout-its-new-ai-assistant-internal-documents-reveal/) and also throwing their hands up on security if [your attacker asks the support bot nicely](https://www.bitdefender.com/en-us/blog/hotforsecurity/hackers-hack-instagram-ask-meta-ai).  Manufacturers quietly shut down operations and [license their brands](https://www.worseonpurpose.com/) to anybody willing to pay the fee to license their reputation, regardless of quality.  Big retailers make deals that none of their suppliers can allow another [retailer to undercut them](https://oag.ca.gov/news/press-releases/attorney-general-bonta-exposes-amazon-price-fixing-scheme-driving-costs), while they take [increasingly large cuts of sales](https://www.marketplacepulse.com/articles/amazon-takes-a-50-cut-of-sellers-revenue), thereby raising costs everywhere at once.  [Media vanishes across platforms](https://www.polygon.com/entertainment/23313193/hbo-max-infinity-train-summer-camp-island-ok-ko-watch/) as companies decide to change direction, sometimes [even if the user "purchased" it](https://www.npr.org/sections/thetwo-way/2009/07/amazon_kindles_lawsuit_for_del.html) and [delete purchases again](https://www.techdirt.com/2026/07/15/sony-deletes-a-bunch-more-movies-from-the-accounts-of-people-who-bought-them/) (in case you thought that the other example seemed like old news), while trying to make sure that you [have no other purchasing options](https://blog.playstation.com/2026/07/01/physical-disc-production-ending-in-january-2028-for-new-games-releasing-on-playstation-consoles/).  Other companies have decided that they have the [authority to arrest you](https://www.npr.org/2026/07/10/nx-s1-5886113/waymo-police-privacy-driverless-autonomous-vehicles), or [appoint themselves judges](https://medium.com/@bjax_/a-tale-of-unwanted-disruption-my-week-without-amazon-df1074e3818b) of your behavior.  And we haven't even gotten to the overt partisan politics, with [CEOs cozying up to tin-pot dictators](https://www.techpolicy.press/tech-policy-is-on-the-front-line-of-fascism-vs-democracy-pick-a-side/) and [openly talking about anti-democratic plans](https://newrepublic.com/post/207693/palantir-ceo-karp-disrupting-democratic-power), or their replacement of employees with LLMs leading to catastrophic failures of their infrastructure.  And I could go on *endlessly*, here, because a consolidating market increasingly assumes that it has a *captured* market, and so can do whatever conveniences it at the expense of customers.  I mean, what, do you plan to move all your documents or change search engines because [someone wants to upend the entire ecosystem](https://debug.com/how/)---and I mean the literal ecosystem here, the space where we breathe and grow food, not some social or technological analogy to an ecosystem---as a fun weekend experiment?  After all, you stuck with them this far, so the boiling frog has already started snowboarding down the slippery slope...or something.

In short, they all think that they've grown [too big to care](https://www.legaldive.com/news/Lina-Khan-Jon-Stewart-FTC-monopoly-interview-antirust-law-amazon-apple/712174/), so they don't care.  This happened because of market consolidation.  In almost every country, nearly every industry has narrowed its competition to generally between two and six giant corporations, all with overlapping stockholders and often having the same employees and executives bounce between them, meaning that they all inevitably make similar if not identical decisions.

What hold do they actually have on people, though?  In a lot of ways, it sounds a lot like my recurring discussion of social constructs:  The power that these companies have *exists*---like the power in money, countries, race, gender, TCP/IP, and so forth all exist---but distinct from bacteria, Mount Everest, and gravity, they exist only because and to the extent that we participate in keeping them in the public consciousness.  People caught pneumonia long before anybody believed in bacteria, but if people generally stop trusting or caring about dollars or Euros or yuan (or all of them), then they fade away, other than the physical artifacts becoming collectibles[^m1CUn7].  In the same way, corporate power over us requires our buy-in.

[^m1CUn7]:  I forget where, and you all know not to come to my fuzzy memory for financial advice for any reason, but I vaguely remember reading that paper currency makes one of the best long-term (as in multi-century) investments, because it routinely gets used, lost, and destroyed, then undergoes general degradation, so any samples of Song Dynasty 交子/jiaozi that your family hypothetically stuffed under their sleeping mats a thousand years ago would've outpaced pretty much anything else that they might have bought *with* the money.

I suppose that makes it more hegemonic---another recurring theme in the blog, I suppose---than socially constructed.  Corporate power needs you to support it, and it increasingly does everything that it can to coerce that support from you, so that you technically consent but not entirely voluntarily.  Or, rather, both terms probably apply, a hegemonic social construct, probably most resembling gender.

This might help explain why some of us react so poorly to others who make a brand a central part of their identity.  I don't *only* mean people who dress up as characters from their favorite billion-dollar franchise, but also the people with clothing prominently displaying the manufacturer's logo, or (something that never fails to provoke a visceral bad reaction from me) people who customize their cars to make the brand more prominent, whether through personalized license plates with the model or adding decals with the brand.  People often dismiss that judgment as classism, but if you think of it less about the people and more about the phenomenon, then it looks more like seeing the hegemony in action, literally manufacturing consent to their power over the market.

Because of that, we *can* wander away from large companies whenever we feel like it.  However, it doesn't happen on its own, sadly.  We actually need to work at walking away.

In fact, in the spirit of full disclosure, since I started writing this post, I have started to go through this process myself, and maybe my example will help illustrate what I mean, here.  Therefore, let me lay out my...actually, a fairly old problem, but one that I could convince myself to ignore until recently, so it feels like a new problem.  And then I'll rethink that problem through the rest of this post, so that I can talk about the issues in parallel to showing how I have worked and will work them out.

First, though, let's get a critical idea out of the way.

## The Why

Because of how this usually gets discussed, I should probably talk a bit about the motivations for distancing yourself from patronizing large companies, before we get to that problem that I talked about.

You absolutely **will not** hurt the companies by going elsewhere.  The amount of money that you bring to these companies can get masked by a rounding error, unless you control a huge corporate account or can convince millions (maybe tens or hundreds of millions) of people to join you.  You "vote with your wallet" downtown when you decide whether to eat lunch at the family diner or the hole-in-the-wall Mexican restaurant, or which florist to pick up a bouquet in a rush to a fancy dinner at someone's house, not when one company sits on hundreds of billions of dollars and your wallet has maybe hundreds of dollars in it on a good day.  Apple cares even less about your protest than politicians care about your third-party protest vote, at best a tiny change to a spreadsheet that nobody looks at.

Instead, you get away from these awful companies for your own safety and health, whether physical or digital.

Look at that short list of problems again.  They want you addicted *and* forced to use technologies that they admit don't do anything useful.  They degrade quality, give up your private information, drive up prices, prevent you from owning what you buy, oppose democracy, and more.  And they see you as their product to sell, even if you paid for the privilege.  You may not stop them from harming the world by taking a little stand, but you *can* reduce the harm that they can do to you by getting away[^J-DNsR].

[^J-DNsR]:  If it makes this framing more concrete, think of it like dealing with an abusive partner.  Parallel to market consolidation, you can almost always count on isolating someone from the support network outside the relationship as a prelude to domestic violence; whether suggesting that someone feels like a bad influence or moving far away, they want to reduce or compromise the competition that might expose the abuse as abnormal.  And in many cases, you can't fix or stop the perpetrator in general, so you need to leave their presence before they make it impossible.

And who knows?  If enough of us do this, then maybe *that* limits their power.  But don't expect it, because that requires a lot of us and a plan for when they run to governments to require their products or services.

## Real Example, Watching Media

Now let's get to the version of this that I recently needed to (start to) face.

To my frequent chagrin, I spend a not-inconsequential amount of time in front of the television, probably too much, even as I watch less.  OK, actually, I probably stand at something close to a thirty-degree angle about eight feet away, behind my laptop on a counter, where I actually spend most of my time indoors, but I usually have the television on, so that seems close enough for our purposes, I think.

Since I [dropped my cable subscription]({% post_url 2021-04-04-cord-cut %}) about five years ago, everything happens across some form of streaming, like most other people these days.  And to mediate the streaming, I use a Roku device.  I don't have much personal love for Roku, mind you.  It sends absurd amounts of data to company servers, and they keep looking for new places to cram ads.  But they do the job of getting the thing that I want to see onto the screen reasonably well, and so far, I have found them a better compromise than (for example) a Google device.

Recently, though, we got the announcement that [Fox Corporation will acquire Roku](https://newsroom.roku.com/news/2026/06/fox-corporation-to-acquire-roku-inc-/ishjvytb-1781489389).  Fox Corporation, for those who don't recall, represents the remnants of the business left behind in selling 20th/21st Century Fox to Disney about seven years ago, now consisting primarily of the Murdoch-helmed right-wing news, opinion, and sports broadcast, cable, and streaming operations.  And as you can guess, this presents me with three new problems, one admittedly speculative.

- While I can justify lowering my standards to deal with Roku as an independent company, giving a multi-billion-dollar corporation any (more) of my data or money on that scale doesn't sit well with me.
- While Roku had already started dropping hints that it would really prefer that users watch right-wing content---almost certainly for the same reasons that I've talked about since the early days of the blog, that it provokes fear/anger, and so makes us more susceptible to ads---having a conduit primarily *for* right-wing content makes me uncomfortable.
- Given that Fox gets most of its money from carrier fees after worming its way into cable bundles (the importance of their sports arm), and this purchase almost certainly stemming from the recent drop in household penetration of cable television, this now puts them in a position where they have the authority to decide who gets onto their platform, and I don't know if I trust them to behave well, given how many carrier fee disputes they've had with cable and satellite companies over the years.

I don't expect to throw the existing device out yet, but I want to start lining up the options for replacements before the device inevitably fails.  To make sure that we all have the same vision, here, I mean a "streaming, digital media player," a computer "appliance" with dedicated software running that, in turn, runs apps connecting to different streaming services to deliver their (pardon the term) content to the television screen.  Increasingly, the manufacturer builds such a device into the television, with projected future ad revenue subsidizing the retail price of the TV.

I want a new device of that sort.

### Your Version

Now, your story (or stories) probably differs from mine in key ways, such as the company or companies involved, the timeline, the stakes, and the like, but you can probably see the same broad shape in your story.  Maybe you want off corporate social media, or an escape from subscription-based software.  Someone else might want to build their streaming audience without hoping that some company's opaque algorithm will remember to notify their fans.  Another person might want to stop getting their groceries, rides, and pretty much everything else through billionaire-owned "platforms" that don't actually do anything but extract money.  Or maybe we all realize that jumping from company to company on any issues along these lines means (at best) postponing the problem by a couple of years, rather than solving the problem.

The specifics don't really matter, because all these stories start with accepting a vague compromise a while ago, but having a moment where the compromise becomes a significantly worse trade-off, leaving you interested in finding a replacement.  And you solve the problem (well) by using the same approximate process that I'll try to apply.  I use the replace-the-Roku example because I need to think it through anyway, and it touches on everything that I need to talk about in this post and its follow-up.

Therefore, let's get into this.

## Actual Needs

Do you know what question I have come to hate more than all others on social media, even more than asking how to get Creative Commons or the Free Software Foundation to release an [anti-AI license]({% post_url 2024-03-03-ai-licenses %})?  I hate this question, in its myriad phrasings:

> What Free Software product should my group use to replace Discord?

I have come to hate this question, because I have found every person posing the question angrily rejecting every proposed answer.  No, this system doesn't organize rooms right.  That system doesn't have the right kind of moderation.  The one over there has broken video calls.  Something else has too much audio latency.  Another one looks too arcane for the group to use.  No customization of this narrow sort over here.  No native app for this device over there.  Oh, well, since the Free Software people won't give a straight answer to the question, then they'll have to stay with Discord.

The problem comes from the fact that Discord (like every product) fills many different purposes to many different people, many of those uses emerging over time by taking advantage of some minor contextual convenience.  The software has a pile of disconnected features, and different groups make completely different subsets of those features part of their culture.  I reluctantly still follow (but don't really participate in) conversations on four Discord servers; the relevant communities could replace almost all of them with an [IRC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IRC) server with an occasional [Mumble](https://www.mumble.info/) call, or *maybe* needing something as complex as a [Matrix](https://matrix.org/) space.  If you still use Discord, your communities probably don't fit that profile.  You might need the ability for people to constantly branch off into video calls or create custom emoji or the like.  Therefore, we both have different views of what the software *does*, meaning that nobody can answer what you can use as a replacement.

As the first step of getting away from big corporations, then, **list what you actually need** from a replacement, instead of assuming that you'll stumble across an exact replica or a candidate with coincidentally the exact subset of features that you happen to need.  Your list should include absolute requirements, anything that you'd consider a strong selling point if available, and "you wouldn't say no" features.  Or if you want to think about this in terms of formal specifications as shorthand, you want the verbs *must* for your minimum, *should* for your preferences, and *may* for everything else that you might find useful or convenient someday but don't necessarily care about now.

### Media Box Requirements

For my case, I definitely have my list of requirements, fairly explicit, in the form of the TV apps that I use regularly.

For me, a viable replacement for a Roku device **must** give me access to media on my [Jellyfin](https://jellyfin.org/) server, [Hoopla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoopla_%28digital_media_service%29), [Kanopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kanopy), and [PBS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PBS).  Those four apps represent the overwhelming majority of where I watch media[^EJCYaM], as [newsletter readers](https://buymeacoffee.com/jcolag/posts/71215) have probably gleaned over the years.  I use each almost every day, so if I can't get all four, then the system fails for me.

[^EJCYaM]:  This also may give some impression of what I meant early by my probably spending too much time watching television.  Hoopla and Kanopy credit-based borrowing systems both usually average to watching about one "thing" per day on each, and most shows on PBS run for an hour.  Add in whatever I watch on my own media server, and we've already probably hit four hours per day.  But then Hoopla and Kanopy also have "free" material each month, which I often check out under the assumption that they selected it for some reason.  Add a few *Star Trek* episodes per month for the blog, the fact that PBS will occasionally drop episodes of a bunch of shows at once including a multi-hour **Great Performances** show---as I edit this footnote, the television has hit hour-three of **I Puritani**, in fact---and the occasional video for the Free Culture Book Club posts, and you can see how this adds up to an uncomfortable amount of time watching television, even as somebody who "doesn't really watch television."

Oh, I almost forgot.  The device **must** also sport a [10-foot user interface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/10-foot_user_interface) on the television, or something in that vein, with some kind of remote control.  All my other requirements (and more) work fine as websites, so without this requirement, I could plug one of my servers into the TV's HDMI port and use the web browser from my usual viewing position.  But to do that, I'd need to set up a mouse and/or keyboard workable from across the room, and that feels miserable in the limited space.  More on that later, but the digression solidifies the need for a television-ready user interface.

After that list, any decent solution also **should** work as something that I fully own, without needing to worry about the fate of yet another company---well, except for the streaming providers, who need to continue to exist for me to use them---ideally as Free Software that I install somewhere[^BFs8dh] and plug that device into the television.  It **should** also support [Peacock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peacock_%28streaming_service%29), and let's say one other source (beyond Peacock, I mean) of <dfn title="Free Ad-supported Streaming Television">FAST</dfn> channels.

[^BFs8dh]:  I specify installing the replacement myself, because Roku, Google, and other devices "run on" Free Software, but proprietary code ties them to their devices, complete with tracking and planned obsolescence.  I care less about the specific license than giving me some distance and insulation from any vendors.

If I can find a fully Free Software solution, then I really only need to worry about the streaming providers keeping their apps available and up-to-date, once I have it all running.  I have a Peacock subscription through something else, and while I check it occasionally, only actually watch a couple of shows per year[^eveE4o], and occasionally one of their FAST channels, so I probably wouldn't miss those rare shows if I needed to drop them.  But despite that minimal use, since I technically pay for it through some other service, I'd like to have the *ability* to make use of it.  And because Peacock has a fairly narrow selection, and they sometimes don't work, another bunch of FAST channels helps when I don't want to watch something specific and only want mildly interesting "noise" on in the background.  If a system nails all three of these and my absolute requirements, then I don't care what happens to Roku or any competitor, but if I need to compromise, sacrificing one of these won't hurt *too* much.

[^eveE4o]:  I won't bother to list *which* shows, but since the start of 2023 when I made it more feasible to search my nightly "diary" notes, I count twelve shows, four of which fall more in the category of "classics" or at least old, and most not continuing.  You can add to that a whopping four films and three background pieces on one of them, a certain major movie musical about witches in a fantasy world.

Finally, the system **may** support other streaming services.  I might eventually want to go back to Ovid, Netflix, Disney+, Apple TV, or similar for a month to catch up on shows; honestly, I would go back to Ovid full-time, at some point, if they had the sort of material that I watch, because they seem like good people and catching the service on frequent sale only costs a couple of dollars per month.  Most of this probably won't happen anytime soon, but I might consider it for various reasons ranging from a discount too good to pass up at a convenient time to management and the political situation changing.  And if my solution would support any of those apps, it'd make it far more appealing, to the point where I could probably recommend it to other people who had similar concerns about their current streaming box[^li8JEQ].

[^li8JEQ]:  Getting *far* ahead of myself, considering that I don't have anything like the infrastructure for this and (probably) not much interest, but if this became a sufficiently usable platform, then one could sell it.  And with sales numbers in-hand, such an enterprise could approach the commercial streaming services to make their apps available, making it possible to challenge the proprietary boxes.  That couldn't happen without already ticking most of the boxes, though.

Does that make sense?  If any of the first batch of conditions fails, then I'll need to find multiple solutions, and so might as well give up and use a web browser.  Provided that multiple solutions somehow check the first batch of requirements, then the second batch of conditions will help distinguish between them, where if I need some proprietary blob for [Widevine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Widevine) nonsense or can only use Peacock through the web browser, I probably won't really worry, but one solution that *also* gives me some of that second list will better fill the role than others.  And the last list, I don't really care about, other than to distinguish between any imagined solutions that fill both the *musts* and the *shoulds*, and if I want to recommend my ultimate solution to people who have different tastes than I do.

### Flexibility

I should note that, even as rigorous as this looks, I can imagine the categories feeling a bit fluid at times.  For example, many FAST channels common to multiple apps run as <dfn title="Digital Rights Management, software that attempts to prevent unauthorized copying">DRM</dfn>-free streams, meaning that tracking down the URLs would allow for assembling an app with at least the channels that I'll watch.  In fact, Jellyfin might have the ability to manage these already[^79ArlC], though I'll probably lose the schedule showing what they plan to play when.  Likewise, PBS doesn't use DRM, meaning that (in the worst case) I could rig up a script to watch for new episodes of shows that I watch, find the video URL, and pull it to Jellyfin until I watch it, the streaming equivalent of a VCR, provided that I delete the files after I finish with them.  That makes those requirements less urgent, and I could potentially find others, if I looked carefully.  Likewise, I *want* to make Free Software a "must," but I realize that licensing agreements with studios (DRM in particular) will make that nearly impossible.

[^79ArlC]:  Checking this out a bit later but before publishing the post, I discovered that many people out there compile the lists as variations on "free IPTV providers," I suppose a technically true description.  In Jellyfin, I can get this sort of thing working by adding one or more "TV Tuners" of *M3U* type, and providing one of the lists of IPTV channels, or creating my own list of what I find there.  Many of the channels have trouble in Jellyfin, such as geographic blocking, failing to load, failing to stay loaded, buffering problems, or plain old uninteresting to me, which makes navigation a bit cumbersome unless I carefully pick and choose to create my own M3U file.  And it wouldn't surprise me to discover that these lists will need periodic refreshing, wrecking that last idea.  But the point stands that, if I need to drop the existing apps that provide these channels, then I do have the option of building something similar for myself in Jellyfin, even with a brittle-but-working program guide.  That doesn't necessarily *remove* the FAST channels from my requirements list, but it absolutely bumps them to the bottom of my **should** list, because it looks Jellyfin comes really close to what I want.

Similarly, while I'd call a remote control---as in a small dedicated device to navigate to only relevant elements in the user interface---an *eventual* necessity, I could almost certainly live with something less-custom for a while.  But if I start thinking about this too much, then I'll start trying to solve the wrong part of the problem at this stage.
