This may seem simplistic and superficial, and if not that, then almost tautological---and at least has the virtue of relative brevity, for once---but I want to suggest that the last decades of political positioning by those in the United States with progressive views has given conservatives and regressives the space to metastasize into what we see today.  I didn't make the connection until recently, but I see two ideas bracketing this.

On one hand, way back when I posted on Quora and characterized somebody's nationalistic, ethnocentric, or otherwise chauvinistic as right-wing nonsense, I'd consistently get the same response, no matter who I responded to.

> Actually, I consider myself more of a centrist.

Billionaires say this sort of thing a lot, too.

![A crowd in a parking lot looking at tape marking off a large tic-tac-toe board, with an X painted (digitally, by me) painted in blue in the center square](/blog/assets/5734973533_6aa31cd008_o.png "X marks the spot, I suppose")

On the other hand, I don't have a quote or paraphrased sentiment for it, but we all know that you don't need to go far into the comments in progressive-leaning media or social media spheres before finding someone railing against "centrist Democrats" as the *real* problem, not the actual fascists who insist that the Nazis and slaveholders didn't get a fair shake in history.

## My Own Failure

I should mention that I failed to connect either of these to anything.  For right-wing people claiming the role of centrist, I rolled my eyes, because *you* don't get to say where your political beliefs fall, because everybody else's political beliefs decide that positioning.  And for the latter, I have rolled my eyes at a political movement seemingly allergic to the future, because they'd rather enable electoral wins by people who want them dead than allow the elections of people who might occasionally compromise on some points in order to push others through.

And occasionally, you'd get silly comments about how "center" in the United States looks like "far-right" in progressive countries.  We probably don't hear as often anymore, because it no longer seems true.

And, as I've said since the start of the blog, I tend not to describe myself, because I don't believe that I get that right.  I certainly don't have the right to decide what qualifies as social progress.  People who hear and read me get to characterize my views, not me.  In that sense, I think that I have participated in the problem that I want to outline, here.

## The Second Coming

In thinking this post out, "random" lines of poetry kept coming to mind as possible titles.  It took me a while to give it any thought---I constantly brainstorm titles up until a post goes out, so I didn't find it surprising---but it finally dawned on me that they all came from the *same* poem, and...it looks a bit relevant.

> Turning and turning in the widening gyre  
> The falcon cannot hear the falconer;  
> Things fall apart; the center cannot hold;  
> Mere anarchy is loosed upon the world,  
> The blood-dimmed tide is loosed, and everywhere  
> The ceremony of innocence is drowned;  
> The best lack all conviction, while the worst  
> Are full of passionate intensity.  
> 
> Surely some revelation is at hand;  
> Surely the Second Coming is at hand.  
> The Second Coming! Hardly are those words out  
> When a vast image out of Spiritus Mundi  
> Troubles my sight: a waste of desert sand;  
> A shape with lion body and the head of a man,  
> A gaze blank and pitiless as the sun,  
> Is moving its slow thighs, while all about it  
> Wind shadows of the indignant desert birds.  
> The darkness drops again but now I know  
> That twenty centuries of stony sleep  
> Were vexed to nightmare by a rocking cradle,  
> And what rough beast, its hour come round at last,  
> Slouches towards Bethlehem to be born?

You've all heard it before.  And yes, I adjusted William Butler Yeats's spelling to modern American standards.  He published in 1920, and died eighty-seven years ago, so the public domain rules {% wiki The_Second_Coming_%28poem%29|*The Second Coming*|en %}, and I have a spell-checker to get annoyed at when it flags ordinary issues...

More seriously, actually read this one through in a modern context.  I don't know if Yeats meant it---he claims to have worried about Christianity's values getting replaced, giving this a purely Biblical interpretation, but I'll note that he had the Gilded Age on the horizon---it seems fairly descriptive of our political situation, no?  We lost the center, and loosed anti-governance libertarians on the world.  *The best lack conviction*, while the worst indulge in their passions.  And we got the "rough beast," not in some religious sense, but in the form of turning governments into money-harvesting devices for the wealthy.

That one line keeps echoing, though.  *Things fall apart; the center cannot hold*.  We have certainly failed to hold the center...

## Meanwhile

It also seems worth wondering where this political positioning comes from.  I mentioned in my post about the [stresses that the far-right has put Democracy under]({% post_url 2026-06-28-years-modern %}) that organizations such as Ad Fontes organize media outlets based on how three people---one who their testing identified as conservative, one liberal, and one centrist---react to the work, and how it both defines what qualifies as the political spectrum and also reinforces it.

And those sorts of activities reinforce their reading of the political spectrum, even though it doesn't match how actual *people* feel.  When you look at reliable polling on issues, you find that a clear majority of Americans want higher taxes on the wealthy, gun control, few if any restrictions on abortion, campaign finance restrictions, backing off on AI, clean energy, ethical codes for government officials, an end to changing clocks, backing FEMA, independent universities, protected National Parks, safe food, foreign aid, backing NATO, conversion therapy bans, vaccinations, housing assistance, paid sick leave, an end to gerrymandering, early voting, and a lot more.  Those issues *cross party lines*, if imperfectly.  When people say that two-thirds to three-quarters of Americans agree on one of those topics, respondents from the parties might differ up to ten percent, but still come in as a majority opinion.

It makes you wonder, then, how Republican policies---which a tiny minority of Americans approve of---get called "center-right" or similar.  I've described this as heavily subsidizing them in the Marketplace of Ideas™ or intellectual gerrymandering, because it has exactly those sorts of effects.  But it also creates a fictional balance where the real world has none, giving the impression that ideas such as "if they need to work, then humans deserve a living wage" represent a bizarre intrusion into serious political thought.

I increasingly suspect that it happens because people whose ideas represent the majority opinion would rather describe themselves as extremists, leaving billionaires, bigots, and their followers to stake out centrism, and making people feel like they need to compromise between ordinary ideas and clear nonsense.

It also leaves plenty of room for people to radicalize further towards fascism.  I mean, if you worry about communism for whatever reason and see the billionaires calling themselves centrists with no pushback, then it seems reasonable to believe that you want *even more* authoritarianism and segregation than Apartheid Clyde advocates, if only to balance the seesaw and make sure that we don't slide in the direction where we all need to treat people with respect.  If we instead insisted that the center included [taxing the wealthy]({% post_url 2026-04-12-fair-share %}), preserving the ecosystem that we need to live, and bodily autonomy, then that positioning looks incredibly silly, and allows space for more progressive thought to grow.

## Broader Story

Granted, I don't believe that the build of fascism-curious weirdos derives *entirely* from this sort of semantic positioning, by any means.  The stickiness of a term like "neoliberalism" made a term like *liberal* toxic, playing into the far-right move of treating it like a slur.  The right quickly adopted the idea of talking about other topics as a way of smuggling their political views and calls to action into mainstream conversation[^7XjjQu], while everybody else only wants to talk politics in political discussions.  Austerity, lax gun control, and other targeted attacks on our safety have convinced people that not only do they live bad lives, but that their lives can't possibly get better, so it doesn't matter what happens to the rest of us.  And as mentioned, we have a media ecosystem that prioritizes keeping right-wing views in the discourse, no matter how discredited, debunked, or outright dangerous they've proven.

[^7XjjQu]:  If you want something more useful than looking for "the Joe Rogan of the left," learn that lesson.  Rogan himself doesn't matter.  His "interview everybody" approach doesn't matter.  His ability to use different topics *as a pretext* to talk about politics, build the audience's outrage at the political topic, and give them something to do about it, *that* matters, because it turns listeners into a politically engaged class without their noticing the change.

I think that the positioning does make a huge difference, though.  It gives fascists credibility as a natural part of the political spectrum and gives them plenty of space to claim that they represent an average, even though virtually nobody agrees with them.

## So What

Yeah, I don't have much to this, except where I came in:  Start describing widely agreed-on, common-sense solutions as the centrist position.  Claim that space and force everybody else out of it, so that they need to describe themselves as aberrations of some sort.

Like I said in my post about [calling Republicans *weird*]({% post_url 2024-09-01-weird %}), the entire philosophy relies on having everybody think of them as unremarkable[^E34ffE] and unthreatening.  As soon as you point out that something doesn't seem quite right about them or point out the direct connection between their policies and the harm done to actual enumerable people, they panic.

[^E34ffE]:  I hesitate to actually recommend this, but think about Donald Trump, if only for a moment.  Or rather, think about the image that he projects---how his followers see him---in the context of writing for television.  What archetype do you have there?  You have the '90s-or-later sitcom father:  Dim, lazy, well past his prime, unhealthy, and desperate to relive his glory days, but part of the cast of picture-perfect family members.  He pitches himself as the "Everyman," perfectly ordinary, even though anybody can see the obscene wealth giving him his start, the illegal actions, the racism, the misogyny, and so forth.  You'd get someone like Kevin James to play the character.

You and I, if someone calls us weird, we giggle and say something like *you don't know the half of it*.  When someone tells us that our approaches to solving problems cause demonstrable harm, we apologize and fix our approach.  The right-wing, though, has an image to maintain, though.  If the Patriot Front clowns need to admit that they don't speak for the majority or the "center," then their militia cosplay makes them...silly, *deeply* silly.  If billionaires don't agree with normal people about anything, then letting them have a ton of money to shovel into elections seems ill-advised.  And the same goes for politicians, podcasters, and your neighbor.  Therefore, if you differ from them, you must do so because of foreign influences, or for cynical reasons, or whatever, because unlike the rest of us, they can't admit to any abnormality on their part.

The center needs to describe where the center of the country's ideals live, not in the middle of some hypothetical space that exists to make fascism seem plausible.  Retake the center and hold that position.  Let someone more radical than you describe themselves as far-left.
