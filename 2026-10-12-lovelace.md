**Correction**:  Ada Lovelace Day falls on the second Tuesday of the month.  I don't know how I got that one wrong, considering that I actually have the day on my holiday calendar.

Today, due in no small part to {% wiki Suw Charman-Anderson %}'s organizing that I actually remember, we celebrate {% wiki Ada Lovelace Day %}, in turn celebrating one of history's great weirdos {% wiki Ada Lovelace %}.  Lord Byron's only legitimate daughter trained in math from an early age in hopes of staving off her father's wacky behavior---probably supporting the working classes---Ada grew up fantasizing about building flying robot horses, had some connection to almost any early 1800s British luminary that you can think of, broke norms, got involved in gambling, tried to build a mathematical model of emotions, and (oh, right) made Charles Babbage's Difference Engine work approachable to a broad audience, and wrote what we now recognize as the first workable[^j-wk9u] computer program.

[^j-wk9u]:  People, mostly men who have an anti-feminist agenda, periodically try to undermine Lovelace's work by pointing to Babbage describing algorithms in his unpublished work, thereby in their eyes making him the "real" first programmer.  By that measurement, though, an anonymous Sumerian from four-and-a-half thousand years ago qualifies as the first known programmer for carving a description of division into a clay tablet, which I think that we can all see as pretty silly, because describing an algorithm to people differs from implementing one in a way that it works mechanically.  By contrast, anyone with a passing familiarity with programming can recognize Lovelace's Note G (computing Bernoulli Numbers) as a complete program, with its operations applied in steps to discrete variables in a loop.

Oh, and she did all that and more before dying at the far-too-young age of thirty-six.  It seems rather fitting to remind people of that, given that we lost {% wiki Margaret_Hamilton_(software_engineer)|Margaret Hamilton %} last week, who lived a much longer life, but similarly hit a bunch of astonishing achievements at a similarly early age.

![Ada Lovelace near the end of her life playing the piano](/blog/assets/Ada-Lovelace-in-1852.png "Yeah, she played the piano, too...")

And as I point out every year, the second Tuesday of October in the United States *also* focuses on a completely fictional version of Christopher Columbus, the man (in reality) so inept and brutal that the Spanish Imperial Crown took time out of their Inquisition and brutal conquest of the Americas to inform him that he had stepped so far over the line that he'd rot in prison, where he died still shrieking about how he *did too* find a secret way to India, while writing out endless apocalyptic prophecies of events that would show him as more than a wealthy proto-fascist.  Why does the United States celebrate such trash?  Nation-building required symbols, and other countries claimed all the non-jackass explorers as their own, so writers such as Washington Irving created a *fictional* Columbus who enlightened Europe on Earth's spherical nature...despite the teeny fact that everybody already knew since at least the Ancient Greeks.

And with all that, on to the week's projects.  And I'll warn you now that I went a bit rogue...

## Mini-Server, Part 36

OK, not really "server," again, since I mostly looked at getting user-side devices running, but I don't feel like trying to think of a new classification...

Because I have increasingly wanted to start moving away from Linux, but don't want to disrupt everything else that I work on with rebooting, reformatting, and so forth, I headed over to my pile of no-longer-usable laptops.  They come in two varieties, both feasibly fixed, hence my keeping them around.

- Broken keyboards, which probably wouldn't take long to fix, but requires sourcing a replacement and hoping that it doesn't need special tools.
- Damaged power connectors, also fixable but requires a bit more care so that the fix doesn't foul up polarity or become even more fragile.

Actually, don't I have an example from early in the blog of a fragile short-term solution to the latter?

![A hole drilled into the back of a laptop case, with a power cord dangling out](/blog/assets/laptop-cord-hole.png "What could go wrong, right...?")

As I pointed out [when I made this mediocre fix]({% post_url 2021-12-06-nicholas %}) almost five years ago, I probably should've bought a hot glue gun and secured the plug in its original location, but that didn't occur to me until afterward.  Before that, I have another machine where the power adapter snapped one of the wires, and I tried to solder that together, buying me another few weeks.

Anyway, since I bought a USB keyboard last week, I leaned towards the first category, which currently only really includes one of the early Dell XPS 13 units that shipped with Ubuntu, which I splurged for in order to help encourage manufacturers to see the non-Windows market as much as because I needed a laptop and had the money.  The left-hand side of the keyboard stopped working, so---as hinted---set it aside for a spare machine until I had the patience to shop for a replacement keyboard and try to install it without random pieces of the tiny laptop falling out.

Plugging it in, it started, but Ubuntu complained about disk errors.  Fine, because I planned to replace it, anyway.  Oh, and it at least claims that the battery has completely died, which similarly doesn't really matter for a few experiments.

I picked what I'd call the top-two operating systems that have strong anti-AI policies and *don't* have builds that would let me test on the Pinebook or one of the other single-board computers that I mentioned a couple of weeks ago.  And...it didn't go well, at least not for me.  My limited experiences probably won't exclude either from consideration again down the line, but I didn't have the time to figure things out, so don't let any of the following scare you away.

### Haiku

Since several people either endorsed it or asked my opinion on it, I started with [Haiku](https://www.haiku-os.org/).  Since I actually own a copy of BeOS---from my first attempt to get away from Windows and didn't care for any of the Linux distributions---I have had the Haiku project's blog in my RSS feed reader since its early days.  After moving to Linux, though, I stopped thinking of it as a serious option until the past couple of weeks.

Burning the ISO image to a USB drive, I plugged it and the new keyboard into the laptop's measly two ports, and pushed the machine to boot from the external drive.  The Haiku installer came right up, and...whined, I guess.  It won't install to any partition that doesn't have the BeOS file system on it.

The installer also locked up its user interface, for reasons that it couldn't bother to tell me, letting me move the mouse pointer around, but no response from any of the buttons or other elements on the screen.  I turn the machine off and back on, and rather than booting to the installer, this time it booted to the Haiku desktop.  Figuring that this would work fine for seeing if I could use Haiku as a daily machine, I decided to roll with this.

Haiku could mount the laptop's internal drive without any trouble, showing me that I stopped using it around 2018 and letting me open documents.  It urged me to only open it as a read-only drive, though, because we shouldn't trust its file system drivers.  That worried me, since any machine that I use regularly will need to  at least occasionally write to media outside the BeOS ecosystem.

Also, much as I hate to say it, and I know that fans will hate me for saying it, but I disliked the user interface.  No affordances to resize windows made themselves apparent.  Digging through the mounted drive opened every folder in a new window, cluttering the desktop with a dozen tiny windows.

Trying to push past that, though, I opened their application installer to see what I'd have to work with, since I could probably get accustomed to or learn to fix my quibbles so far.  It sat for a while, then gave me a cryptic paragraph of an error message about maybe showing out-of-date information, which I took as "you didn't set up Wi-Fi," since I didn't.  And...I couldn't figure out where to do that.  Their [wireless networking page](https://www.haiku-os.org/docs/userguide/en/workshop-wlan.html) claims that I only need to open the network widget, but it only gives me the *Open network preferences...* option that lets me start and stop services.

At that point, less than impressed with the usability and unable to install software or try a web browser, I threw my hands up and moved on, at least for now.

### Ironclad

Relatively new to me, the [Ironclad kernel](https://ironclad-os.org/) implements most of what Linux does, but the team has begun formally verifying the correctness of the code, which feels like almost the opposite of Linux, Fedora, and Debian all refusing to reject LLM-generated "contributions," because they either don't want to alienate people (who don't do any of the work) or think that they need more code faster.

Again, I burned the [Gloire](https://codeberg.org/Ironclad/Gloire) Ironclad distribution to a USB drive, booted the laptop to it, and immediately hit two details as it booted the operating system, which amounted to a problem.

- I would need to log in as either `root` or `user`, no password.
- It wouldn't recognize the external keyboard.

Do you remember when I mentioned that I stopped using this laptop because the left-hand side of the keyboard doesn't work?  Especially without a graphical interface, this makes it rather difficult to type `root` or `user`, since about half those keys don't do anything.

Again, I gave up there, at least for now.  The documentation suggests that a (hideous) desktop environment exists, but Gloire apparently doesn't boot into it.  I'd love to see something like [Elementary](https://elementary.io/)'s Pantheon desktop running there, though.

## Recipe

I tried out two experimental but quick ideas, this week, one still in progress.

{% cook 1|Oat Milk %}
Toast @rolled oats{1%cup} in a pan.  Blend well with @water{3%cup}.  Strain the solids out through cheese cloth.
{% endcook %}

It...didn't come out well, but seems to at least point in a decent direction.  First, searching around, it looks like the oats---after toasting, though most people don't toast---should probably soak for about half an hour to soften them before running them through the food processor.  Second, this created something viscous, closer to heavy cream than any milk, so the next round should probably use about half to two-thirds of the oats or use a filter with a tighter weave.

Honestly, at this thickness, I wonder if I should try making non-dairy yogurt instead of trying to water it down[^sDujxR] into a usable beverage, or if the (presumed) sediment would prevent it from fermenting properly by keeping the bacteria from accessing food.  I think that I have some starter in the refrigerator that someone left me, and the rice cooker does have a yogurt mode, so it seems worth a shot.

[^sDujxR]:  I find this result suspicious, because I tried making oat milk twice before without toasting the oats, running oats and water through a blender.  Trying to strain it through a coffee filter produced what I'd largely call water, barely any flavor or body.  Using a metal gadget with tiny holes in it, I got something slightly better, but with so much sediment that it only really worked as a porridge ingredient.  This seems more cohesive, but it wouldn't surprise me if it separates out into a weak milk and a ton of sediment.

Anyway, I said that I had two quickies.

{% cook 1|Sauerkraut %}
Massage @kosher salt{½%Tablespoon} into @shredded cabbage{⅓%head} for about ~{10%minutes}.  Move to a jar if not already there.  If not covered by released juices, add enough water to cover plus more salt.

Weigh down the cabbage and let sit at room temperature for at least ~{4%days}.
{% endcook %}

For the weight, I used a sheet of parchment paper pushed into the jar as a makeshift bag, filled with an old set of ceramic pie weights.  Admittedly, I haven't tried to eat it, yet, since I only started it later in the week.

Honestly, I *wanted* to start a jar of kimchi, but kept dragging my feet on buying the fish sauce, scallions, and so forth, so I compromised and put part of the head of cabbage to use in something that required significantly less preparation.

## Sewing

What do we have here...?

![A fabric mask, of the sort that became popular during COVID-19 lockdowns and N95 manufacturing hadn't yet ramped up.  This one comes in a hideous traffic-cone orange](/blog/assets/orange-mask.png "Orange you glad that I won't wear this?")

Longtime readers know that I have spent a couple of years or more trying to push myself to get back into sewing.

In junior high school, we had a year with one semester of home economics and one of wood shop.  I did reasonably well with both, but took to the former much more thoroughly, where I even volunteered to finish abandoned projects after school[^ADVusO], and kept up most of what I learned there for quite a while.  That preference worked in my favor, since I haven't even *seen* a band saw or a spot welder---yeah, they handed me a spot welder in wood shop; long story---since.

[^ADVusO]:  Reading it back, I acknowledge that something sounds fishy, here.  But I did spend a couple of weeks baking quick-breads and finishing tote bags that other students had left behind.  I still have at least one of those tote bags floating around, though it has become more of a static container.

At some point, I fell out of practice, first through not having ready access to a sewing machine, and eventually not even really bothering to hem pants or reattach buttons by hand or (when I ultimately bought myself one) by machine.  That brings us to this mask.  To answer some questions:

- Did I need another cloth face mask?  No, I have plenty.
- Would this mask protect anybody from anything?  I doubt it.
- Would I find this acceptable to wear outdoors?  Heck no.
- Where did I put the bands to attach the mask to my face?  I didn't bother, in no small part because of the prior three answers.  They *would* get sewn into the seams on either side, though, to form loops to go around the ears or head.
- Could I have found thread that matches?  Probably, but I wanted to inspect the work for the first couple of runs, though maybe finding that exact color would take effort...and a complete lack of taste.
- Did I mean to buy that color?  I had no issues with orange, but the picture on the website definitely under-sold how much orange it pumps out per square inch...

To ease myself back into the habit, I went with Free Sewing's [Florence face mask](https://freesewing.eu/designs/florence/), the only one of their [low-difficulty projects](https://freesewing.eu/designs/#filter=%7B%22difficulty%22%3A1%7D) that wouldn't take much fabric *and* I would have some reasonable possibility of actually using, if I made it again.  To put that another way, I will almost certainly never need a skirt or a Roman-style cloak, and didn't want to waste one or more yards of fabric on the exercise.

For reference, when I first encountered Free Sewing, they had a CC BY license for the patterns indicated on the website.  In one of the revisions, they dropped it, which worried me a bit *until* noticing that they ship the designs---from which the software generates the patterns based on the user's measurements---with the software, so it falls under the same [MIT License](https://codeberg.org/freesewing/freesewing/src/commit/3192b78b70ec093ec90a0c03ec6791a04e5b5dc0/LICENSE) as everything else...though copyright for sewing patterns gets a bit weird as utilitarian descriptions, so it arguably doesn't matter much.

The fabric itself falls somewhere on the upper end of the "atrocity" spectrum.  I happened to start thinking about exercising again and found an online fabric store with some clearance sales of a buck-fifty per yard, primarily this horrific orange...you know the stiff, ribbed fabric that gets used for polo shirt collars and cuffs?  Think of that, but a *lot* of it, and more shockingly orange than even the photograph implies.  Maybe it'll fade to something more acceptable in the wash.  And I did also order some more reasonable fabrics, for later, plus the existing stock that I've optimistically accumulated[^qX1zF9] over the years, which mostly felt too pricey to experiment with.  I probably won't have any qualms, though, about experimenting with this stuff.

[^qX1zF9]:  Because I have wanted to get back into sewing for a long time---especially at times when I have felt some job dissatisfaction, as in gainfully employed but needing another outlet---I have periodically picked up ten to fifteen yards of something on sale, thinking that it'd push me into going fully Free Culture on my wardrobe without bothering with practice.

Regardless, even though nobody should rely on these sorts of cloth masks[^1lcjRG] for safety, and I would call this maybe the worst possible fabric to use for a mask (you'll notice where I needed to let the seam drift off the mask near the bridge of the nose, for example, because the four (each folded) layers of fabric became too bulky for the machine's foot), I decided to plow ahead to get a feel for sewing again.  And I *think* that I'd call this successful at that much, at least.  If you exclude the time taken to constantly re-thread the needle---because it somehow kept getting lost, probably me getting the tensioning wrong---and ripping out the thread where the free side managed to slip underneath my seam, it probably took about an hour from printing the pattern to declaring victory.  Despite the stiff, heavy fabric used for the shell and the lining, it also comes surprisingly close to fitting my lower face.

[^1lcjRG]:  I did see reports of one researcher getting good results by stitching a HEPA-rated vacuum bag as the middle layer of a DIY mask, but who still has vacuum cleaners that take bags...?

As I move to larger garments, the orange may (counter-intuitively) become less obnoxious, because I can absolutely wear hideous pants or whatever around the house, and the material might even work out well for the winter.  Until then, the bolt sits upright on my couch like the world's ugliest houseguest.

I had a few frustrating moments while scheduling the week's toots, last week, getting duplicate-key responses with every response, making it difficult for me to work out whether anything worked.  That required a tiny adjustment to Ruby's JSON parser.

```ruby
JSON.parse response.body, allow_duplicate_key: true
```

Mastodon only repeats some ID that I don't use or care about, so it doesn't matter how it handles the duplicates to me, but it looks like the last value to come through overwrites the others, as you might expect.
